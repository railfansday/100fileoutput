#5220. Frank Lloyd Wright House Taliesin West Reopens After Fatal Accident

Renowned architect Frank Lloyd Wright’s former home in Scottsdale, AZ has officially reopened following a freak accident that killed a visiting dignitary.

Taliesin West reopened to the public on Saturday, Oct. 24, eight days after Rogério Renato Mateucci, 55, was killed while on a walking tour of the property on October 16.

Mateucci, a top official at Pontifical Catholic University of Paraná in Brazil, was stuck by a piece of roofing material dislodged by strong winds, police have now confirmed.

The National Weather Service reported east winds of 22 mph in Scottsdale on the day of the fatal accident, with gusts up to 32 mph.

Scottsdale police Sgt. Allison Sempsis told The Bee that a piece of acrylic glass struck Mateucci and that the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation cooperated with the investigation.

“The Foundation mourns the loss of Rogério Renato Mateucci, who tragically died on October 16 in an accident at Taliesin West,” said Joseph Specter, President and CEO of the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, in a statement provided to Realtor.com. “We continue to hold Mr. Mateucci and his loved ones in our hearts and continue to offer our deepest condolences.”

While the property was closed to the public for the police investigation, which determined the incident was weather-related, the Foundation conducted a thorough safety inspection of the Taliesin West, including its roofing and structures, according to the statement.

“Weather Station technology has been added to enhance our existing safety procedures at the site, to assess temperature, wind speed, air pressure, and rain,” said Specter. “This will offer the Foundation localized safety data to further inform the daily practice of opening our site to the public.”

With the inspection complete and safety measures in place, Specter said the Foundation was “confident that Taliesin West is safe for visitors” and prepared to reopen the property on Saturday.

Taliesin West was established in 1937 as a winter getaway for Frank Lloyd Wright and his apprentices. The campus, which was designed to reflect the ruggedness of the desert landscape, was originally constructed with canvas panels for the roof, instead of traditional materials.

After Wright’s death in 1959, the home was updated to allow for year-round occupation by the foundation and Taliesin Institute. Updates included replacing the canvas roof with insulated acrylic panels, inclosing interior spaces, and adding air conditioning.

In recent years, the Taliesin Institute has been looking into ways to return to a fabric roofing system using more durable materials that weren’t around during Wright’s lifetime, according to a May interview with Rebecca Barron, Director of Preservation at the Taliesin Institute.

The exploration of modern materials is part of the Taliesin Institute’s value-based preservation, through which it considers the physical structure and aesthetics of a site in addition to preserving the “intangible heritage” of the site.

“In this way, the fabric roof project continues to ongoing process of innovation and experimentation that was always at the heart of this place,” said Barron.

Authorities have not tied the October 16 accident to the Institute’s preservation work, and the exact origin of the panel that struck Mateucci remains unclear.

#5219. Intel buys quantum software pioneer Classiq for $2.2B

Intel Corp. said today it has agreed to the terms of a blockbuster acquisition for the quantum computing software startup Classiq Technologies Ltd.

It’ll pay $2.2 billion to buy the Israeli company, making it the second-largest acquisition in its history after its $15.3 billion deal to buy Mobileye back in 2017.

Classiq has likened itself to the Microsoft of the quantum computing world, having developed an operating system that enables the execution of complex algorithms on a range of different quantum computers. Its software is agnostic to the wide range of quantum machines currently being developed by companies such as IBM Corp., Google LLC and IonQ Inc. That includes systems that use trapped ions, superconductors, photons, carbon nanotubes, topological networks and the spin qubit-based system developed by Intel.

The startup’s software is designed to run on top of specialized processors that were developed specifically for quantum algorithms by a company called Quantum Machines Ltd. The operating system serves as a platform for developers to design, build, test and run software designed to run on any kind of quantum computer.

For instance, BMW used Classiq’s platform to develop quantum software that’s used to simulate car aerodynamics, so they can be modeled in different scenarios, such as where various components are built using different materials. The idea is that by stress-testing different ideas in an ultra-realistic simulation, it can avoid the need for more expensive real-world testing.

Another customer is HSBC Holdings Plc, which used Classiq’s platform to develop a quantum algorithm for fraud detection. Meanwhile, the Italian bank Intesa Sanpaolo used Classiq to build an application that allows it to price options over shorter timeframes to increase its profitability per transaction.

One of the most notable things about today’s acquisition is that Intel Chief Executive Lip-Bu Tan was one of the earliest investors in Classiq, participating in its $33 million Series B round of funding in February 2022. He returned to participate in the company’s $110 million Series C in May 2025. All told, Classiq has raised just over $200 million in funding.

Intel said Classiq will become its global development center for quantum computing. It intends to combine Classiq’s platform with its existing quantum spin-qubit manufacturing process to “create a user-friendly full-stack quantum computing platform” that will enable quantum technology to mature as a mainstream computing business.

In a statement, Intel likened Classiq’s technology to electronic design automation and computer-aided design software, saying that it could have the same impact on quantum computing that those technologies had on chip design and advanced engineering. “Providing this high-level abstraction enables quantum computing to scale beyond custom hand-crafted circuits and applications to become a mainstream computing platform,” the chipmaker said.

The deal is not just a bet on the future of quantum computing, but also a major move by Intel into the world of operating systems. In some ways, it’s reminiscent of Google’s acquisition of Android 2005, a move that enabled the search engine giant to gain an entry into the nascent smartphone industry. Android has since become the world’s most popular smartphone operating system by far, giving Google substantial influence over various aspects of the industry, including hardware development.

Classiq isn’t the only company developing operating systems for quantum computers, though. Intel’s biggest rival now will likely be IBM Corp., which is not only seen as a leader in terms of quantum hardware, but also software.

IBM has developed the Qiskit platform, which also enables hardware-agnostic quantum application development. Qiskit hasn’t been around as long as Classiq’s platform, but IBM’s brand recognition and status as a quantum pioneer gives it strong credibility as a solid alternative.

Intel said the acquisition is subject to customary closing conditions and regulatory approvals, but if all goes well, it expects the deal to close during the first half of next year.

#5217. 5 Exciting Ways to Invest in Real Estate

When it comes to exploring enticing investment opportunities, real estate often stands out as an excellent solution for making and growing wealth. No matter how the world around us changes, there will always be a demand for property in attractive areas. This has led countless people to diversify their portfolios over the years, buying rental properties, second homes, and even office spaces.

In recent years, as the world has continued to evolve, the marketplace for real estate investors has evolved with it. Today, people no longer need to buy and flip entire properties to make money. There are numerous tips for getting started and ways you can start your journey into the property world, and some options are actually simpler and more affordable than you might expect. Here are some of the best, most interesting ways to invest in real estate, if you want to avoid the traditional buy and flip strategy.

Not all real estate investments have to include purchasing actual bricks and mortar in the physical world. The digital landscape has evolved drastically in recent years, introducing new opportunities for innovative entrepreneurs to make money buying virtual assets. As the concept of the metaverse continues to gain traction, digital real estate is growing increasingly attractive.

With digital real estate, companies purchase a section of the blockchain in a metaverse landscape, where they can build online experiences, or simply store other assets. In 2021, sales on four of the biggest metaverse platforms for real estate reached a whopping $501 million. This indicates learning how to invest in digital real estate could be a worthwhile strategy for any consumer. Not only is it potentially profitable, but the digital real estate also requires less work to manage and maintain than a physical house or property.

Even if you’re not familiar with this form of property-based investing, you probably know the term crowdfunding. Made popular by sites like GoFundMe and Kickstarter, the crowdfunding strategy involves individuals pooling their resources together to fund a product or business. In the real estate world, investors can work together to purchase a property, such as a corporate space, warehouse, or block of flats, reducing the initial cost of a purchase.

The approach allows investors to participate in products that would have been impossible to access in the past because of high capital requirements. Individuals can use this strategy to diversify their portfolios with a variety of different locations and property types. Plus, you’ll earn a passive income through rental profits, and property appreciation.

Another real estate investment strategy gaining increasing attention in recent years is REITs. These are basically investment vehicles responsible for owning and operating income-generating assets, such as rental properties and office blocks. They’re traded on traditional stock exchanges and provide individuals with an opportunity to invest in the market, without directly owning a property or having to manage the investment themselves.

REITs can come with several attractive features, including regular distributions of dividends, and the ability to explore a range of different sectors. You can even use this strategy to spread your portfolio across industrial, residential, and commercial properties. They’re also a highly liquid investment option, making them appealing to beginners.

Finally, one interesting way to invest in real estate, without buying a physical property outright, is to spend some of your money on shares in PropTech startups. PropTech, or Property Technology startups, are companies focused on developing tools and real estate designed to transform the property landscape. They develop things like property management solutions, smart home technology, and virtual reality applications for tours.

If you already have experience in the world of stocks and shares, investing in a PropTech company could be an excellent way to utilize your existing knowledge and diversify your portfolio. It’s also a chance to get your foot in the door with a company that’s actively disrupting the real estate space, and the market as we know it.

Investing in property has become a much more diverse and versatile opportunity for individuals in recent years. No matter how much money you can afford to spend, or where your expertise lies, you can find plenty of ways to expand your portfolio with disruptive real-estate investment strategies.

#5211. The Efficacy of Broad Match + Audiences Strategy in Real Estate

When considering digital marketing strategies, the realm of real estate is no different from any other industry. The key objective remains the same - reaching the target audience with an impactful, compelling message that can inspire action. An increasingly popular approach adopted by real estate agents is the broad match + audiences strategy. This approach leverages the power of Google AdWords' broad match feature and combines it with audience targeting to attain superior marketing results.

The real estate sector thrives on the availability of a vast array of properties, each with a unique combination of attributes that appeal to different prospective buyers. Recognizing this immense diversity and matching it with the correct buyer is a daunting challenge that real estate agents constantly grapple with. This is where the broad match + audiences strategy steps in, optimizing the process by expanding reach while ensuring relevance.

The broad match feature in Google AdWords is a type of keyword match that allows your ads to show whenever someone searches for that keyword or a related phrase. It can include searches that are misspellings, synonyms, related searches, and other relevant variations.

With the broad match feature, you're casting a wide net over an extensive range of search queries. It brings with it the advantage of gaining visibility across a wider spectrum of potential buyers who might not use the exact keywords that you've selected for your campaign.

In the real estate industry, property attributes can be incredibly diverse, ranging from "3-bedroom apartments in New York City" to "colonial-style houses with large gardens in Austin". By leveraging the broad match feature, agents can reach potential clients who use various search terms that directly or indirectly relate to their listing.

Audience targeting is a method used in digital marketing to segment audiences based on specific criteria like demographics, interests, buying behavior, etc. Google AdWords allows you to target your ads to specific audiences, ensuring they are seen by those who are most likely to be interested in your offerings.

In the context of real estate, audience targeting can be a game-changer. For instance, if an agent specializes in selling luxury homes, they can target audiences with high-income levels and a demonstrated interest in luxury living. Alternatively, if an agent focuses on properties suitable for first-time buyers, they can target younger audiences who have recently started searching for homes.

The marriage of broad match keywords with audience targeting may seem counter-intuitive at first, considering one aims at breadth and the other at specificity. However, the combination forms a robust strategy that takes the best of both worlds - wide reach and precise targeting.

The broad match feature ensures a wide reach, allowing real estate agents to surface their listings to a large number of prospective clients. But, broad match alone can lead to high impression counts with low conversion rates as the ads may be presented to irrelevant audiences. This is where audience targeting steps in to bring precision.

When used in conjunction, the broad match feature ensures that your ads reach a wide array of searchers, while audience targeting ensures these ads are shown to a specific subset of this wide audience who are most likely to be interested in your property listings.

#5195. Is the Housing Affordability Crisis Becoming a Generational Wealth Fight?

Jasmyn Carter thought she had done what prospective homebuyers are supposed to do.

She had a good debt-to-income ratio, she paid her student loans, and by 2022, she had saved $30,000 in cash toward a home.

Carter, whose family has lived in Texas for eight generations, told Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico at an Oct. 14 CNN town hall that when she went house hunting, a family from California put an additional $80,000 down in cash.

“We cannot afford a house,” she told him.

Talarico said he was hearing the same frustration from young Texans who had been promised that working hard, going to school, and playing by the rules would deliver the American dream.

With the Nov. 3 midterms just days away, new polling suggests that frustration is rippling across how young Americans see their economic future.

The Harvard Youth Poll reported on Oct. 22 that, for the first time in its history, Americans aged 18 to 29 are more likely to expect to be financially worse off than their parents—27% to 25%. Two years ago, optimism led pessimism by 8 percentage points.

That negative sentiment is shared across generations. Nearly 9 in 10 registered voters want Democrats and Republicans in Congress to work together to make housing more affordable, according to an April Bipartisan Policy Center/Advocus survey.

But the harder question is what “affordable” should mean—and for whom.

Housing makes that question especially difficult to answer because it's both a necessary expense and an asset—compare that to a lower grocery bill, which is just cheaper.

A lower home price, meanwhile, also means someone else's asset—and often, their greatest source of wealth—is worth less.

And the generations enter that conflict from very different positions.

Just 24% of adults aged 18 to 29 owned their homes in 2025, according to the Federal Reserve, compared with 83% of adults 60 and older. Among homeowners, median home equity was $90,000 for those under 35 in 2022, compared with $250,000 for those 65 and older.

Some of that gap reflects age: Older households have had more time to let their equity grow. But today's younger households also face a worse entry point.

From 2019 to 2024, inflation-adjusted home values rose 30% while incomes for households headed by someone under 40 rose 9%. Over that period, the share of renter households under 40 earning enough to afford the monthly cost of a typical home fell from 56% to 37%.

And once accumulated, housing wealth can become an advantage in the next purchase. Repeat buyers put a median of 23% down in 2025, compared with 10% for first-time buyers, and 30% of repeat buyers paid entirely in cash.

It can cross generations, too: 26% of younger millennial buyers received down payment help through a gift or loan from a relative or friend, according to the National Association of Realtors®.

So the divide is not literally old versus young. It's between households already connected to housing wealth and those trying to enter the system without it—a divide that maps heavily onto age and can be reinforced by what families have available to pass down.

That leaves policymakers trying to solve two problems at once: Help younger households acquire the asset without eroding the wealth older households have accumulated in it.

“From a home price perspective, these goals are incompatible,” explains Joel Berner, senior economist at Realtor.com®. “Prices can't both go up for existing homeowners and down for prospective ones.”

An August working paper from economists Joshua Coven, Sebastian Golder, Arpit Gupta, and Abdoulaye Ndiaye shows just how complex delivering relief is.

The researchers found that lower property taxes make homes cheaper for existing owners to hold, but some of the benefit can become capitalized into higher prices while giving older owners more reason to stay put.

In their model, raising California property taxes to Texas levels lowered prices and required down payments while increasing holding costs for older owners. Young homeownership rose; elderly homeownership fell.

That same economic conflict is reinforced at the ballot box.

More than 6 in 10 people aged 50 and older voted in the 2022 midterms, according to AARP, compared with roughly 3 in 10 people younger than 30. An October survey across 43 competitive congressional districts found 83% of voters 50 and older were extremely motivated to vote, compared with 66% of those under 50.

Affordability is motivating older voters, too. AARP found rising prices and affordability were their top concern in those districts.

But affordability looks different once someone owns the asset.

“The ancillary costs of homeownership are more impactful on existing [homeowners] than home asking prices or current mortgage rates,” Berner says.

Property taxes, insurance premiums, and HOA fees can make a home more expensive to keep even as the owner accumulates equity. Slowing those costs can help incumbent owners, Berner says, although there remains a risk that some of the savings eventually become reflected in higher home values.

Voters of all ages will be weighing that confluence of immediate and knock-on effects across the country in November.

In Florida, voters will consider Amendment 3, which would, among other changes, expand the state's homestead exemption for nonschool property taxes.

An AIF Center for Political Strategy poll conducted by McLaughlin & Associates found 36% support among voters younger than 30, compared with 55% among voters older than 40. A separate Mason-Dixon survey found a smaller gap in the same direction: 48% support among voters aged 18 to 34 versus 56% among those 65 and older.

Of course, those polls don't explain why voters split by age. But they're telling in a state where the economics of ownership already depend on when someone entered the market.

In Miami, the owner of a newly purchased median-valued home would pay about $10,024 a year in property taxes, according to the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy. Someone who bought an equally valuable home in 2012 would pay just $3,166, largely because assessment limits restrict how quickly taxable values rise for existing owners.

Even so, NAR estimates that Amendment 3's tax savings could move roughly 40,000 additional renter households under 45 above its income-qualification threshold for a starter home.

It's a clear illustration of why the generational conflict is so difficult to untangle.

California's Proposition 37, meanwhile, starts from the other end. It would authorize up to $25 billion in revenue bonds for loans covering as much as 17% of the purchase price of qualifying newly constructed homes. Participating homeowners would repay the loans.

“Every day, we hear from middle-class Californians who simply want a fair shot at homeownership for themselves and their families—but affordability continues to stand in the way,” California Association of Realtors President Tamara Suminski told Realtor.com in October. “By providing down payment assistance loans—at no cost to taxpayers—this measure tackles one of the biggest barriers and brings the dream of homeownership closer to reality.”

President Donald Trump has stated that goal explicitly.

At a January Cabinet meeting, Trump said he wanted to make homes easier to buy without reducing the wealth existing homeowners had accumulated.

“I don't want those values to come down,” he said. He later argued that lower interest rates could “keep the values up for the people that have housing and let other people buy housing.”

Berner agrees lower mortgage rates would quickly improve first-time-buyer affordability. But stronger demand would likely push prices higher, he says, eroding some of the gain.

“The longer answer is housing supply growth and income growth,” he says.

Income growth offers the least direct conflict among generations. If earnings rise faster than inflation and home prices, prospective buyers gain purchasing power without requiring existing owners to lose equity.

Supply attacks the other side of the equation by reducing scarcity—but its success can make the trade-off more visible.

“As far as proven results, Austin, TX, is a great example of supply responding quickly to demand, especially in the new-construction starter segment,” Berner says. “However, home price softness in the metro is illustrative of the trade-off for existing homeowners.”

He also points to Montana, which is trying to make that supply response easier, with reforms allowing more accessory dwelling units, multifamily development, and other forms of housing that Berner says could create more starter inventory.

The two routes suggest the generational goals are not completely irreconcilable. But Carter’s experience points to one more complication: Affordability is relative.

Remember that California family who put down an additional $80,000 in cash? They weren’t alone.

California Policy Lab research found San Francisco Bay Area movers landed in neighborhoods where home values were about 50% lower than those they left, and became homeowners at higher rates.

So a buyer priced out of one market may become the stronger bidder in another—shifting affordability pressure rather than eliminating it.

“The issue in audio is it’s not object oriented; it’s channel oriented,” co-founder and Chief Executive Valtteri Salomaki told SiliconANGLE in an interview.

For broadcasters, making that connection means following a player’s sounds through a crowded arena. Edge is betting the same approach could help robots and other physical AI systems interpret the world around them — including activity their cameras cannot yet see.

Humans do this automatically because we exist in a multimodal world: Sight, sound and physical sensation all come in at once. In the physical AI world, sound has always been treated as a second-class citizen to vision because cameras are already everywhere, in everyday life and used for everything. For humans, sound is fundamental to navigating everywhere.

Audio comes in channels, Salomaki explained. It’s connected to microphones, which are positioned around a space or on objects and they listen in locations. These produce signals that identify sounds – they don’t directly associate with anything until something else turns them into readily identifiable information.

“It’s split into two layers,” Salomaki said. “One is how do you create a digital twin of all sensors. The other one is how do you create AI machine learning models to source separate sound elements.”

In the sports arena demonstration, the system uses microphone locations, pickup patterns and player tracking to select useful audio. It then associates locations; visual and audio are paired to identify objects — such as players, the ball, even the coach or a referee with the whistle.

The company said users can import 3D spaces or 2D layouts with coordinates and place sensors within that representation. This allows a spatial map of the environment and its sensors; this is essentially the “digital twin” that Salomaki is referring to.

Other use cases could involve autonomous vehicles, which could be given digital ears to hear if a siren is approaching. Humans identify sounds and direction because our ears are on the sides of our heads. If a sound comes from the left, it’s louder on that side, even if we can’t see an object there.

Anyone who has heard a siren approaching or receding has also experienced the Doppler effect, where the pitch increases or decreases depending on how fast it’s coming or going. A higher pitch can give an autonomous vehicle an immediate warning that an emergency vehicle is approaching from a blind corner (and its direction of approach), even if the lights aren’t visible on camera.

Edge has a general model that it develops and fine-tunes for customer needs. These models face different delay target requirements for real-time activity. Salomaki cited a sub-33-millisecond tolerance for sports broadcasting and said other environments might tolerate 100 or 200 milliseconds.

Useful perception needs to arrive on time for most applications because hearing what happens needs to be paired with seeing.

The company is building toward hardware independence with its digital twin software and AI models, allowing Edge to assemble new applications around a core technology. This way, it will become audio infrastructure that will work alongside existing microphones and cameras customers already have for their current physical AI robots, media and other systems.

With the funding, Salomaki said, Edge will complete integration with existing enterprise customers and make the platform’s components transferable across industries ahead of a Series A.

Live sports has already given Edge a commercial market as it developed and validated its perception technology, using that to help train and build its models. Its Virtual Sound Engine, which automatically captures and isolates sound from complex environments, is already deployed commercially across the industry. Customers and partners include American audio equipment manufacturer Shure Inc., the National Basketball Association, Fox Sports Media Group and the United States Tennis Association Inc.

“In order to achieve the result everybody wants in physical AI, computers need to become a little bit more human,” Salomaki said.

The new agents are aimed at a problem Regal says shows up in calls such as auto insurance claims, in which someone reporting an accident tends to explain what happened, where and who was involved in one go. Agents built on conditional logic often circle back for details the caller already gave, just so each field gets filled. Building the whole agent on a single prompt keeps the conversation open, and in Regal’s view it leaves business processes only loosely followed.

Goal Oriented Agents put two models on each call. Regal calls them an actor and a director, and both start work as soon as the caller stops talking. The actor, a smaller and faster model tuned for dialogue, replies right away. Running behind it, the director checks whether the goals for the current stage have been met and can move the call to a new stage with fresh instructions for the actor.

Businesses build the agents out of goal nodes, each listing the goals to be met by the end of the node and any prerequisites that come first. Booking a callback can count as one of those goals. A prerequisite is something the agent has to establish before it can act, such as the caller’s date of birth. Regal said those can be written as plain-language conditions too, for example “the caller mentioned a death in the family.”

The director reviews progress after every turn, so one answer can tick off several items at once. Regal’s sample auto insurance claim has a caller named Dan describing the vehicle damage, photo evidence and injuries in his first message. All three get marked as collected, and the agent goes straight to his name. Once it has that, the director sees location and policy number are still missing, so the actor’s next question is where he is.

Regal said complex intake and qualification calls are among the strongest uses it has seen for the agents. The company named Medicare enrollment, roadside assistance and mortgage qualification as uses the design opens up. Medical intake that complies with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act is another.

Google LLC, AAA and Toyota Motor Corp. are among the more than 200 enterprises Regal says it serves. Customers of Five9 Inc.’s contact center platform gained access to Regal’s voice agents through an integration in August.

Regal is a venture capital-backed startup that has raised $83 million to date. That includes a $40 million round in October 2024.

Insurance premiums (landlord insurance typically costs about 25% more than a standard homeowners policy)

Gaps between tenants, when you pay the mortgage without rental income

#5163. Why More Buyers Are Turning To Adjustable-Rate Mortgages Today

When mortgage rates climb, buying a home can start to feel like trying to hit a moving target. Every jump in rates makes monthly payments bigger, and that pushes a lot of buyers to look for another way in.

More and more of them are finding it in the adjustable-rate mortgage, or ARM.

Here’s how an ARM differs from a more traditional mortgage. With a regular fixed-rate mortgage, your rate stays the same for your whole loan. But with an ARM, your rate is locked for the first few years, then it can move up or down based on market conditions.

Some people don’t like that level of uncertainty – and that’s okay. But others are drawn to this option specifically because there’s usually a lower rate to start when you opt for an ARM. And in an affordability-strapped market, that’s going to gain popularity.

Bankrate recently found that, in September, rates for ARM products were over a full percentage point lower than a regular fixed-rate loan. Depending on the ARM product, that can make a difference of $150-300 in the monthly payment on a median-priced home.

And for some buyers, that’s enough to turn “not right now” into “let’s do this.” You can see it in the numbers. Data from the Mortgage Bankers Association (MBA) shows that, in October, roughly 1 in 10 people applying for a mortgage chose an ARM (see graph below):

If you think you may want to consider this yourself, here’s what you need to know about how it works.

An ARM is like a phone or cable plan that has an introductory deal for new customers. You start out at a lower price, and then it adjusts after a specified amount of time. A popular loan is the 5/1 ARM.

The “5” means your introductory rate is locked for the first five years, and the “1” means that the rate can change every year after that. The rate change is called the “reset.” And here’s the really important part.

When your rate resets, your mortgage payment could go up or down. The good news is that most ARMs have caps, which are limits on how much your rate can rise each year and over the life of the loan. But either way, you have to be ready.

To see if this type of loan may be right for you, here are some things to think about and discuss with your loan officer:

Before you decide, ask your lender to work up the numbers and walk you through the different potential scenarios you could run into with an ARM. That way, you go in with your eyes open.

And, if you do go with an adjustable-rate mortgage, just know that you’ll have choices when the reset time arrives. You could:

Do nothing and keep your loan in place if the new payment still fits your budget.

Refinance into a fixed-rate loan with a payment that never changes.

Refinance into another ARM with a new lower rate locked for a specified timeframe.

Talk to the loan servicer about assistance or modification.

Remember, a refinance is a brand-new loan that you’ll have to qualify for – even if it’s on a home you already own. So, keep tabs on your credit and debt levels and ask your loan officer how major financial moves like job changes and major purchases will affect your mortgage readiness. It’s important to keep your credit and finances in “mortgage shape.”

One more quick note. ARMs got a bad name after the financial crisis of 2008. Back then, there were some risky loans with small starting payments that later shot way up. Others let people borrow without proving how much money they earned.

But now, there are new laws to protect both borrowers and lenders. The biggest change is the Ability-to-Repay Rule, which says that before a lender makes a home loan, it has to check real paperwork (like pay stubs, W-2 forms, and tax returns) to make sure you can afford it. The lender also has to look at what your payment could be after the reset, not just the low starting payment.

So, you don’t have to worry that today’s ARMs are the same as the ones we saw back during the crash.

“What happens if these agents aren’t available?” Scimone said. “What happens if the data that the agents are deciding on is not available? A core part of your resilience posture has to contend with the notion that if our agents go down, our work is not happening.”

Companies are still cautious about letting agents act alone. A survey of 25 enterprise technology and security leaders  led by theCUBE Research’s Krista Case found that 19 had autonomous agents in production. None allowed those agents to perform high-risk actions without human approval.

“It doesn’t mean doing it willy-nilly and just throwing caution to the wind,” he said. “It means being very thoughtful around where can’t you fail, putting deterministic guardrails in place … and letting the business run. Right now, I’m seeing too much caution in the space on that front.”

Dell applies that discipline internally, giving every agent tight identity and access control, extensive logging and traceability of decisions and actions, Scimone explained. That groundwork matters because only four of the survey’s 25 respondents had tested an AI agent incident scenario. Dell has also expanded its cyber resilience tools and joined OpenAI Group PBC’s call for collective action on cyber defense.

Attackers have used AI for years but haven’t yet deployed it at full scale, according to Scimone. He expects that to change soon.

“We haven’t hit that curve that I do believe is coming, and in the coming quarters, not in the coming years,” he said.

Vellante pointed to one encouraging finding from the survey: Organizations granting agents bounded autonomy showed stronger recovery readiness than those relying solely on human approval. In Scimone’s view, AI agent guardrails designed up front let companies take more risk, not less.

“Organizations that design those governance systems upfront are going to be the ones, ironically, that can move the quickest and can take the most risk because they can do it confidently with the right insights and the right data to know that they’re not overstepping,” Scimone said. “At the same time, they’re not under-leaning.”

#5138. Holiday Season Is the Best Time For Homebuyers to Score a Deal in These 7 U.S. Metros

Early November is the best time for homebuyers to score a bargain in three U.S. metro areas: Milwaukee, Riverside, CA and Tampa, FL.

In Detroit and three Florida metros–Fort Lauderdale, Miami and West Palm Beach–house hunters may benefit from holding out a little longer. Mid-December is typically the best time of year to get a deal in those places.

This is according to a Home Economics and Redfin analysis of housing market data; the analysis was originally completed in April. The analysis identifies the time of year when discounts off a home’s original asking price–through price drops and/or negotiations between buyer and seller–typically approach their seasonal peak.

Nationwide, discounts tend to peak in early fall before plateauing or shrinking as winter approaches. But housing is local, and in these seven metros, peak discounts typically arrive later. Homebuying slows as the holidays approach, leaving sellers with fewer potential buyers–giving the buyers who remain more room to negotiate. And some late-year listings have been sitting for months, making sellers more motivated to strike a deal before the end of the year.

“There’s a tradeoff to shopping for a home late in the year: Buyers may have fewer fresh listings to choose from, but they also face less competition, and many sellers are ready to make a deal,” said Redfin Senior Economist Asad Khan. “A seller who has been waiting months for an offer may be more willing to negotiate on price or other costs than they were in the spring. Buyers shouldn’t purchase a home just because the calendar says it’s a good time for deals, but those who find the right home may have room to negotiate.”

In Florida, the best deals for buyers typically arrive in November or December, partly due to weather. South Florida’s biggest wave of buyers–retirees and seasonal residents–often show up after the new year, when the weather is sunny and comfortable in Florida but cold in the Northeast.

“Local buyers have a good shot at getting a deal in November, mainly because it’s just before the snowbirds arrive,” said Todd Edwards, a Redfin Premier agent in Tampa. “House hunters have a window of opportunity to negotiate on listings that have been lingering on the market since summer: Sellers want to close a deal before the new year, and there’s often not much competition from out-of-towners yet.”

Hurricane season, which runs through early fall, also keeps some house hunters away and leaves lingering listings ripe for discounts once it passes. Additionally, Florida’s high concentration of luxury homes tend to sell below asking price, especially late in the year when sellers are eager to get those properties off their books.

The story is a bit different in Milwaukee and Detroit. Those northern metros have a fairly short selling season: Cold-weather markets compress the window for touring and closing on homes; sellers keep at it later into fall, trying to close a deal before the depth of winter hits and buyers hibernate.

That delays the usual seasonal slowdown until November in Milwaukee and December in Detroit, later than in most of the country.

The November and December discount windows in Milwaukee, Riverside, Detroit and much of Florida aren’t a one-time event; they show up as a recurring pattern each year, regardless of whether the broader market favors buyers or sellers.

But the seasonal advantage could be particularly useful to buyers this year. The U.S. housing market is already firmly tilted toward buyers, with 63% more sellers than buyers—the biggest imbalance in Redfin records dating back to 2013. A jump in listings coupled with sluggish homebuying demand has given house hunters in many parts of the country more options and more negotiating power. Miami is one of the strongest buyer’s markets in the nation, with more than twice as many sellers as buyers. Tampa, West Palm Beach, Fort Lauderdale, Riverside and Detroit are also buyer’s markets.

Milwaukee is one of just five seller’s markets in the country. With more buyers than sellers in Milwaukee, deals aren’t commonplace–so buyers there may want to pay extra attention to seasonal patterns.

The best time to buy a home is more complex than the week when buyers are most likely to score a discount.

There’s typically a tradeoff between selection and savings. New listings generally peak months before the best deals arrive. For instance, Milwaukee typically has the most new listings in mid-May and the most fresh inventory in mid-July,then its best deals arrive in early November. Detroit’s new listings typically peak in early August and fresh inventory in early September, months before its mid-December dealmaking sweet spot.

That means buyers who wait until November or December may have fewer homes to choose from. But those who prioritize price—and are flexible about their timing—may find sellers more receptive to negotiations. Ultimately, buyers should balance price, selection and their own circumstances.
