The 2024 playoffs were supposed to be the start of something special in Orlando. The Magic had drafted Jalen Suggs, Franz Wagner and Paolo Banchero in three straight lotteries and had just pushed Donovan Mitchell and the Cavaliers to seven games. They were young, talented, and when they went all in on the Desmond Bane trade last summer, just about everyone officially stamped them as an Eastern Conference contender.

Unfortunately, we've never fully gotten to see what this team could be, and it's starting to look like we never will. Injuries continue to hit them as hard as any team in the NBA. Two games into the 2026-27 season, it's Banchero reportedly headed for a four-to-six week hiatus after suffering a high ankle sprain in Orlando's loss to the Timberwolves on Friday.

The Magic just cannot catch a break. Over the last two seasons, the Banchero-Wagner-Suggs trio has managed to play in just 25 games together. Last year, Wagner and Suggs missed a combined 73 games to injury. The year before that, Banchero and Wagner managed just 40 games together.

They literally suffered the same injury that year. Banchero tore his oblique in the first week of the season, missed 34 games, and just as he was gearing up to come back, Wagner tore his oblique and missed the next six weeks.

It's all the more frustrating given the glimpses of actual magic we've seen from this team at full strength. They had the No. 1-seed Pistons down 3-1 in the first round last year until Wagner, of course, missed the last three games of the series with a calf strain and Detroit rallied to win the series.

This season, you figured, or hoped, that the needle of injury luck would finally swing in their favor. But here we go again with Banchero, who was off to a roaring start this year with 35 points in the opener and another 14 through the first half on Friday. He was shooting almost 70% from the field. He made three of his four 3-pointers against Atlanta.

Aside from the injuries, there have been real questions as to whether Banchero and Wagner can ultimately fit as a duo given both of their shooting inconsistencies. We need to see them together for longer stretches to see if they can make it work, or if Banchero's shooting can improve. The basketball gods just won't let the experiment get off the ground for any real length of time.

It's too bad, because when Banchero is cooking, he's as lethal a scorer as there is in the league. The Magic can create a lot of inverted mismatches with Banchero's and Wagner's ability to handle the ball and initiate offense at 6-foot-10. Orlando's defense is elite; it had Detroit in a straitjacket for much of that series last year. The pieces are there.

But this Banchero injury might seal the fate of this season before it even starts. That sounds premature, but the Magic are already 0-2, and without Banchero for potentially a month and a half, they're very likely going to have to dig out of another deep hole to even think about competing for a playoff spot in the extremely deep East. Truth be told, they were going to have an uphill battle even had they stayed healthy.

Now all they can do is hold on as tightly as possible and pray to the basketball gods that nobody else gets hurt before Banchero comes back. The same basketball gods that have seemingly put a curse on a team that might be nearing its end without ever really having had the chance to get started.

Again, that sounds dramatic. But this feels like a make-or-break year for the Banchero-Wagner duo. The Magic lack the future equity to make another big move. Moving off one of these two is the only real shakeup available. The Magic tried switching coaches, but that's not going to make Banchero or Wagner shoot better and it's not going to make them stay healthy. This was the last shot to really see if this duo can work, and it could not have gotten off to a worse start.

#3015. How Sal Frelick became Brewers' unlikely World Series hero with Game 2 blast: 'He has this edge about him'

MILWAUKEE -- Ian Seymour kept testing Sal Frelick late Saturday night, almost dismissive of what the Brewers outfielder might be capable of producing.

But Frelick has never been one to shrink when his mettle is challenged. His competitive fire only burns hotter.

In the bottom of the sixth inning in Game 2 of the World Series, with the Brewers on the brink of erasing a four-run deficit for the second time this postseason, the lefthanded Rays reliever kept feeding Frelick fastballs.

All nine pitches of the at-bat, in fact. Alternating between two variations of the pitch for much of it.

Sinker. Four-seam. Sinker. Four-seam. Sinker. Four-seam. Sinker. Four-seam.

"I'm an eight-hole hitter," Frelick said, detailing the number of fastballs he saw. "I'm a lefty. You have to assume a guy like that is coming at you."

Frelick kept fighting, just as he had his whole career. He fouled off half of the first eight pitches he saw and ultimately worked the count full.

Seymour became predictable, a creature of habit, not doubling up on the same pitch twice to that point.

That was until the ninth pitch of the at-bat.

Seymour tested Frelick again, throwing a four-seamer for the second consecutive pitch, this one hanging over the inner third of the plate. Frelick didn't miss it, crushing a solo shot to tie the score at four apiece.

Bedlam ensued at American Family Field. Yellow towels twirled in unison throughout the stands. And as the stadium erupted, so did Frelick, rounding first base. He pointed toward his bullpen. They pointed back. Because for these Brewers, deficits have become invitations. Adversity only fuels the congregation.

Winning feels inevitable, as Milwaukee's 5-4 victory in Game 2 demonstrated.

Frelick, who'd hit just four regular-season home runs, didn't deliver the final blow. Cooper Pratt did. Yet he made certain the Brewers would have the chance to land it.

"He hit four lasers tonight," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said. "He hit four balls right on the screws. We talked about it in pregame, he's got the 'it factor.'"

The "it factor" that Murphy sees in Frelick was forged on the high school football field, where he earned Massachusetts Gatorade Player of the Year honors as a quarterback in 2017.

The "it factor" was cultivated on ice skates, in a state where hockey borders on religion.

The "it factor" was instilled by his father, Jeff, who played football at the University of Pittsburgh before injuries cut his career short.

The "it factor" was fortified through doubt. For all his accolades, Frelick still had to fight to be seen. At just 5-foot-8, he was accustomed to being overlooked.

"Everybody's built differently," Murphy said. "Everybody expresses it differently. I think that Sal was a young man that had very good physical skills, but he wasn't big in stature like a lot of these guys are. So, as a smaller guy, I think he always was having to prove how great he was. He has this edge about him that he believes in himself."

It's an edge Murphy likened to longtime Red Sox second baseman Dustin Pedroia, whom he coached at Arizona State. Pedroia possessed physical gifts Frelick doesn't, but both share an insatiable desire to dominate, regardless of what their natural abilities suggest.

"Murph will tell you firsthand that it's never pretty watching me take a swing or run or field or whatever it might be," Frelick said. "I just always felt like that was where I was able to get my edge was just kind of turn everything into a competition. Put myself in the box and iso me and the pitcher. Or on defense, just iso me and the ball. How can I beat it?"

Maybe not the prettiest swing, but the prettiest of outcomes. A reminder of how October has a way of washing away the tribulations endured just to get there. How an entire regular season can be reduced to a footnote when a player delivers in the postseason.

Because Frelick's regular season was anything but memorable. He hit just .230 with a .614 OPS.

A right shoulder injury sidelined him for six weeks, costing him half of July and most of August. And when he returned, his role had diminished. Though the Brewers have long embraced platoons, Frelick found himself largely relegated to facing right-handed pitching.

Luis Lara, the 21-year-old prospect called up in July, had taken some of Frelick's playing time down the stretch. But Frelick didn't sulk. His focus only sharpened. His emotions, rarely on display, are encased in steel. He can take tough coaching.

In May, Murphy acknowledged he didn't tell Frelick he loved him enough. So he scribbled himself a reminder: Tell Sal, "I love you, Sal."

Then he thought better of it. Crossed it out.

"You know what? He doesn't need me to tell him that," Murphy said.

Beneath the humor was a truth: Frelick doesn't need constant reassurance. His competitiveness, his confidence, his unwavering sense of self -- all nurtured over time.

"It was great," Frelick said of his home run. "Like, sort of this blackout feeling. Then it kind of ends. We were tied. We weren't winning or anything."

The Brewers now have a chance to capture the first World Series championship in franchise history as the series shifts to Tropicana Field, where the Rays have been dominant, going 59-27 this season, including the postseason.

It will be another test for Milwaukee in a series decided by one run in the first two games. The Rays won't go quietly.

Yet at every turn, Milwaukee has answered the challenge.

And on Saturday night, Frelick answered his.

Because as leaves fall and flowers wilt, Frelick has bloomed.
