#4920. Ripple Wins Final OCC Approval for Trust Bank Behind $2.6B RLUSD Stablecoin

Ripple Wins Final OCC Approval for Trust Bank Behind $2.6B RLUSD Stablecoin

Ripple has received final approval from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency to establish Ripple National Trust Bank, a federally supervised national trust bank, the company said on Monday.

The approval completes a process that began when the OCC conditionally approved Ripple's application in December 2025, alongside those of Circle, Paxos, Fidelity Digital Assets and BitGo.

Once open, the bank will manage the segregated reserves backing the Ripple USD (RLUSD) stablecoin, act as collateral trustee of those reserves for the benefit of RLUSD holders, and provide fiduciary digital asset custody services to enterprises. RLUSD will still be issued by Standard Custody & Trust Company, Ripple's limited purpose trust company regulated by the New York Department of Financial Services, so the stablecoin will now sit under both state and federal supervision.

"For years, we've said crypto and banks work better together. Final approval for Ripple National Trust Bank is what that looks like in practice," Ripple CEO Brad Garlinghouse said in the release. In a tweet, he added: "The revolution isn't happening outside the system. It's happening from within it."

#4918. OCC calls on community banks to enter the stablecoin payments market

The U.S. Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) on Monday called on community banks to enter the stablecoin market, including by applying for permission to issue their own dollar-pegged tokens.

The regulator said it wants community banks to help shape the future of payments and encouraged them to apply directly or through subsidiaries to become permitted stablecoin issuers under the GENIUS Act.

“These opportunities complement the OCC’s Community Bank Comeback initiatives to right-size regulation and tailor supervision so community banks can grow, compete, and continue serving their communities,” said the OCC, the independent bureau of the Treasury Department that regulates and supervises national banks, federal savings associations and federal branches and agencies of foreign banks.

The invitation comes as U.S. banking groups have pressed lawmakers to restrict stablecoin yields, concerned that interest-paying tokens could draw deposits away from traditional banks. The OCC is now trying to motivate smaller lenders to enter the same market, rather than leave stablecoin payments to large banks and crypto companies.

In a separate bulletin published Oct. 21, the OCC said stablecoin issuers must comply with its newly finalized GENIUS Act regulations starting Jan. 18, 2027, when federal approval requirements take effect.

Existing issuers that submit substantially complete applications by that date may request temporary waivers allowing them to continue operating while regulators review their applications. The OCC said it generally expects to grant such waivers where appropriate.

The regulator has yet to begin accepting applications but said the process will open after the required federal paperwork approval is completed.

The OCC has recently been under fire. The Independent Community Bankers of America (ICBA) sued it, accusing it of exceeding its legal authority in granting national trust-bank status to crypto firms.

However, the OCC’s series of trust charter approvals for crypto firms is different, as those firms do not operate under the same business model or intend to offer the same services as typical community banks, an OCC spokesperson told CoinDesk recently. Senator Elizabeth Warren asked the OCC to explain its chartering of nine crypto-focused institutions, which she argued do not meet federal regulations and constitute a risk to the financial system.

#4917. Bat Beauty Contest returns October 24

Published Oct 20, 2026 10:30 AM EDT

With Alaska’s fat bears done packing on their winter weight and spooky season in full swing, it’s time to turn our collective animal-loving attention to a smaller mammal—bats. The Bureau of Land Management’s (BLM) annual Bat Beauty Contest returns on October 24 and promises “plenty of batty puns.”

Beginning Saturday, October 24, BLM will post photos of the competitors to their Facebook and Instagram accounts and ask you to vote for the best-looking one. All of the competing bats are part of wild populations that live on land managed by the public agency and are photographed by wildlife technicians. The winner will be announced (appropriately) on Saturday, October 31.

The bats in this competition are known for their squishy faces and clever names. In 2024, a hoary bat (Lasiurus cinereus) from Oregon named Hoary Potter took the crown. Other contestants included a long-eared myotis (Myotis evotis) named Honey Bunches of MyOtis, a myotis bat (Myotis myotis) named Robert Battinson, and western small-footed bats (Myotis ciliolabrum) named Batt Damon and Batlor Swift. The contest was not held in 2025 due to the 43-day-long federal government shutdown.

The contest is not all bat puns and a way to keep the spirit of Fat Bear Week alive. It falls during Bat Week (October 24-31). Bat Week raises awareness about the Earth’s only flying mammal. More than just a Halloween symbol, bats play a critical ecological role as plant pollinators and through eating insects that can threaten crops and forests around the world. According to Bat Week, one bat can eat up to its body weight in insects every night. Only three species of bats eat animal blood, with two specializing in bird blood. Female lesser long-nosed bats even make tequila possible. Every spring, they migrate from south-central Mexico up to the southwestern United States, following “nectar corridors” of columnar cacti and agave plants. They pollinate agave plants, which are then used to make tequila and mezcal. When they feed on cactus fruits and nectar, they may also help disperse cacti seeds. Other bats eat pollen, nectar, or fruit.

Bats are increasingly threatened due to habitat loss, light pollution, and diseases like white-nose syndrome. This fungal disease infects the skin of the muzzle, ears, and wings of hibernating bats. In about 20 years, it has killed millions of bats across North America and affects about half of the species on the continent. Bats also are often misunderstood as carriers of diseases such as rabies.

The planet is home to over 1,400 species of bats, and they make up almost 20 percent of all mammal species. Bats also can be found almost everywhere on Earth, even in desert and polar regions. To look up which bat species live near you, you can use Bat Week’s state bat database.

Regular aerobic exercise may help postmenopausal women strengthen executive function, according to new research.

The study, published in the Journal of Alzheimer’s Disease on August 1, suggests that cognitive benefits may appear within 3 to 6 months. This highlights that exercise can be a practical strategy for preserving brain health after menopause.

Brain health strategies are crucial during and after menopause, with two-thirds of cases of Alzheimer’s disease occurring in women. Menopause can influence cognitive decline, as well as resilience.

Dung Trinh, MD, a physician at MemorialCare Medical Group and Chief Medical Officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, said the findings are encouraging, but noted some limitations.

“This was a secondary analysis of a relatively small study, so I would view it as important evidence that deserves confirmation rather than as definitive proof,” he told Healthline.

The study included 93 cognitively healthy females ages 20 to 67, 43% of whom were postmenopausal. Each participant had below-average cardiorespiratory fitness and didn’t exercise regularly.

The participants were randomly assigned to groups for either a 6-month aerobic exercise program or a stretching-and-toning program.

The greatest cognitive benefits were seen in individuals who engaged in 45 minutes of aerobic activity four times a week, who saw improvements in executive function. These cognitive gains were first observed at 3 months and strengthened through the remainder of the program.

Executive function involves working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. In daily life, this includes managing tasks, making decisions, staying organized, and maintaining independence.

“Stretching and mobility work have important benefits — namely, flexibility, comfort, range of motion, balance, and stress reduction, to name a few. However, aerobic activity produces a more sustained cardiorespiratory and metabolic stimulus,” said Kecia Gaither, MD, a double board certified OB-GYN and maternal fetal medicine specialist, and associate professor of Clinical Obstetrics and Gynecology at Weill Cornell Medicine. Gaither wasn’t involved in the study.

Aerobic activity is rhythmic and continuous, increasing your breathing and heart rate. Moderate intensity means you can talk but not sing while participating in the activity.

Aerobic exercise can affect the brain in various ways.

“Exercise may help by improving cardiovascular fitness, reducing vascular risk factors, and supporting brain plasticity, all of which are relevant because vascular and metabolic health are closely tied to later cognitive decline,” Trinh said.

While aerobic exercise doesn’t prevent Alzheimer’s disease or slow the progression of dementia, it can help improve overall cognitive function as you age.

One of the ways aerobic exercise can affect the brain is in its structure. It can help protect brain regions such as the hippocampus, which is important for memory.

Aerobic exercise can also benefit the brain’s white matter. Regular aerobic activity may support connections in the brain that help with focus and processing information.

Short-term workouts can also have immediate cognitive benefits, like boosting thinking skills right after you work out.

#4909. New comet accidentally discovered by space telescope

Published Oct 20, 2026 11:15 AM EDT

Launched in 2023, the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Euclid space telescope observes some of the most distant regions of the visible universe for signs of dark matter and dark energy. But even while focused on the “big picture,” Euclid’s onboard equipment is surprising astronomers by capturing tiny photobombers like errant comets.

Its recent portraits of one specific dusty space rock are especially impressive, however. Euclid spotted a never-before-seen comet sailing through space more than 807.7 million miles from Earth. The telescope isn’t designed to automatically flag an image interloper. Instead, the bright blemish seen in multiple images was spotted by a moving object detection program designed by Mikko Pöntinen, an astronomer at the University of Helsinki in Finland.

“I was excited to see my software flag a ‘streak’ smeared across several images—the result of a fast-moving object,” said Pöntinen. “Further inspection revealed that it had a fuzzy cloud around it, indicative of an active object like a comet that is shedding dust and gas.”

Pöntinen quickly relayed his findings to the International Astronomical Union’s Minor Planet Center (MPC), which then passed the news along to researchers around the world. On October 8, the MPC confirmed the newly discovered comet, which is now classified C/2026 S1 (Euclid).

C/2026 S1 was seen from five different fields of view over multiple days in the Euclid Deep Field North region of the telescope’s scans. Astronomers now know the comet’s trajectory isn’t located on the same horizontal plane as the planets. Instead, its path is technically taking it over the solar system along a multi-thousand-year orbit.

Euclid’s discovery is particularly striking because the telescope’s six-year mission is specifically mapped to avoid areas of the sky that are laden with comets and asteroids.

“Very few comets are expected to be discovered by Euclid, so it was a happy coincidence,” added Euclid archive scientist Bruno Altieri.

#4907. Ten Decrees: Old Masters, New Money

Lucho Poletti on redrawing Hieronymus Bosch and Pieter Bruegel the Elder around money, Nixon’s 1971 decree, and why bitcoin art keeps returning to the old masters.

On August 15, 1971, Richard Nixon announced that he had directed Treasury Secretary John Connally “to suspend temporarily the convertibility of the dollar into gold.” The suspension was never lifted. Fifty-five years later to the day, Lucho Poletti unveiled the first of ten drawings responding to that decree.

Europe had experienced its own monetary upheavals. Hieronymus Bosch and Pieter Bruegel the Elder worked during an era of expanding banking, credit, and speculation. Antwerp, where Bruegel worked, became one of Europe’s great commercial centers. Both painters populated their compositions with hundreds of figures, constructing elaborate worlds in which human behavior, social order, and moral judgment could be examined simultaneously.

Bitcoin artists have increasingly returned to this visual tradition. Pepenardo’s Master Cuts Out the Stone, exhibited by BMAG earlier this year, reimagined Jan Sanders van Hemessen’s The Surgeon as a study of who extracts and who is extracted from. Carlijn Kingma’s The Waterworks of Money, shown at Bitcoin Amsterdam last year, depicted the modern monetary system as an interconnected landscape. Complex systems seem to invite pictures that hold everything in a single frame.

Lucho Poletti has made bitcoin-related art since 2017, describing his practice as visual propaganda for the monetary revolution. A contributor to the Bitcoin Conference art gallery since 2021, he returns to Bitcoin Amsterdam this November with Ten Decrees.

The series reimagines ten paintings, four by Bosch and six by Bruegel, around money. Fiat, Latin for “let it be done,” becomes the organizing principle. Nine titles issue commands: let there be fury, profit, chaff, a game, folly, hunger, refuge, collapse, and ash. The tenth, Ultima Fiat, reverses that authority: let it be the last.

Unveiled on August 15, 2026, the anniversary of Nixon’s announcement, the series follows a biweekly release schedule beginning August 29 and concluding January 3, 2027, the anniversary of Bitcoin’s Genesis Block. Each drawing contains hundreds of figures and objects, with inscriptions often visible only at close range. The 30-by-20-inch prints, produced in editions of ten, form a composition spanning approximately four meters when arranged in two rows of five.

Five drawings have been released, most recently Fiat Moria, after Bruegel’s Netherlandish Proverbs, on October 24. The sixth, Fiat Fames, follows November 7.

Ahead of Bitcoin Amsterdam, November 5–6, I spoke with Poletti about the recurring influence of Bosch and Bruegel on bitcoin art, the relationship between allegory and propaganda, and translating his densely detailed drawings to the conference’s main-stage LED installation.

BMAG: Bosch and Bruegel seem to be having a synchronistic moment among artists interested in Bitcoin. Both worked in what is now Belgium and the Netherlands, during a period when Antwerp was emerging as a major financial center and the Habsburgs were debasing coinage. What is it about transitional periods (like the current one) that draws artists back to Bosch and Bruegel and their densely populated landscapes?

Lucho Poletti: Because they had already solved the problem I was stuck with: how do you draw a whole society going wrong at once? Bosch and Bruegel put hundreds of people in a single frame, each busy with their own concerns. The system shows up in the sum of it. You don’t look at a Bruegel, you search it. It’s a great way to depict the effects of money, which nobody really sees and everybody feels.

They also lived through a money problem a lot like ours. Silver from the Americas poured into Europe through Seville and on to the bankers in Antwerp. Prices across Western Europe rose sixfold over 150 years. Philip II defaulted in 1557 and the crash spread across all of Europe. Two years later Bruegel painted Netherlandish Proverbs, a village where everyone is absorbed in something foolish and nobody can see the whole.

The four Bosch pieces are about appetite: the temptation, and the bill that shows up after. The six Bruegels are about the crowd, and how society behaves once the rules stop making sense.

BMAG: The series draws on the original meaning of the F-word every bitcoiner knows. Nine of your titles use fiat as a command, while the tenth, Ultima Fiat, seems to reverse that authority. How did you arrive at Latin as the language of the series, and why does the final decree change speaker?

Lucho Poletti: Fiat was never a noun. In Latin it’s a verb, “let it be done.” It’s the word God speaks in the Latin Genesis before the light comes on: fiat lux. Someone with authority says so and it happens. That’s a literal description of how fiat money comes into existence, so I wanted the verb in every title.

Latin wasn’t a stretch for me. I’ve titled work in it for years: Moneta Divina, Cypher Deos, and Novus Moneta Seclorum dating back to 2020. The dollar already speaks it, too. Turn over a one-dollar bill and there are three Latin mottos on the back. Ultima Fiat, Fiat Lucrum and Furia Fiat had their names before the series did. Once there were three decrees in concept, I kept going until I had ten, and the name for the whole series became obvious. Ten Decrees has the Ten Commandments sitting under it, which suits work rebuilt out of painted sermons. In Fiat Lucrum there’s a lighthouse at the top left with FIAT LUX GENESIS 1:3 engraved into it. The title is the phrase rewritten for the money printers: let there be light turned into let there be profit.

Nine of the decrees are issued to you, in the flat voice an institution uses. Let there be profit, let there be hunger, let there be a game. The tenth is the only one we get to say back. Ultima fiat means “let it be the last,” which is closer to a plea than an order. Every one of us has been living under the first nine since 1971, whether we noticed or not, and Bitcoin is how we issue the tenth. It closes the series on January 3, the anniversary of the Genesis Block, which also happens to be my birthday.

BMAG: Bosch and Bruegel constructed extraordinarily complex visual worlds where allegory, satire, religious symbolism, and everyday life coexist. Their paintings invite close inspection but rarely offer a single, definitive interpretation. In Ten Decrees, you’re borrowing their compositions while introducing a more explicit critique of the monetary system. How do you approach the relationship between ambiguity and message in your work?

Lucho Poletti: The words carry the message so the people don’t have to. Bosch’s Garden of Earthly Delights doesn’t have a single word in it. Fiat Lucrum has more than fifty lettered objects, and some are as blunt as a protest sign: four money presses lettered QE-1, QE-2, QE-3 and QE-∞, and a little hand-lettered card on a broker’s desk that reads genius, we print, you pay, have a nice day. Nobody has to wonder what I think of the money system.

I keep the ambiguity in the people. In Fiat Lucrum there’s a crowd cheering a woman who holds up a $ on a golden staff under a sign that says ETF, and that crowd is wearing my mask. So is the man signing the leverage contract, and so is the woman taking the mortgage. In Fiat Palea a man in the mask is knee-deep in a pile of printed bills. Bosch drew his own neighbors into the middle of the garden, and I drew mine. If the ₿ on a forehead were a badge of honor, I wouldn’t hand it out like that. It’s more a badge of the aware, the initiated. Knowing the money is broken doesn’t make one a saint.

I’m careful with endings too. These pieces show the exit while not promising a way out. Near the middle of Fiat Moria there’s a lit doorway with MONEY ₿ BETTER WORLD over the arch, and a plain-faced man on the step looking back at the square. He hasn’t gone in yet, and nobody’s stopping him. Where you’d be standing in that picture is your call.

BMAG: In Fiat Lucrum, your reinterpretation of Bosch’s Garden of Earthly Delights, you introduce a system of waterworks: pipes, reservoirs, and flowing liquidity. Bosch often allowed viewers to recognize things that the figures within his paintings could not. What possibilities does that kind of visual allegory offer you when depicting something as abstract as the monetary system?

Lucho Poletti: What Bosch never had in his garden was plumbing, so I gave it some. Water organizes the whole of Fiat Lucrum. It starts clean high in the mountains on the left, pours out of a machine in Eden and settles in a reflecting basin. Cut into the basin’s wall there’s an inspection window with fish swimming behind the glass. Somebody built a viewing port into the reservoir so you could check it, and every full node on the Bitcoin network is that window.

Downstream the water widens into a lake. In the middle sits a glass tank lettered FRESH LIQUIDITY, with BOTTOMLESS engraved on the bowl, and eight people are having the party of their lives inside it. The valve wheel that controls the flow is on top of the dome, outside the glass, where none of them can reach it. Monetary policy gets set by someone who isn’t at the party. And the tank leaks. From there the water runs orange, down a stream and over a waterfall into a pool where a tick with a $ on its cheek drinks it through a hose.

That’s the Bosch trick you’re describing. The bathers can’t see the leak or the valve, and you can see both from across the room. Richard Cantillon worked out around 1730 that new money helps whoever gets it first and costs whoever gets it last. I drew it as a watercourse because everybody already understands water: whoever’s upstream drinks first, and everyone downstream gets what’s left.

BMAG: Looking across the series, are there particular details, references, or artistic decisions that you feel are especially important but might be overlooked by viewers? What would you want someone encountering these drawings for the first time to spend more time looking at?

Lucho Poletti: Up close is where these work, and most of what matters in them is small.

Start with the plain faces. Everybody spots the Satoshi masks and the $ skulls first because they’re the loud ones, but the plain faces belong to the people the system is happening to. In Furia Fiat a line of bald men crawl on their hands and knees with red tubes plugged into the backs of their skulls, and the red is the same color as the fluid in a bowl a few meters away that says TIME. That’s what’s being drained out of them. In Fiat Palea, Adam and Eve stand under a serpent with a $ for an eye. They’re wearing the mask, but neither of them has a ₿ on the brow yet.

Then look at how characters connect across a distance. In Fiat Ludus a sack lettered STIMULUS sits on a bench next to an older figure, and a little way off a kid walks away with the same sack on his back, lettered THE BILL. A blindfolded boy feels around with his arms out while the man lunging in beside him holds up a bunch of $ balloons the boy can’t see. Even the enrollment forms are signed by Seymore Debt and Max D. Out.

In Fiat Palea a broken crate stenciled EXECUTIVE ORDER 6102 sits in the straw, and further back, bolted across a vault, a plate reads WINDOW, NO REDEMPTION. Roosevelt took the gold in 1933 and Nixon closed the window in 1971, and I put the two close enough that you read one and then find the other. Up higher in the same piece, a guy in the mask with WHOOPS across his vest is pouring gasoline on a burning bank. In Furia Fiat, the $ skulls stand on GNOSIS and clutch books of knowledge, keeping them for themselves.

The bubbles are dated. Fiat Ludus has a sphere with tulips growing out of the top, marked 1637 and 1720, and another with a hen sitting on a WALL STREET egg, marked 1929 and 2008. Variety’s front page the day after the 1929 crash read WALL ST. LAYS AN EGG. Some of the skies have thin straight lines of cloud behind the smoke, the kind a plane leaves.

Watch the prices, too. A price in dollars only goes up: at CANTILLON’S hot dog stand in Fiat Ludus the 5¢ is crossed out and the sign says $9. A price in sats only goes down: a fish stall in Fiat Moria has 4000 crossed out, then 2000, and now it’s 1000 SATS. In Fiat Ludus, the carrots changing hands have dropped from 7,500 to 3,000 SATS on the chalkboard next to the dollar hopscotch.

The Latin is there to be read. MORITURI TE SALUTANT, those who are about to die salute you, is carved around the rim of the arena in Fiat Ludus. EX NIHILO, out of nothing, is cut over an empty vault in Fiat Palea. In Fiat Moria the $ skull leaning out of the window has a plaque on either side of him: MUNDUS VULT DECIPI, the world wants to be deceived, and $TULTORUM INFINITUS EST NUMERUS, the number of fools is infinite, with a $ for the S. Fiat Lucrum has a little panel that runs a whole life in four stops: school, debt, work, death.

And in Fiat Moria there’s a small round time machine with NO RETURN on its side and 1944-2026 on the dial. A man is climbing out of it, staring at the village. 1944 is Bretton Woods, when the world tied its money to the dollar and the dollar to gold. He left then and he’s only just arrived, and he can’t quite believe what he’s looking at.

None of that comes with a caption. You find it by standing close for a long time, the way you would with a Bruegel, and the finding is the whole point.

BMAG: Each drawing is 18,000 pixels wide, populated by hundreds of figures, with every Satoshi mask drawn by hand and details that only reveal themselves up close. You’ve described the work as “crafted, not generated.” At a time when AI can convincingly imitate the Old Masters, what significance does that labor have for you, and how does it shape the way you want these works to be experienced?

Lucho Poletti: “Crafted, not generated” is a phrase I came up with to sum up where the work in these pieces actually is. There are machines in my process, but they’re only one part of it. A model I trained on years of my own work does the rough first pass on a composition. I think of it as an apprentice. Bosch came out of a family workshop, and the masters of his day leaned on assistants for the groundwork so their own hours could go into the meaning. Mine roughs out the scene and gives me a base to build toward the standard they set. Then I spend weeks on each piece in a grueling manual process in Photoshop, making the decisions and drawing in hundreds of figures, objects, symbols and phrases until the art says what I mean.

AI can imitate the look of a Bosch or a Bruegel pretty convincingly now. What it can’t do is mean anything. It won’t decide that the valve on the liquidity tank in Fiat Lucrum belongs outside the glass, where the bathers can’t reach it. It won’t decide that the slop on the ground should be piggy banks wired into the money presses, each with its own name: SAVINGS, PENSION, NEST EGG. It doesn’t make jokes either. It’s never going to sit a fool in a jester’s cap on a platform lettered TAKE IT AT FACE VALUE while a $ skull does his makeup. My personal philosophy is that if I don’t do the majority of the work, it’s not my art. I hold myself to that, and anyone who looks at these up close can see a person made the decisions that matter.

That’s how I’d like people to take these in: up close, and for a long time. I don’t ask anyone to trust me on how they were made. The work answers that better than I can.

BMAG: You’ve been part of the Bitcoin Conference since 2021, and this November Ten Decrees comes to the main stage at Bitcoin Amsterdam, not far from the historical centers where Bosch and Bruegel worked. These drawings are extraordinarily detailed, designed to reward close inspection. How are you approaching their translation to monumental LED screens, and does exhibiting them in the Netherlands carry any particular significance for you?

Lucho Poletti: The prints were meant for one person standing close to search the work and find details that speak to them. An LED wall gets a thousand people at fifty meters, which is an amazing opportunity to create a visual experience for the still pieces, so I’ve done my best to create a compelling one for each piece.

Each one gets fifteen seconds, in the order they were released. The big center wall carries the center of each piece in one slow pass from left to right, so the room gets the shape of it. The screen on the right opens on the Bosch or Bruegel it came from, with its name and date, and halfway through it wipes across to mine. The screen on the left side pulls out lateral details you’d never catch from the floor, like the ship of fools riding the mast in Fiat Moria, or the pigs feeding at the DEBASED trough. All five released so far play back to back as one loop, old master and new money side by side the whole way through.

The Netherlands matters to me. Bosch spent his whole life in ’s-Hertogenbosch, about an hour south of Amsterdam by train. Some of Bruegel’s proverbs are still in use in Dutch: people still say someone hangs his cloak to the wind when he turns with the crowd, and in Fiat Moria, a man wearing a Satoshi mask jumps off a mountain, holding his cloak in his hand—a reference to the Dutch proverb about changing one’s position to suit the prevailing winds.

Amsterdam has its own chapter in this story. The Bank of Amsterdam opened in 1609 to give merchants a money nobody could debase, and for over a century its bank guilder traded at a premium over ordinary coin because people trusted the silver was in the vault. David Hume held it up as the model of a fully backed bank. It had been lending to the Dutch East India Company quietly, and when that became public in 1790 the premium vanished and the bank declared itself insolvent. I can’t think of a better city to show a series about monetary decrees and trust, two months before the last one lands on the Genesis Block anniversary.

  • A new study found that women with macromastia (enlarged breasts) had higher odds of chronic migraine, neck pain, and obstructive sleep apnea than women without the condition. 
  • The findings suggest that macromastia may lead to more than physical discomfort, potentially contributing to multiple health conditions that affect quality of life. 
  • Women with macromastia experiencing frequent headaches, neck pain, or poor sleep quality should talk with their doctor to learn about their treatment options. 
  • General macromastia is often treated with breast reduction surgery, but other strategies, such as a supportive bra and physical therapy, may help alleviate discomfort.

Enlarged breast tissue, known medically as macromastia or breast hypertrophy, is relatively common among women.

The exact prevalence of the condition is not fully understood, but some research estimates that macromastia affects around 1–5% of women.

This estimate climbs after menopause, with around 18.6% of women experiencing an increase in breast size, which may be linked to weight gain and hormonal shifts.

However, macromastia can occur during other life stages, such as puberty or pregnancy.

Women with disproportionately large breasts often experience discomfort and pain, and may turn to breast reduction surgery for relief. 

Now, new research suggests that macromastia is associated with more than just physical pain.

A cross-sectional study found that enlarged breasts are associated with higher rates of chronic migraine, neck pain, cervical radiculopathy (a pinched nerve in the neck), and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

The findings, published September 22 in Headache: The Journal of Head and Face Pain, add to a growing body of research examining how macromastia may affect overall health and quality of life. 

“Many people think of enlarged breast tissue primarily as a cosmetic issue, but our findings suggest it may be associated with a broader range of women’s health concerns, including chronic migraine, neck pain and sleep-related breathing disorders,” lead study author Kristyn Pocock, MD, assistant professor of neurology at Wake Forest University School of Medicine, said in a news release.

“By better understanding these connections, we can help ensure women receive more comprehensive evaluations and care for symptoms that may affect their daily lives.” 

Macromastia linked to 40% higher prevalence of chronic migraine

To better understand how macromastia may impact quality of life, researchers reviewed medical records from 687 females treated at headache and neurology clinics affiliated with Stanford Medical Center from 2017 to 2024. 

The cohort included 347 females with macromastia and 340 without.

After accounting for factors including age, body mass index (BMI), depression, and anxiety, researchers found that those with macromastia were:

  • 40% more likely to have chronic migraine (a headache on at least 15 days per month for more than 3 months)
  • roughly twice as likely to experience neck pain
  • 54% more likely to have obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)
  • more than twice as likely to have cervical radiculopathy, a condition caused by irritation or compression of nerves in the neck

The study was a retrospective, cross-sectional analysis, meaning researchers reviewed existing medical records rather than following participants over time.

This means the study does not prove that macromastia causes migraine, neck pain, or sleep apnea, warranting further investigation.

“These findings raise important questions about how enlarged breast tissue may contribute to painful symptoms for women,” Pocock said. “More research is needed to determine whether treating macromastia could help certain women find relief and enhance quality of life.” 

Why macromastia may affect the neck, head, and sleep

Researchers don’t yet have an explanation for why macromastia is associated with these health conditions.

One possibility, Pocock previously suggested, is mechanical strain. 

Larger breasts can place additional load on the neck, shoulders, and upper back, which may contribute to musculoskeletal pain and nerve irritation. 

Pocock has also discussed how other factors may play a role, such as:

  • inflammation
  • hormonal effects
  • mood changes
  • physical activity
  • sleep quality

Further studies are needed to determine the exact role these cofounding factors may play in the possible health effects of macromastia.

Neck pain may drive breast reduction treatment decisions

The researchers examined breast reduction surgery among people with macromastia. 

Of the symptoms studied, neck pain was significantly associated with undergoing breast reduction surgery, known as a reduction mammoplasty. 

Migraine, sleep apnea, and cervical radiculopathy were not significantly associated with this surgical treatment. 

This finding raises questions about whether some symptoms associated with macromastia may be overlooked when people seek treatment, suggesting another avenue for further research.

Could breast reduction improve symptoms?

Preliminary evidence indicates that breast reduction surgery may help some people with headaches, but the evidence is still limited.

In a small 2025 prospective pilot study, which was also co-authored by Pocock, 34 women with symptomatic macromastia and headaches underwent breast reduction. At follow-up, 79% reported at least a 50% reduction in monthly headache days. Headache frequency, severity, and related disability also improved. 

Another pilot study led by Pocock published in 2026 found improvements in headache frequency, severity, disability, and sleep apnea risk after surgery. The sample size for this study was also small, with 34 participants, highlighting the need for further research.

The researchers also emphasized that breast reduction should not yet be considered a treatment for headaches because larger studies are still needed. 

Sherry Ross, MD, co-founder of Oneself, Intimate Skin Care, and the Pair-a-Docs Podcast, said that women with macromastia experiencing chronic pain may wish to consider breast reduction surgery.

“In my 30+ years as a practicing OB-GYN, I have never met anyone who regretted having breast reduction surgery,” she told Healthline. 

Talking with your doctor about macromastia

If you live with macromastia and experience frequent headaches, persistent neck or shoulder pain, numbness or tingling, loud snoring, or significant daytime fatigue, it’s important to discuss all of your symptoms with your doctor. 

A comprehensive evaluation can help guide you to the treatment that you need, whether it’s breast reduction surgery or other methods. 

Your healthcare team may recommend a more supportive bra, physical therapy, or an NSAID or other pain reliever to help you find relief. 

“The most important step in improving your comfort and health is to get a properly fitted bra,” Ross said. 

“There are dedicated bra stores that can properly fit larger-breasted women with full-coverage, well-supported bras, with and without underwire… both for everyday wear and for exercise.”

Ross emphasized the importance of proper breast support for regular physical activity, a first-line defense against other health conditions like obesity, diabetes, and heart disease. 

She recommended exercises that are better suited to larger breasts to avoid excessive breast bouncing and discomfort, such as: 

  • swimming
  • cycling
  • walking
  • strength and resistance training

“Strength training that focuses on the upper body and core is helpful as well,” Ross said. “Select workout clothing that is comfortable, breathable, and moisture-wicking to avoid excessive sweat and moisture buildup.”

Lastly, Ross advised maintaining a healthy weight and eating a well-balanced, plant-forward diet to support overall health and longevity.

#4875. Roman sarcophagus inside Westminster Abbey originally held a different body

Published Oct 20, 2026 7:01 PM EDT

A formerly inexplicable Roman sarcophagus discovered at England’s Westminster Abbey over 150 years ago is likely a rare example of medieval funerary recycling. Although archaeologists stopped short of definitively identifying who was buried inside, the team confidently narrowed down their candidates to a pair of high-ranking Benedictine monks.

“We have two viable contenders for the occupant of the sarcophagus,” said Westminster Abbey archaeological consultant Warwick Rodwell, co-author of an accompanying study published today in the Journal of the British Archaeological Association.

The mysterious limestone tomb was originally crafted during the 4th century CE, when the Roman empire’s jurisdiction still extended as far away as Britain. By the mid-13th century, King Henry III constructed a Benedictine sacristy above the sarcophagus, and it remained undisturbed for nearly 600 years. In 1820, workers accidentally cracked its lid into six pieces after unintentionally exposing the tomb. It would take until 1869 for archaeologists to finally repair the damage, as well as discover its confounding origins.

The Roman sarcophagus has baffled researchers ever since. The limestone enclosure featured an inscription to Valerius Amandinus, a rich local who lived nearby sometime between 300 and 400 CE. But the sarcophagus lid clearly displayed a much more recent, medieval Latin cross. The cross implied the sarcophagus possibly housed someone other than Amandinus, but who?

Extensive stonework analysis and skeletal carbon dating finally point towards two probable suspects. The first possibility is the Westminster Abbot Aelfwig, who died around 1020. The second is his successor, Wulnoth, who died in 1049. Further skeletal examinations indicate the man was 35 to 49 years old and around 5-foot 9-inches tall when he died. Interestingly, this contradicts the initial 19th century theory of a slightly shorter, “robust young man” less than 30 years old.

“We do not know the date of birth of either. But either would fit equally well if we assume the Abbot was in his early twenties when the appointment was made and close to fifty when he died,” explained Rodwell.

If Rodwell had to pick between the two, he may lean towards Aelfwig, since he was already a Westminster monk during that era.

The monk’s associates likely first placed him in a timber coffin that they lowered into the sarcophagus. Next, they sealed it with a re-carved cover displaying the Latin cross. But regardless of who resided in the tomb, its reuse wasn’t considered a slight. Instead, its original craftsmanship was intended to honor the deceased monk. It’s unlikely the mourning monks gave much thought to anyone else’s opinion on burial recycling, especially those living nearly 1,000 years after their respected Abbot’s passing.

#4870. NBA Star Steph Curry Didn’t Realize His Blurry Vision Was a Sign of an Eye Disease

  • NBA All-Star Stephen Curry is opening up about living with keratoconus, an eye condition that can cause blurry or distorted vision.
  • Curry was diagnosed after former Golden State Warriors general manager Bob Myers noticed him squinting during a game and encouraged him to get his eyes checked.
  • Keratoconus can worsen over time, but early diagnosis and treatment can help correct vision and may help prevent the condition from progressing.

Twelve-time NBA All-Star and two-time MVP Stephen Curry is known for leading the Golden State Warriors to four NBA championships.

In addition to basketball accolades, being part of the Warriors dynasty made him aware of an eye condition he was living with called keratoconus (KC).

The condition causes the cornea to thin and bulge outward into an irregular cone shape instead of staying round.

This can cause blurry or distorted vision, increased sensitivity to light, glare and nighttime halos, frequent or rapid changes in eyeglass prescription, and eye irritation or redness.

“I didn’t know anything about it before I went to the doctor, nor did I really know that I had truly blurry vision or, like, distorted images and stuff,” Curry told Healthline. “It was kind of just the way I saw things, and for whatever reason, I didn’t ask the questions or go have an eye screen and all that until our general manager for the Warriors, his name’s Bob Myers, [pointed it out].”

In 2018, Myers was watching Curry play on TV and noticed that when the camera zoomed in on Curry sitting on the bench, he was squinting as he watched his teammates and looked at the Jumbotron. Myers took a photo of Curry squinting on the TV and texted it to him after the game.

“He’s like, ‘Hey, you were doing this the whole time they had you on camera. Have you gotten your eyes checked?’” said Curry. “And then that was the first idea of like, ‘Wait, is there something off about the way, you know, my vision was?’ Thankfully, I had access to go to an eye doctor and got a screen. And that was the first time I heard the word keratoconus.”

How treatment for keratoconus changed Curry’s vision

At 29 years old, Curry was diagnosed with KC and learned that it’s a progressive eye disease that can worsen over time.

“[There’s] a certain time window of your life [when it becomes] an irreversible sight-threatening condition,” he said.

Randy McLaughlin, OD, associate professor of clinical ophthalmology at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, said the condition can worsen if not treated and may make obtaining a driver’s license or working difficult.

He said people with the condition are usually fitted with rigid gas permeable contact lenses, which are firm lenses made of breathable plastic and silicone that allow oxygen to pass directly through to the cornea. Some people get larger scleral contact lenses, which are wide enough to vault over the cone-shaped cornea and rest on the white of the eye.

“Patients may be treated with a medical therapy called corneal cross-linking, which hopefully stops the progression of keratoconus,” McLaughlin told Healthline.

Once Curry figured out corrective lenses, he said, “I could actually see clearly and stop squinting, stop having those weird little headaches that would come, that I didn’t associate with my vision.”

While it took some trial and error to get his lens prescription correct and feel stable enough to play basketball, once he did, he couldn’t believe the difference in his vision.

“[I] was the most annoying person probably that first two weeks, ‘cause any time I walked in the room, either I’d be awkwardly staring at you, ‘cause I can see you clearly, or making a comment [like], ‘I can see all the dust in the air, like, this is incredible,” Curry said.

On the court, he could see his teammates better.

“[It] was just a whole new world, for sure,” he said. “[I] realized for the longest [time] I was just adapting and adjusting to what I saw.”

Curry continues to have eye screenings twice a year to monitor the condition.

Keratoconus symptoms and why early diagnosis matters

KC is most often diagnosed during puberty and the teenage years and progresses during a person’s twenties. Delayed diagnosis may increase the risk of irreversible vision loss.

McLaughlin said KC is easily diagnosed at an eye doctor’s appointment with an instrument called a keratometer. However, additional testing may be required.

Your eye doctor may test for it if they notice sudden or severe changes in your astigmatism or nearsightedness, if your vision hasn’t improved with standard eyeglasses, or if you have an irregular corneal reflection.

Although the condition is not inherited in most cases, it can occur in some families.

“Family history of every patient is important, including any family history of keratoconus,” said McLaughlin.

To spread awareness about keratoconus and the importance of annual eye exams, Curry partnered with Glaukos for its Could it be KC? campaign.

Because KC disproportionately affects Black and Hispanic populations, he especially hopes to reach these communities.

“[The] reach that I have and the authentic story of what this has kind of been like for me is the reason I’m doing it,” Curry said. “The work that me and my wife are doing in our foundation in Oakland — the kids that we’re serving — this is a message that they need to hear.”

To connect with more young people, he took part in an educational community event at Madison Park Primary in Oakland on Wednesday, September 23, 2026. At the event, he introduced a new picture book, “Eye on the Ball,” inspired by Curry’s own experience living with KC, and designed to make conversations about eye health and KC more approachable.

“You want to give a visual, especially for kids, to kind of destigmatize what it means to go to a doctor, ask uncomfortable questions, be in that environment where it can be overwhelming at times for sure,” said Curry.

He empathizes with children and people of all ages who are living with undiagnosed KC.

“I can only imagine [because] the way that I saw the court, sometimes struggling to see the screen to watch film, like, little things that you just adapt to and figure out, for younger kids, high school kids, college, [those] in the workforce, you realize…that’s energy exerted,” Curry said. “It’s a hurdle that shouldn’t necessarily be a thing that you have to deal with, but it’s only going to get corrected if you have the awareness to go get it checked.”

He encourages people to see an eye doctor if something doesn’t feel right.

“[There] were plenty of moments for me in that respect, where… things are designed a certain way to be able to see them, and if you can’t, you have to make adjustments to do it, or you have to ask somebody, ‘You know, what does that say up there?’”

Curry hopes that sharing his story encourages more people to schedule eye doctor visits and that more people with KC get the care they need.

“[Hopefully their] outcome is similar to mine, as well, where it’s just a matter of getting checked and getting the right answers for what your situation is,” he said.

#4867. Ethereum fixes Glamsterdam bug that could have sent staking deposits to builders

Ethereum developers have patched a flaw in the upcoming Glamsterdam upgrade that could have let someone walk off with ETH that staking services meant for new validators.

The fix, which changed the rules for handling validator deposits, was merged Monday, a week after developers turned it down.

Glamsterdam splits up two jobs that are bundled together today. Builders bundle transactions into blocks, and validators, who stake ETH to help secure the network, pick and confirm them. Both can deposit ETH, and their money is supposed to be tracked separately.

The problem appeared during the upgrade itself, when Ethereum would process deposits that were still waiting to be credited.

Researcher Avsetsin identified a scenario in which an operator first registers as a builder using a particular public key, the identifier tied to a validator or builder. If a staking service then deposits ETH to start a validator under that key, the draft rules would credit both deposits to the builder.

“The operator can withdraw the protocol’s funds,” Avsetsin wrote. No validator gets created, so the staking service can’t produce the proof it would need to recover its money.

Avsetsin, listed as a member of Lido’s GitHub organization, examined how Lido, Origin, and Rocket Pool could be affected in what the researcher called “quick AI research.” The findings depended on how each protocol handles deposits.

“These are conditional code findings, not confirmed live exploits,” avsetsin wrote, adding that existing checks rejecting an earlier builder deposit could stop the attack. No losses have been reported.

Developers closed the proposed fix on Oct. 19. “There was not enough support from clients to make this change at this time,” wrote Justin Traglia, who maintains Ethereum’s consensus specifications. Clients are the separate software programs validators run to take part in the network.

The fix was reopened after further review, avsetsin added tests on Oct. 23, and Traglia merged it Monday.

Under the new rules, pending deposits are sorted by their withdrawal credentials, the setting that says where a deposit’s money can eventually go. Validator deposits stay with validators even if a builder is using the same key.

The change is now part of Ethereum’s consensus specifications, and client teams still have to build it into their software. Glamsterdam has already run on the Sepolia test network, but its Oct. 26 launch on Hoodi, a second test network, was postponed.

Glamsterdam needs a new Hoodi date before a mainnet launch can be scheduled.

#4866. Monkeys also avoid germs after viral outbreaks

Published Oct 20, 2026 7:05 PM EDT

When a new disease emerges, things can get a little weird. We start to clean groceries with antibacterial wipes, the sound of a sneeze can trigger our fight-or-flight response, and we generally become more cautious. Humans may not be the only primate species that change their behavior in the face of disease.

According to a study published today in the journal Royal Society Open Science, Japanese macaques (Macaca fuscata) that lived through a deadly outbreak in 2020 became noticeably more cautious about food that appeared contaminated, even though this disease was not spread by food or feces. This indicates that purposefully shifting behavior to avoid a pathogen can be shaped by our appearance and that disgust may have deep evolutionary roots. It also sheds light on the behavioral immune system—a collection of behaviors that help animals avoid an infection before the biological immune systems respond.

“We can show now, in non-human primates, that this is the same for them,” Federico Rossano, a study co-author and cognitive psychologist at the University of California San Diego, said in a statement. “Even with no knowledge of how disease is transmitted, being exposed to others getting sick and dying changes the likelihood of engaging in behaviors that potentially could lead to some form of sickness.”

In 2020, the staff at a primate sanctuary in southern Texas confirmed the presence of simian retrovirus Type II. This disease can cause AIDS-like immunodeficiency and is primarily transmitted through bites and scratches. It caused severe illness and death in some of the monkeys. The sanctuary staff separated the groups and implemented testing and quarantine measures in response to the outbreak. Since the groups live in separate enclosures, scientists then had the chance to compare animals with similar backgrounds that had experienced very different social and disease environments.

In total, three groups of monkeys experienced the outbreak while one had not. The team tested

55 macaques to see how they responded to a choice between food that was put on a clean wooden surface and food placed on a surface covered with real macaque feces. The food with the feces was sealed under a layer of clear epoxy, so that the monkeys could see the food and poop without risking infection.

In the group that had been exposed to the outbreak, some of the individuals had been infected while others had lived alongside sick monkeys without getting sick. This allowed the researchers to question if experiencing an outbreak without getting infected could change the macaque’s behavior. To test that further, the clean surface was replaced with a brightly colored kitchen sponge in some of the scenarios to see if the animals were just avoiding unfamiliar objects.

Previous studies found that macaques aren’t necessarily deterred by contamination—as long as the payoff is tempting. When presented with a favorite treat (such as a peanut) wild Japanese macaques would still eat food from a surface covered in feces, but will avoid it for food like grains of wheat.

The team found that macaques from groups that went through the outbreak were far less likely to eat the food from the contaminated surface. They ate from the fake contaminated surface in only 47 percent of trials. The monkeys that did not experience the outbreak ate from the potentially contaminated surface in 79 percent of trials.

The monkeys from outbreak-exposed groups also behaved similarly whether or not they themselves had been infected. According to the team, this suggests the behavioral change was associated with their experiencing living through the outbreak, instead of actually getting sick

“Groups have histories, and those histories show up in behavior,” added study co-author and UC San Diego cognitive psychologist Stephan Kaufhold. “Living through the disease outbreak appears to have made those monkey groups more cautious around food that looks contaminated.”

The virus was spread through blood or saliva from bites and scratches, and not through contaminated food. Interestingly, the macaques who saw their fellow primates get sick, were more reluctant to eat food placed on a surface that visually suggested contamination in some of the tests.

In addition to shedding light on how illness affects primate behavior, it also offers insight into the evolution of disgust. The team did not measure disgust directly, but found that monkey’s avoidance of potential sources of infection can change with their experience, similar to what humans do.

Human disgust is believed to play an important role in protecting us from pathogens. We recoil from the smell and sight of poop, spoiled food, and other potential sources of infection. Our sensitivity to those cues can also change depending on context.

Since primates are capable of social learning, the team believes that heightened pathogen avoidance could be learned from other members in the group and persist over time. Future studies could see if newly introduced animals adopt the behavior of a group that has already experienced an outbreak.

Elon Musk issued a similar warning on Sunday, saying debt funding is drying up for AI neoclouds, firms whose revenue depends almost entirely on data centers.

That matters for crypto. AI has been one of the most potent narratives driving risk-taking across financial markets, bitcoin included. Bitcoin and Nvidia both bottomed within weeks of ChatGPT’s debut in late 2022 and have rallied significantly since. Instability in AI could therefore spill over into crypto.

These warnings come as markets grow uneasy over whether AI’s promise will show up on balance sheets. The tech-heavy Nasdaq has slid 6.7% to 29,263 from 31,360 since Oct. 6, with AI-linked firms such as Nvidia and Oracle falling more than the index.

The stress is showing up in credit markets too. The cost of insuring against defaults on the debt of several AI-linked firms has hit record levels. This insurance is called a credit default swap (CDS). The buyer pays a yearly premium, and if the borrower goes bankrupt or fails to pay, the buyer gets a large payout. The premium, also known as the spread, is quoted in basis points. At 100 basis points, insuring $10 million of debt costs $100,000 a year. A wider spread means investors want more compensation to take on the credit risk.

The scramble for CDS shows in volumes too. “Pricing at these levels implies near or above a 50% probability of default over five years, up from below 30% a year ago,” Biemann wrote.

Biemann pointed to Lehman Brothers as an example of CDS working as an early warning. Lehman’s CDS averaged less than 100 basis points in the third quarter of 2007 and topped 1,400 by mid-September 2008, when it filed for bankruptcy and rocked global markets.

Biemann said investors can view CDS today “as warning lights rather than countdown clocks.”

In other words, AI firms aren’t necessarily headed the Lehman way. Besides, activity in CDS tied to the large AI leaders looks more like hedging than positioning for a crisis.

Large AI leaders with strong cash flow pay 50 to 120 basis points for five-year protection, Biemann said. He said these levels “still look more like the price of portfolio insurance than a prediction of financial distress.”

Protection on the broad investment grade market remains comparatively contained. “The concern is confined to business models that require debt markets to remain open while spending stays high and cash flow remains limited,” he said.

The sympathetic nervous system is part of the body’s involuntary nervous system that triggers your “fight or flight” response in stressful or dangerous situations.

Published Oct 21, 2026 1:13 PM EDT

Already looking for children’s toys for the holidays? Are you also a big Halloween fan? Well, do we have a  gift for you! Allow us to introduce the present no child could ever love (unless maybe they were possessed by a demon or a big fan of The Nightmare Before Christmas): The Creeping Baby Doll!

This nightmarish 1871 creation is everything you don’t want in a children’s toy. It’s noisy. It moves—seemingly propelled by painted plaster baby arms and legs. And it’s way creepy.

The mechanical baby doll has two legs, two arms, and a head all made of painted plaster (which is undoubtedly a bit chipped after more than 150 years). These plaster bits are really the only thing that actually look somewhat like a baby. The rest of the doll’s body is made up of brass clockwork. Some sort of dress or outfit would’ve likely covered its brass body back in the day.

Both the arms and legs are attached to gears that make the plaster limbs move so it looks like the baby doll is crawling (hence, the name—though we would argue there’s perhaps a reason they went with “creeping” instead of “crawling”). The entirety of the toy moves using two toothed wheels hidden under the baby’s body. A flat piece of wood covers the baby’s back to hide the mechanical instruments that make up the baby’s insides.

New York City-based inventor George Pemberton Clarke developed the horrific toy as an improvement to his colleague’s own creeping baby doll. That’s right, there were two! His associate, inventor Robert J. Clay, had filed the patent for his own creeping baby doll on March 14, 1871. Clarke later filed his patent on August 29th of that same year. They really thought this toy would be a hit apparently.

To be fair, there’s a long history of inventors trying to mimic human movement in machines. As far back as ancient Greece, there are stories of human mechanical models dumbfounding awestruck audiences. In the ninth century, Persian alchemist Jabir ibn Hayyan created moving statues. In the 10th century, Chinese engineer Yan Shi (nicknamed the Artificer) created life-size, human-like, moving figurines to the delight of King Mu of Zhou.

Mechanical automata reached their heyday during the Enlightenment and into the Victorian age. Automated sailing ships decorated tables, mechanical birds sang from gilded boxes, and silver swans floated down peaceful streams.

But even when you take into account the long history of automata, it is hard to look at Clarke’s Creeping Baby Doll and not think it’s a horror movie prop. In fact, it kind of was.

According to a Boing Boing, the antique toy actually inspired a very obscure 1972 horror film called Miriam: The Wind-Up Killer Doll. The film follows a group of friends who find a vintage crawling baby doll at a garage sale. But, in true horror movie fashion, an evil force possesses the baby doll, and the demonic doll starts killing off the friends one by one. (We didn’t say it was an original scary movie.)

That said, maybe we don’t actually recommend getting your favorite child Clarke’s Creeping Baby Doll. That decision may come back to haunt you.

#4832. Bitcoin Wallet Maker Keystone Pulls Devices as Stolen Key Opens Door to Fakes

Bitcoin Wallet Maker Keystone Pulls Devices as Stolen Key Opens Door to Fakes

Keystone, a maker of Bitcoin hardware wallets that keep the secret codes controlling your crypto offline, said Monday it will destroy 18,835 unsold devices after attackers obtained the key that proves its hardware is genuine. The company has also suspended shipping of all unshipped orders from its website.

For buyers, the risk is a counterfeit wallet that looks brand new and passes Keystone’s own verification check, because the attacker obtained the key that the check relies on. A criminal could then sell such a device to someone who loads real savings onto it.

“If it is exposed, an attacker could use it to produce counterfeit devices that pass authentication checks,” Keystone said in its statement. “These devices could then be sold or shipped to customers, who might unknowingly use them as genuine products, potentially resulting in asset theft.”

Keystone calls it a WebAuth key, a secret code written into each device at the factory so a page on the company’s website can confirm the hardware is real. It cannot be erased or rewritten, and the 18,835 units in inventory that carry it are being destroyed rather than sold.

Keystone first said Saturday that a third party had obtained customer shipping information. Monday’s statement added the stolen key and a count of 25,964 affected buyer records, but it does not say who is responsible or when the attacker first got in.

The incident affected records from Keystone’s Shopify store, the e-commerce platform that handled its online sales. Keystone says 51,083 buyer records were involved, but 25,119 had already had their personal information erased, leaving 25,964 from 2024 to 2026 that still held names, contact details, shipping addresses and order information. Purchases through Amazon and other channels were not affected.

Some of that data should already have been deleted. Keystone’s policy was to erase buyer records after 180 days, and the automated bulk-deletion tool it built for the job did not work reliably.

“Some erasure tasks failed, and we did not detect and address these failures in time, resulting in certain personal information being retained beyond the permitted period,” Keystone said. “We failed to fulfill our commitment to erase customer data on time, and we accept responsibility for this failure.”

Customer lists are a recurring weak point for wallet makers. Ledger’s 2020 leak eventually reached about 292,000 customers, and those who received phishing emails afterward worried they could become targets of home invasions.

#4827. WWII fighter plane crash found in Hungary after nearly 80 years

Published Oct 21, 2026 3:05 PM EDT

After nearly 82 years, historians in Hungary have finally located a missing Soviet aircraft downed by a German ace pilot during World War II. After a monthslong investigation that involved painstaking archival dives and eyewitness interviews, a team led by Hungarian Aircraft Wreck Research Association co-founder István Kocsis pinpointed the Yakovlev Yak-1 fighter plane. The aircraft was buried over 17 feet underground roughly 50 miles southeast of Budapest. Despite the poor condition of many of the plane’s components, the recoverable remnants still included portions of the aircraft’s fabric covering and even some of the pilot’s personal items.

“Discoveries of this aircraft type on such a significant scale are extremely rare,” a member of the Hungarian Defense Forces said during a recent press conference announcing the find.

The crash dates back to November 1944, after Soviet Guards Lieutenant Alexander Semyonovich Andrianov encountered German Lieutenant Peter Düttmann above the town of Törtel. Unfortunately for Andrianov, Düttmann was already infamous for his dogfighting abilities, and the Nazi pilot eventually shot down his Soviet foe. Although Andrianov successfully ejected with his parachute, he was later taken prisoner of war. Although Andrianov’s fate is unclear, historians estimate roughly half of all Soviet servicemen taken as POWs died while in German hands.

The Yak-1 fared even worse. Constructed from composite materials like steel tubing, wood, and even fabric, the crash destroyed the aircraft and immediately scattered the wreckage. With no one to claim it, the plane slowly sank into the region’s particularly loose soil over the next eight decades.

Much of the plane remained intact underground, but military archaeologists chose to rely on a recovery strategy called block lifting. Instead of excavating at the site, heavy machinery portioned out hefty chunks of earth that the researchers then carefully cleared one layer at a time. This layer-by-layer method allowed them to identify and preserve numerous smaller pieces of wreckage, including the engine serial number and construction identification that matched Andrianov’s Yak-1. The team also exhumed some of Andrianov’s personal belongings, including a book signed by the pilot.

While much of the wreck was heavily deformed from the crash’s impact, restoration experts are confident they can reconstruct many of the Yak-1’s parts. Once preserved and documented, these sections of the plane will go on public display at Budapest’s Military History Museum while the rest remain safely archived.

#4825. Wall Squats, Planks Once a Week May Help Lower Your Blood Pressure

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Isometric exercises, even if done only occasionally, could help improve blood pressure, according to a new study.

Researchers report that study participants who did these types of exercises once or twice a week saw reductions in blood pressure over a short period.

They added that participants who engaged in isometric exercises three times a week maintained improvements in blood pressure over a longer period.

The researchers said that previous studies had indicated that isometric exercise training reduces blood pressure more than other forms of exercise and even some drug treatments.

However, they said previous research had not established how often isometric exercise training needs to be performed to maintain these health benefits.

Isometric exercises involve a person holding a pose for a few minutes at a time. Examples include wall squats and floor planks.

The researchers said their study provides important information for people who don’t have time to exercise daily.

The researchers wrote that the study “offers evidence that lower frequency [isometric exercise training] can help maintain short-term [blood pressure] reductions, providing a pragmatic option for individuals with limited time or adherence challenges.”

They published their findings on September 29 in BMJ Open Sport and Exercise Medicine.

Experts not involved in the research said it provides helpful guidance for people who want to improve their blood pressure.

“The main takeaway is that you don’t need to exercise every day — or even several times a week — to experience significant blood pressure benefits from isometric exercise,” said Michael Fredericson, MD, a professor of orthopaedic surgery at Stanford Health Care.

“We already knew that performing isometric training for even just a few weeks can lower blood pressure,” added Amanda Paluch, PhD, an associate professor of kinesiology at the University of Massachusetts Amherst and chair of the American Heart Association’s Physical Activity Committee. “This study adds to the research by looking at how much training is needed to maintain those gains.”

“It is encouraging to see measurable improvements in blood pressure with a relatively brief and infrequent exercise routine,” said Jennifer Wong, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of Non-Invasive Cardiology at MemorialCare Heart and Vascular Institute at Orange Coast Medical Center in California. “These findings suggest that even small, consistent efforts can make a meaningful difference.”

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that nearly half of adults in the United States have high blood pressure. That’s about 120 million people.

The condition, also known as hypertension, increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.

High blood pressure occurs when the force of blood pushing through blood vessels is consistently too high.

Blood pressure readings consist of two numbers. One is the systolic pressure (top number), which measures pressure when your heart beats. The second is diastolic pressure (the bottom number), which measures the pressure between beats.

Treatment usually involves medications such as beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors, as well as lifestyle factors such as a healthy diet and exercise.

In their study, the researchers aimed to determine how much isometric exercise was needed to lower and maintain blood pressure.

The researchers recruited 100 adults with an average age of 37. Subjects included male and female participants with normal-to-high-normal systolic blood pressure (120–140 mm Hg) who engaged in less than 150 minutes per week of moderate physical activity.

The participants also had no injuries or illnesses, were not taking and had not previously taken blood pressure medication, were not smokers, and did not consume more than 14 units of alcohol per week.

The participants were divided into five groups. One group served as a control and did no isometric exercise, while the other four groups followed the same four-week home-based lower-body isometric training program.

The regimen consisted of four sessions of isometric wall squat exercise, separated by two minutes of seated rest, performed three times a week.

During the next four weeks, the participants were split into four training schedules.

One group continued to exercise three times a week. A second group exercised twice a week, while a third group exercised once a week. The remaining group stopped the exercises entirely.

Blood pressure, heart rate, and other cardiac measurements were taken at the start of the study and after four weeks and eight weeks.

The researchers reported that the four groups who completed the initial four-week exercise routine showed significant reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to the control group.

Over the next four weeks, the researchers found no statistically significant difference in diastolic blood pressure among the groups.

However, the researchers reported that participants who continued isometric exercise training three times a week achieved the greatest reduction in systolic blood pressure compared with baseline. That group also maintained improvements in other cardiac measures.

The group that had stopped doing isometric exercises after the initial four-week period showed a reversal of their blood pressure improvements.

The researchers wrote that the study “demonstrates that while lower frequency [isometric exercise training] may be sufficient to maintain [blood pressure] reductions over the short term, higher frequencies are required to preserve these benefits and the associated vascular and cardiac autonomic adaptations.”

The researchers noted that the eight-week study period was limited and that further research is needed. They also acknowledged that the participants were from a relatively small cross-section of people.

There are several ways isometric exercises may help lower blood pressure.

“Repeat isometric exercises (contracting a muscle and holding a position) seem to change how the blood vessels regulate pressure between workouts,” said Kevin Shah, MD, a cardiologist and program director of Heart Failure Outreach at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California.

“Blood pressure comes down to how hard the heart pumps and how much resistance the blood meets in the vessels,” added Paluch.

“Research suggests isometric training works mainly on that second part. It helps your blood vessels relax and open up, so blood flows through more easily.”

“Isometric exercise seems to promote cardiovascular adaptations that can lower blood pressure,” explained Fredericson. “Over time, this training can enhance the cardiovascular system’s ability to regulate blood pressure.”

Experts interviewed by Healthline say that the wall squats used in the study are one of the most beneficial isometric exercises.

They also recommend static leg extensions, handgrip exercises, planks, and exercises that involve sustained muscle contraction.

“Start slowly, with manageable goals,” Wong said. “Even one or two short sessions per week can be a starting point,” she said. “Begin with brief holds, allow adequate rest between sets and sessions, and gradually build up as strength and comfort improve. The goal is to establish a routine one can maintain consistently.”

Paluch recommended starting small and keeping it simple. She says 10 to 20 minutes per session is sufficient.

She suggested four 2-minute holds of a wall sit or handgrip squeeze with 2 minutes of rest between each hold. Those can be done two to three days a week.

“The message is that consistency matters,” she said. “If you’re able to maintain three times a week, that appears to be the best way to maintain the gain, but it’s not an all-or-nothing situation.”

“You don’t have to maintain the initial pace forever to get something out of the training, especially when life gets in the way and adherence or time becomes a challenge,” Paluch added. “Even getting in one day a week is better than nothing.”

For those just getting started with exercise, Shah recommended starting with a short walk before adding isometric exercise.

“A 10-minute walk on most days is a manageable first step,” Shah said. “Gradually increase the length of walks and then add strength training. Consistency matters more than an exhaustive routine that is not sustainable. Remember: Progress and not perfection.”

Fredericson advised starting slowly and building from there.

“For someone completely inactive, even 5 to 10 minutes of movement per day is a meaningful starting point,” he said. “Walking is an excellent option because it requires little to no equipment or preparation. From there, they can incorporate strength training two or three days a week.”
