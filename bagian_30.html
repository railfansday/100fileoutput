#3118. 4 key plays from the Dallas win over the Eagles

Boy, does it feel good to be back in the win column again. After falling to 2-4, the Dallas Cowboys were able to pull away late to secure the 38-21 win over the Philadelphia Eagles.

Dallas set the tone on the ground and ran the ball down the Eagles' throats in a physical performance more akin to what we should expect from an offensive line of their caliber. The defense still needs to perform better overall, but there were signs of progress, albeit against a depleted opponent offensively who featured a less-than-full-strength Saquon Barkley.

Dak Prescott threw multiple scores, and maybe all isn't lost this season as some might have feared. The Cowboys are one game back in the win column of the division-leading New York Giants in the NFC East. The team might be buying their chances of winning the division title and is ready to build the roster further versus dismantling it.

So what were the pivotal moments in Monday night's big win? Here are the four key plays that earned the Cowboys the victory.

The Cowboys have come under a lot of fire lately, primarily for getting bullied on both sides of the ball in the trenches. You got the sense that if the Eagles felt like they could push Dallas around in front of their home crowd, it would ignite their belief that they could beat up on the Cowboys like many teams have done this season.

Instead, the Cowboys turned the tables on the Eagles and their harshest critics. Javonte Williams found his footing early with a couple of big gains during the team's first drive. On one play, Williams ran off to the left side of the offensive line and ran through a gaping hole for 15 yards that set up the Cowboys' first points of the game.

Upon looking at the replay, the play was blocked so well that Tyler Smith had no one to hit. From early on, the offensive line established themselves with that run and created big lanes all night for Williams, who set a career-high in rushing yards.

This run was indicative of the team needing to play angry, and it certainly showed how they mauled the Eagles interior defensive line. If you needed any further indication of how dominant the offensive line was, the graphic above is pretty telling. That first series and that first big run let the Eagles know they were going to be in for a long night.

Without a key time out called, this play likely doesn't happen. On 4th and 1 from the Philadelphia 48-yard line, the Cowboys lined up in an attempt to draw the Eagles offsides with a hard count, and it appeared that the Cowboys had no intention of going for it on fourth down.

However, after Nick Sirianni called a timeout, it gave Brian Schottenheimer more time to reassess his decision, opting to trust his offense. Javonte Williams easily picked up the fourth down, and the team capitalized thanks to George Pickens.

Although you'd prefer not to be in a shootout against the Eagles' backup quarterback, it definitely helps having the caliber of weapons the Cowboys have at their disposal. George Pickens has been under the microscope this week after some not-so-veiled comments by his head coach regarding his effort in the running game.

Let's call it a call to action, because Pickens made one of his best plays of the season. The throw from Prescott is excellent, but the effort by Pickens to catch the ball cleanly off first contact with his hands is top-notch. To have the wherewithal to get both of his feet in bounds to complete the process of the catch was elite.

That touchdown was the result of Schottenheimer making the key decision to go for it on fourth down.

After the defense was strung out on the field for yet another extensive drive that has become all too familiar this season, the Cowboys had to answer with points. Having surrendered a nine-minute drive, you felt that familiar dread start to sink in, and started to think this would be the moment it would begin to slip away.

Except the Eagles threw the Cowboys a gift when they committed one of their five penalties on the night. While working back to the outside on a route, Riq Woolen grabbed CeeDee Lamb, drawing the flag for defensive holding.

It was a pivotal sequence because Lamb dropped the pass thrown to him on this play. With the penalty, the Cowboys avoided giving the ball back to Philadelphia and proceed to end the drive with Lamb's second score of the night and a two-point conversion to pull ahead 28-21.

DeMarvion Overshown has teased the Cowboys with his talent for the last few seasons. Injuries have kept him from him fully reaching his potential, and prevented him from being a full contributor to the defense. Overshown hadn't played since the season opener when he was injured and sidelined with a hamstring issue.

His interception of Tanner McKee was a long time coming. Overshown did a great job of reading the eyes of the quarterback to break on the ball, undercutting the out route and making the timely interception. If the Eagles had an outside chance, that was the moment that removed all doubt.

The defense has a lot more work to do, but they did show flashes in the second half. They got pressure on the passer and broke up passes in the secondary. Overshown's interception stopped the Eagles' last best chance, and sent Dallas on their way to the win.

The 122nd World Series begins Friday night at American Family Field in Milwaukee. The National League-best Milwaukee Brewers will host the American League-best Tampa Bay Rays, and one way or the other, baseball will have a first-time champion this season. Neither the Brewers (established 1970) nor Rays (established 1998) have ever won a World Series.

"The 2020 World Series was kind of a blur. The whole postseason was a blur. I'm a heck of a lot happier we're in this situation now coming out on the other side of that," Rays manager Kevin Cash said on Thursday. "Those games, they were intense, there was a lot of anxiety in them, but I felt that same intensity and anxiety in the ALCS just two or three days ago. Excited to go and watch our guys do their thing in the next week to ten days."

Historically, the winner of Game 1 of a best-of-seven has gone on to win the series 65% of the time. It's 64% in the World Series, so basically identical. Just last year, though, the Los Angeles Dodgers lost Game 1 of the World Series at home, then rallied to beat the Toronto Blue Jays in seven games. Winning Game 1 certainly gives you a leg up, but it does not mean the series is over.

Here now are three key questions heading into Game 1 on Friday night.

Jacob Misiorowski, the likely NL Cy Young winner, has not been himself this postseason. He failed to complete five innings in either of his two starts, not necessarily because of ineffectiveness, but because of high pitch counts. Misiorowski threw 88 pitches en route to allowing two runs in four innings in Game 1 of the NLDS. In Game 1 of the NLCS, it was one run on 94 pitches in 4 ⅓ innings.

Misiorowski's high-octane fastball has played down this postseason. The velocity is plenty good. He averaged -- averaged -- 101.0 mph with his heater in the NLDS and NLCS. Hitters are not swinging and missing nearly as much as they did during the regular season, though, and the resulting foul balls have run up his pitch count.

The Rays had the lowest strikeout rate (18.8% of plate appearances) and lowest whiff rate (20.7% of swings) during the regular season, by a lot too. The Blue Jays were a distant second with a 22.2% whiff rate. Misiorowski has had trouble missing bats with his heater this postseason and he'll now face the best contact-hitting offense in the sport. That could be a problem.

"He hasn't been as crisp," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said about Misiorowski on Thursday. "I don't think people are up there thinking it's going to be 104, and I think they're elated if it's anything less than that. They're obviously looking at an area. They're trying to exploit everything. He's exposed, like all these guys get at some point. He's exposed. They're going to attack a certain way, and we're going to try to counter that attack the way we counter it."

The Brewers are expected to carry five lefties in their World Series bullpen: Aaron Ashby, Shane Drohan, DL Hall, Kyle Harrison, and JoJo Romero. Ashby, Hall, and Romero are the trusted late-inning guys. Drohan started a bullpen game in the NLCS. Harrison is more of a break-glass-in-case-of-emergency option. He'll get the ball deep in extra innings, something like that.

The Rays have shown little power against lefties this year, which is hard to believe with right-handed thumpers Junior Caminero and Yandy Díaz anchoring the lineup. Ryan Vilade, Tampa's go-to righty platoon bat, will undoubtedly get several key pinch-hit at-bats in the World Series, and perhaps as soon as Game 1. The Rays must solve Milwaukee's lefties to win the title.

The Brewers swept the Dodgers in the NLCS and have not played since last Thursday. They've played only eight games in the last 25 days. Rest is good. In the three-wild-card era, teams that have five or more days off going into a postseason series are 13-10 in Game 1. Teams have so many tools available to them (Trajekt hitting machines, etc.) that it is easier to navigate that downtime than it was even five years ago. Murphy's right and you can't recreate real, but you can come pretty close. The layoff is definitely a subplot going into Game 1.

#3024. 81-1: Week One Overreactions

Last week was some of the best sleep I had gotten in a long time. Climbing into bed with the biggest, dumbest grin on my face, giddily scrolling through my phone on Canis, Reddit, and YouTube, seeing what everyone had to say about just how awesome the Minnesota Timberwolves are. Seriously, it was great.

I would whisper myself to sleep, repeating the mantra "82 and 0, 82 and 0…," then dream of show-stopping sequences like Ant juggling the ball through traffic and rocking the rim, Melo doing the jump rope shuffle into a one-footed rainbow three, Beringer blocks, Kuminga lobs, TJ step-back threes, and secret handshakes that will be studied by kinesics experts and nonverbal communication researchers for decades.

There were also dreams of glory, of hoisted hardware, of an "anything is possible" redux with the legend himself, KG, sitting courtside, tears of joy glistening in his eyes. One can dream, but most of these dreams actually did happen, so why not that one as well?

Yes, I know the Wolves will officially not be going 82-0 this year, and that's too bad. I had high hopes we could get it done this year. I've reached out to the NBA about reviewing the tape and possibly getting that loss to Cleveland reversed. They haven't responded, but that's on them.

Barring a reversal, just because we're not going 82-0 doesn't mean we can't go 81-1, and putting the Cavs debacle aside there is still so much to be giddy about when it comes to this new look Wolves team. So let's overreact, shall we? No single loss is going to stop us from listening to our hearts on repeat.

Is this year's Wolves squad reminding anyone else of the 95-96 Chicago Bulls? You don't even have to squint to see that Anthony Edwards is obviously MJ, while Scottie Pippen has undergone some sort of bifurcation process and split off into both LaMelo Ball and Jaden McDaniels. Then you've got defensive stalwart and human highlight reel Jonathan Kuminga, the Stifle Tower Rudy Gobert, Ayo Dosunmu, TJ Shannon, the French Prince Joan Beringer – hold up…

Are the Wolves actually better than the 95-96 Bulls? How is that even possible?

With a small enough sample size, anything is possible.

Also worth noting, in that historic Bulls season, they played their third game at home against the Toronto Raptors, and their next game on the road against…you guessed it…the Cleveland Cavaliers. Of course, unlike us, they beat Cleveland, but they weren't on the second night of a back-to-back. Just something to consider when we're comparing our respective places in NBA history.

That Bulls team also still holds the best net rating in NBA history at +13.4. While I do think that's beatable, I firmly believe the Wolves have a real shot at breaking the NBA record for point differential, previously held by the 2024-25 Oklahoma City Thunder at +12.9 PPG.

Right now, even with the loss to the Cavs, the Wolves are second in the league in point differential at a still absurd +17 PPG, behind only Micah Nori's Portland Trail Blazers. We love Micah, but there's no way his team keeps this up. The Wolves, on the other hand, will, because of the power of friendship and Rudy's goatee that Chris Finch strokes for good luck when no one is looking.

If the Wolves continue to not just beat the teams they should beat, but consistently crush them, a double-digit differential is sustainable.

And it wouldn't hurt to embarrass some of our fellow West contenders while we're at it.

By the way, last year's Melo-led Hornets team had a double-digit point differential from January 3rd onward, and this team is leaps and bounds better than that fun but flawed Hornets squad. To add to that, as those mid-season buzzsaw buzzers were just starting to take flight, one game that especially turned heads was a 27-point drubbing of the Thunder in OKC.

I've hitched my wagon to the Ant MVP train, and so far so good. With LaMelo allowing him to slide into his more natural shooting guard position, this fully realized version of Ant has never had this much space to work with. So far, after four games, he's putting up 33.3 points per game on 72.1% true shooting. Ant has increased both his scoring and true shooting averages every year, which means…cue Disco Stu…

If the Wolves go 81-1, it will be hard not to give him the nod. But if, for whatever reason, something goes horribly wrong and the team finds itself struggling to claw its way to 60 wins, I think those numbers will still suffice, and Ant will have himself his first real piece of NBA hardware.

It's as simple as this: Ayo is a stud, and the Wolves are going to be the best team to come out of the West. Historically, the studliest bench player on the best team wins Sixth Man of the Year. This isn't even an overreaction; it's just the most logical take.

It's basically Giannis and an assortment of chips, and I guess Bam but sometimes he is so invisible that calling him out at all, I feel, is one acknowledgement too many. And by the way, aren't you supposed to trade the bag of chips? That's how we got Ayo.

If I go to the gym and the Heat are playing, I will watch the game while on the treadmill and declare myself an honorary member of the 2026-27 Miami Heat.

With Ja finally gone, the Grizzlies are no longer just "fine in the West". They're on a roll, with Ty as the engine. He's always been a bucket getter, but for the first time in his career he's getting a chance to really let it fly, putting up nearly 20 shots a game (his previous high was 8.8 two seasons ago on the Cavs).

From logo threes to his signature floater, there's no reason to believe he won't keep his scoring output at least in the low-to-mid-20 point-per-game range. And if the Grizzlies keep winning, this race is all but over.

I will admit to this very much being confirmation bias.

The Houston Rockets have been the most impressive team in the West so far, besides the Wolves, of course. I wouldn't be surprised at all if those four teams are the ones wrestling for top seeding.

Obviously, the Wolves will be sitting comfortably in first, 20 or so games ahead of the next tier of teams when it's all said and done. 81-1 is undefeated. That made more sense in my head.
