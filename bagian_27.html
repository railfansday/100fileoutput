#3420. Did Starbucks Just Give Investors Nearly 2.9 Billion Reasons to Buy Its Stock?

Starbucks (SBUX +0.34%) is infusing its stock with a tiny shot of something good. Earlier this month, the unavoidable coffee giant declared its latest dividend raise, and the timing felt opportune -- the investor morale-boosting move came several weeks in advance of the company's fiscal fourth-quarter earnings release, scheduled for Wednesday after market close.

The hike wasn't particularly robust; nevertheless, it showed the company is concerned about how dividend investors perceive it. Let's dig into the latest payout improvement and see whether that, along with the company's recent performance and future potential, makes the stock a buy.

Starbucks announced that dividend raise on Oct. 7, stating that its board of directors approved a $0.01 increase in the quarterly common stock payout to $0.63 per share. This puts the annual amount at $2.52 per share, yielding just shy of 2.5%.

This keeps Starbucks' increasingly lengthy annual dividend raise streak alive, as the company has lifted the payout every year since initiating it in 2010.

And while a $0.01-per-share increase won't make income investors pounce on the stock, it'll enhance management's reputation for keeping shareholders happy. After all, with the move, Starbucks' annual dividend spend on its outstanding shares will reach nearly $2.9 billion.

Besides, Starbucks prefers to allocate capital for other purposes. In its dividend raise announcement, the company quoted CFO Cathy Smith as saying the raise "reflects our continued confidence and belief that disciplined execution of our Back to Starbucks strategy will drive sustainable long-term growth and value creation."

Despite the hopeful words, Starbucks saw a top-line slump in its fiscal third quarter. There's a big asterisk next to that fact, however, as the company divested a majority stake in its China business by forming a 60/40 licensed joint venture led by the domestic investment management firm Boyu Capital.

That means overall revenue from the sprawling Asian nation has declined, but what it earns today boosts the company's profit margins.

The China shift was a key reason why Starbucks posted that overall revenue decrease (of slightly over 1% year over year to $9.3 billion). A more revealing fundamental was global comparable store sales, which raced nearly 8% higher, crushing the 6.2% from a mere one quarter earlier.

Breaking that down further, North America comps were 8.1%, and international comps were 5.7%. With Starbucks no longer weighed down by operating the China cafes directly, that percentage surged -- it was more than double the second-quarter international figure of 2.6%.

Profitability leaped in the quarter; however, the headline net income number was skewed by that sale of the China stake. To a lesser extent, federal tariff refunds also added to the number, which, under generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP), surged 87% to almost $1.05 billion. Yet a more revealing line item, operating income, improved by 5% to $980 million.

Starbucks likes to attribute such improvements to the aforementioned Back to Starbucks -- the strategy captained by its star CEO, ex-Chipotle Mexican Grill chief Brian Niccol. He and his team hatched the initiative after he joined the company in 2024; it focuses on making the cafes more welcoming and tightening order fulfillment and inventory management, among other adjustments.

While I tend to take grand-sounding corporate initiatives with a grain of salt, I feel that there's some truth in the company's assertion.

I go to Starbucks cafes semi-regularly, and I've noticed more than a few of these changes; I do get my java quicker, it seems, and there's less "counter congestion" of food/beverage items at the pickup table thanks to changes in the ordering system.

So all this, plus the satisfying continuation of the company's streak of dividend raises, makes Starbucks a buy, right?

I wouldn't go that far. Yes, those improvements are tangible and feel as if they might be long-lasting, if not permanent, and the fundamentals are growing impressively of late. Yet if we look at the stock's valuations, they're high.

Forward P/E is over 32, making Starbucks' equity priced more like a growth stock, and I don't feel it's that dynamic a company, even with those recent (and solid) year-over-year improvements. I believe many investors are captivated by the turnaround story, but this is a company regaining its former strength and power, not one poised to vault ahead for years to come.

Yes, it's always pleasant when one of our companies raises a dividend. For me, though, Starbucks' latest hike isn't dramatic enough to buy the stock. I'd say the same for its recent upward lift in the fundamentals, as impressive as it has been.

#3419. Europe’s winter energy crunch may already be underway. Two U.S. stocks that may benefit

Oil flows through Hormuz are rising, more ships are safely getting through the Strait, and oil prices seem to be coming down — at least for now. Goldman Sachs wrote Wednesday that Persian Gulf exports have "recovered to their 2025 average after doubling in September." The firm expects Brent crude prices to "moderate" to $85 per barrel by year's end. That's good news on oil, and maybe good news for both the United States and China.

My take → I know the term "reopen the Strait of Hormuz" is popular, but I just can't bring myself to say it. Hormuz is an international waterway, and no one country can "close" it. Yes, Iran — or the Houthis in Yemen — can threaten ships in Hormuz or near the Red Sea, making it more dangerous to pass through. But no country has the power to "open" or "close" a public, international waterway. I get that I'm the outlier in saying that — and I wouldn't have it any other way.

The not-so-good news involves natural gas and Europe. Despite the improvement around Iran and oil, it could still be a long, expensive winter for our friends in Germany and much of Europe.

For five years, I've been highlighting a few of the continent's major energy challenges: how more and more U.K. families have had to choose between heating and eating — aka "energy poverty"; how rising electricity costs are hurting industrial companies; and how American liquefied natural gas has become a type of "Marshall Plan for energy," helping make sure the lights can stay on across Europe.

One big worry I highlighted in my reporting was that parts of Europe might face critical natural-gas shortfalls at times during the year. Thankfully, those worries proved wrong. The worst-case scenarios did not happen, largely due to the weather. Much of Europe has enjoyed a multi-year run of fairly benign weather: not too hot, not too cold. That enabled countries to preserve precious natural-gas storage.

This summer, Western Europe broke the 2003 record for its hottest summer on record. And while much of the continent does not use air conditioning at the same levels as the U.S. or some other countries, cooling does exist — and it's getting more and more common. So when temperatures climb, companies and consumers want to crank it. A/C is great, but it's a huge power drain. As power demand rose, natural-gas inventories were drawn down.

The Swiss Federal Office of Energy tracks natural-gas storage levels against the five-year average (for more, click here). The chart below highlights that the European Union's storage level is at its lowest point in those five years.

As you can see HERE, Germany is in slightly worse shape. France, not shown, is as well.

Despite lower storage levels and higher prices, European leaders remain seemingly unbothered. A group representing the coalition writes that, despite "lower storage levels compared to historical levels, the Commission and EU countries reconfirmed that EU gas supply remains stable."

One: Natural-gas storage remains "stable" only because of American exports. U.S. companies are saving Europe from an even more dire energy scenario. This is not hyperbole. The natural gas liquefied and loaded onto ships in Texas, Louisiana, and other ports is the difference between Europe having "stable" supplies and Europe struggling to meet demand. The sabotage of Qatari gas has made Europe even more reliant on LNG from the U.S. and — wait for it — Russia.

It's hard to believe, but Europe is still buying billions of dollars' worth of natural gas from Russia, albeit via ships and not the ill-fated Nord Stream pipeline. My producer Harriet Taylor and I stood incredulously and watched cargoes of Putin's gas steam into the Port of Rotterdam. Many believe because Nord Stream was blown up that Moscow isn't making any money off Europe's energy needs. Think again.

European leaders still argue they will cut out Russian LNG by January 1 and end all contracts to buy Russian gas by late next year. Color me skeptical. Given that people tend to get angry when they don't have electricity or heat, I would venture to say that Russia will be selling plenty of gas into Europe well beyond these feel-good headlines. Time will tell.

Two: It's not just about supply; it's also about price. On the ICE exchange, prices for October futures on European-traded natural gas are more than double those for February. While many power companies have already locked in purchase costs, anyone needing to buy gas on the open market is going to face the double shock of higher gas prices and higher shipping costs to get the LNG. For spot cargoes, Europe has to compete with buyers in Asia, with both sides ready to pay whatever they have to in order to keep the lights and heat on this winter.

If you're looking for a silver lining, it's that prices have come down in recent days, buoyed by hopes of a real, longer-lasting peace deal that allows for risk-free shipping through Hormuz. Peace in the Middle East would be the best outcome on many levels.

Beyond that, it would also be hugely helpful if Europe had another relatively mild winter, reducing demand for heat and natural gas. This would keep storage levels sufficient and allow power companies and countries to get a better head start on refilling natural-gas storage for next year.

Much of that LNG supply is going to come from the United States. We have more natural gas than we can export. If you're looking to invest around this theme, two obvious names are exporters Cheniere Energy (LNG) and Venture Global (VG). Less obvious: the biggest holders of U.S. LNG capacity are based in France and the U.K. They are TotalEnergies (TTE) and Shell (SHEL).

TotalEnergies CEO Patrick Pouyanné — arguably the most important CEO in global oil and gas — spoke with us this week about Europe's energy challenges, prices, and more in a Power Insider interview.

Speaking of TotalEnergies, it's time to buy that stock — and BP, too. So says the team at HSBC.

HSBC is upgrading both BP (BP) and TotalEnergies to buy. Higher natural gas prices play a part in the call. The firm raised its TTM European natural gas price forecast — what we showed you above — by 34% for the rest of this year and 40% for next year, while also slightly raising its 2028 outlook. Analyst Kim Fustier also sees "substantial upgrades" across both firms' oil and refining-margin outlooks, as well as huge cash generation and stock buybacks.

BP and TotalEnergies are not the only energy stocks the firm loves. It maintains its buy ratings on Shell (SHEL), Spain's Repsol (REP-DE), and Chevron (CVX). Fustier sees about 20% upside for her Buy-rated stocks.

I think it's a fascinating call, in part because Fustier acknowledges that the situation around Hormuz may not improve rapidly anytime soon. HSBC's base case is that the situation is prone to "repeated breakdowns" and "continued uncertainty," though it does see shipping volumes continuing to improve.

I agree that BP is a company to watch closely over the next 12-24 months.  The company made a hard pivot away from its core competency - oil and gas.  The company once known as British Petroleum even went so far as to make an ill-fated rebranding effort to be known as "beyond petroleum."   That didn't last long.  While the company remains a player in solar and battery technologies and has a joint venture in wind power, it is refocusing back on what originally made it one of the world's biggest companies.

The iPhone Duo is now in Apple stores, even if most buyers won’t get theirs until January. Here’s how to make the most of a hands-on demo.

The iPhone Duo went on sale Oct. 23, and anyone who didn’t preorder the device on Oct. 16 is now looking at a delivery date in late January. Apple stores have demo units on display, though, so you can spend time with Apple’s first foldable long before one could arrive at your door. A few minutes is enough to check what a spec sheet can’t tell you: how the hinge feels, how visible the crease is, how the phone sits in your hand folded and unfolded and how apps behave when you open it up. Here are six things worth testing on your next visit to an Apple store.

Here’s what should you be looking to do when you actually pick up the iPhone Duo. Because while the foldable phone might have a fun, unique design, you may run into things you’re unfamiliar with — or even dislike — when using it day to day. You’ll want to be confident that the device is a good fit for your needs before plunking down $2,000 or more for the folding phone.

We’re going to break down a series of things you should check out when you finally get to play with it at the Apple Store.

When the Duo was first revealed, Apple showed off a very intriguing animation when you unfold the it. Leave it to Apple to make a function so simple look so aesthetically pleasing.

It’ll likely be the first thing you notice when you first open the Duo up to see its inner display, and there’s a good chance you’ll continue to open and close the device to see the effect. At the end of the day, it’s a parlor trick — but a very good one, adding delight to ordinary use.

Foldables, well, fold, and they typically come with creases. The Duo has a minimal crease and a matte display, both of which are wins. That said, even the most noticeable creases tend to “disappear” when you’re viewing content — you just get used to them while watching TV shows or YouTube videos. The Duo’s minimal crease should be even easier to ignore.

And let’s not forget about the matte screen. Several foldables ship with glossy displays, but the iPhone Duo will have a less shiny screen, making it easier to view in bright environments without annoying reflections.

The outer passport-size display on the iPhone Duo may be a little awkward for watching videos because of how it crops content to fit the new aspect ratio. Still, it’s handy to prop the phone up in tent mode and watch shows that way. You also have a large inner display to work with, but because of its passport-style design, even that screen has a different aspect ratio than the widescreen format people are used to on regular phones, so there will likely be some letterboxing in videos.

Try watching something with the phone set up in both ways to see if it bothers you enough to dissuade you from buying the phone.

Again, it’s not an iPhone Duo issue but a foldable phone issue — the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold 8 is the same size and format. The aspect ratio of the display might crop the top or sides of videos, so it’s worth trying out if you expect to watch a lot of full-screen videos.

Like videos, apps on foldables can sometimes appear a little weird, and that could be the case with the iPhone Duo‘s aspect ratio. When you’re at the Apple Store, check out how your most-used apps look and feel on both the inner and outer displays, and make sure you rotate the inner screen to see how apps behave in this orientation, too — that wide screen could be great for watching vertical videos or scrolling through social media.

If some apps, such as Instagram, look a little different or are wonky, it’ll hopefully be just a matter of time until the developers make an update that optimizes the experience for this new design. Keep in mind that you can also have two apps open onscreen at once, making it easier to multitask.

If you want the best camera experience on an iPhone right now, you should be looking at the iPhone 18 Pro or Pro Max, but that doesn’t make the Duo a slouch for shooting photos, either.

Yes, the Duo is missing a third camera (telephoto), and if that’s important to you, then you probably shouldn’t even be considering Apple’s foldable. But still, if you’re going to shell out this much money for a new phone, test out those cameras — even if it’s in a controlled environment like an Apple Store.

Another thing to check out before you pull the trigger is your selfie shots with this new phone. There are two main ways to do this: with the phone closed, holding it up in the regular way you’d take a selfie using the outer screen’s 12-megapixel front-facing camera, or with the inner display open and shooting with the twin 48-megapixel rear cameras (the outer screen will show a preview of the shot).

There’s also a front-facing camera underneath the inner display, which is mostly for video calls — and which we suspect would produce the least-impressive shots from the device. Try them all and see what you think of the pics. And since you’re at an Apple Store, make sure you delete them afterward.

The odd but interesting aspect ratio of the front display on the iPhone Duo may be a little awkward for typing, so it’s best to do a typing test when you have one in front of you. While the inner display will provide a roomier and more comfortable typing experience, you probably won’t want to have to open up the Duo every time you want to shoot off a quick text to someone. If you have bigger hands, typing on the front screen could be a bit of a nightmare.

Typing one-handed on the small cover screen should be manageable, but try it out to see if it works for you. Some may find it convenient; others may find it cumbersome.

And when you’re typing on the inner display, try the Duo in both portrait and landscape orientations to see which feels better. You can also choose between a split keyboard and larger keys. It’s thoughtful of Apple to offer options that let you tailor the typing experience to your preferences, but you may find that none of them suit your style.

Does the internet performance on your Windows PC ever feel sluggish? Testing the actual speed can tell you if it matches what you expect or what’s promised from your internet provider. To run a speed test in the past, you typically had to go to a dedicated website or install a special app. Now Microsoft is rolling out an integrated speed test tool designed to save your time.

Also: The right way to run an internet speed test

Currently available to Windows Insiders using the Experimental channel, the new built-in internet speed tester measures your download and upload speeds over Ethernet or Wi-Fi. The tool first tests your download performance and then segues to uploads. The final results show you the average speeds for both.

To try the new tool, you need to be part of Microsoft’s Windows Insider Program if you haven’t already joined. When setting this up, make sure you choose the Experimental channel. Since these Insider builds can be unstable, don’t do this on your primary PC. Instead, use a spare computer, a dual-boot setup, or a virtual machine. I run the Insider builds in a VM through VMware Workstation Pro.

Even with the Experimental channel, I had to enable a special flag for the test. To do this, go to Settings, select Windows Update, and then click Windows Insider Program. Select the option for Feature Flags and scroll down the screen to the entry for Quick Settings Speed Test. Click the drop-down button next to it and change the setting to Enabled if it’s not already set that way. Then restart your PC.

Also: Slow Wi-Fi? I found 10 proven ways to fix your internet

After you log back in, you can access the new speed test one of two ways. Left-click on the System tray and choose Speed test from the Quick Settings menu. Otherwise, right-click on the Network icon and select “Test internet speed” from the pop-up menu. With the tool open, just click the Test button to kick it off. The graph will show the download and upload speeds as they’re being tested, with the results giving you the numbers for both.

If this sounds familiar, that’s because Windows 11 already includes an internet speed test tool. In your current version of Windows 11, right-click on the Network icon in the System tray and choose “Perform speed test” from the menu. But as you’ll see, this test isn’t integrated into Windows. Instead, it takes you to a Bing search page where you then have to run the test and see the results on the page. In contrast, the new tool is built into Windows, making it a vast improvement over the existing one.

Also: Ookla’s Wi-Fi diagnostic tool offers one-tap troubleshooting

“Because the test runs against your active internet connection, you can quickly understand how your current connection is performing without leaving your workflow,” Microsoft said in its blog post. “This updated experience builds on the internet speed test we previously introduced for Windows Insiders, bringing the test and its results directly into Windows.”

Microsoft didn’t reveal the source behind the speed test. But the Info icon for the tool says “Powered by SpeedTest,” an indication that this is from internet speed tester Ookla. To gauge the results, I ran both the built-in speed test and the one at Ookla’s website several times, and the performance for both downloads and uploads were similar between the two.

Since the new speed test is only now hitting the Insider Experimental channel, we might have to wait a while before it officially rolls out to all Windows 11 users. But the tool is convenient to use, runs smoothly, and appears to be accurate. So, hopefully we won’t have to wait too long.

#3408. Why does the FCC hate Minions routers?

The viral Minions-shaped routers are caught by the same ban as TP-Link.

The post drew millions of impressions and Amazon's stock, priced at $62.99, was gone by the next day.

Unfortunately for Minions fans, the FCC treats the router version of Kevin and Bob like every other overseas consumer router. Since March, the agency has banned all new foreign-made network routers from getting the authorization they need to go on sale in the US, unless the manufacturer has an exemption. ASUS, Eero and Netgear all have exemptions, but Davolink isn't. So, while the two existing Minions routers stay legal to sell, we're not going to be seeing a Wi-Fi 7 Minion stateside anytime soon, and firmware updates for the duo are only guaranteed through the end of 2028.

Davolink first showed the routers at CES back in January 2023, under an exclusive design agreement with Universal and put them on sale in South Korea that summer. Kevin, AKA DVW-632, is a tri-band Wi-Fi 6E router that stands at 9.6 inches tall, with a 2.5 Gbps WAN port, one gigabit LAN port, WPA3 security and mesh support. Its AXE5400 rating breaks down as 573 Mbps on the 2.4 GHz band and 2.4 Gbps each on 5 GHz and 6 GHz. Bob, the DVW-642, is the smaller one, a dual-band Wi-Fi 6 router with a gigabit WAN port and a top rating of 1.77 Gbps.

Davolink's core business is supplying gateways and access points to the country's three big carriers, SK Broadband, KT and LG Uplus. The US government's vulnerability database has only one entry for a Davolink product: a weak password flaw in a DVW-3200N router from 2018. It was rated 9.8 out of 10 for severity and fixed with new firmware.

The taller Kevin's AXE5400 rating puts it in the same class as TP-Link's Archer AXE75. RTINGS, which has tested both, says the Minion comes out on top in terms of speed and range. But while the more traditional-looking Archer has four gigabit LAN ports, Kevin has only one, so anything beyond one wired device will need a network switch. Wi-Fi 7, the generation after Kevin's, adds 320 MHz channels and lets a device use several bands at once.

The FCC added "routers produced in a foreign country" to its Covered List on March 23. That addition was triggered by a national security determination it received three days earlier from an interagency body convened by the White House. Equipment that makes it onto the Covered List can't get the FCC authorization a wireless device needs before it can be sold in the States. Law firm Wiley's interpretation is that "produced" covers any major stage of making a router, including design and development, regardless of the nationality of the company.

Routers authorized before the March listing can still be imported, sold and used, and the FCC authorized Kevin and Bob between November 2023 and February 2024. Listing also bars changes to authorized devices, software updates included. The FCC has waived that for things like security patches and compatibility fixes until at least January 1, 2029, which is why banned drones and routers in the US will still get critical updates until 2029, and its engineering office has said it will recommend writing the waiver into the rules. If that doesn't happen, and the date doesn't change, Davolink will have to stop patching Kevin and Bob after 2028.

Exemptions, which the FCC calls Conditional Approvals, are granted by the Department of Defense or the Department of Homeland Security and last for up to 18 months. Applicants must disclose where every component and the firmware come from, justify building abroad and commit to a dated plan for US manufacturing, including spending figures and quarterly progress reports. Netgear was approved on April 14, Eero on April 22 and ASUS on September 9. Davolink, which has reported operating losses since 2022, hasn't publicly said whether it has applied for a Conditional Approval.

There are performance improvements across the board in Ghostty 1.4 that yield significant gains compared to prior Ghostty versions.

The biggest win came from a surprising discovery: on macOS, the kernel caps every read from a pty at about 1 KiB no matter how large the read buffer is. AI is finding new holes in the infrastructure of the internet. Is anyone around to patch them?

For the past week, software engineers around the globe have been scrambling to update the backend of websites after a team using an OpenAI model discovered two major vulnerabilities in PHP, a critical piece of web software.

PHP, open-source scripting software that underpins as many as 70% of websites worldwide, can become outdated when website owners don’t update to newer versions of the software, making them vulnerable to attacks. Shortly after alerts were issued about the new vulnerabilities, new versions of PHP were issued, and site operators were urged to update. Some government websites went dark in Europe as these updates were applied, but for much of the web, it’s been left to individual website owners to apply the fixes.

No law compels them to do so, although if a website suffers a data breach, the owner could be liable to customers or clients. And for web surfers, it’s not obvious what PHP version a site is running without running terminal commands or using an online PHP checker.

Given that some websites are no longer actively managed — I own a website that was latent and hadn’t had a PHP update or updates of its WordPress software in years, for instance — the PHP story has shown that the web’s aging architecture is proving to be insecure due to neglect and dwindling resources to keep it safe.

One of the most shocking revelations that has come out of the last week’s race to get websites updated and safe is that an organization tasked with making sure security flaws in PHP are addressed is itself on shaky ground.

Reuters reported that the PHP Foundation, a nonprofit formed in 2021 to work with researchers and cybersecurity companies to ensure a crisis like this wouldn’t happen, has only one full-time staff member and lacks the resources to expand.

The Foundation’s Executive Director Elizabeth Barron told Reuters that she’s the sole staff member and works with about 14 part-time contractors and unpaid board members. As AI-generated threats increase, there’s twice as much demand to change PHP code to address them as in the past. “Yet the number of contributors hasn’t doubled,” Barron said. While companies and governments rely on PHP, “It is clear PHP is severely underfunded,” she said.

A representative for PHP Foundation did not immediately return a request for comment.

Nearly all small businesses have some sort of web presence, whether it’s a social media account, a Google listing or a website they own. But small business owners, a survey from Network Solutions found, may not be addressing the PHP issue because they don’t know it affects them.

The report, based on a survey of 121 small business owners with their own websites, found 45% of them had never heard of PHP and 71% didn’t know if their websites use it. A large majority of 71% of these site owners hadn’t heard about the recent PHP flaws.

Rick Radinger, principal systems architect for Network Solutions Group, said that the survey showed awareness and knowledge gaps, but that in addition to not knowing about the current PHP problem, they may not think it’s their responsibility to act on a fix. “An owner might know about the warning but could assume their hosting provider will handle it,” Radinger said. “Others might need additional help but aren’t sure who to contact or next steps.”

Some of the mobilization about the PHP alerts included updates from large web infrastructure companies like Cloudflare and web hosts such as GoDaddy. In a blog post, Cloudflare said it deployed new web application firewall protections to detect and fend off attacks. GoDaddy made updates to its managed hosting and Windows hosting services that didn’t require website owners to take extra steps, but said that domain customers who manage their own sites or run virtual private servers may need to update PHP themselves.

In addition to its security updates, Cloudflare has also introduced a PHP Capture the Flag contest as part of its Bug Bounty Program on HackerOne. “We’ve put a deliberately vulnerable PHP installation behind our (firewall), and we’re inviting security researchers on HackerOne to try to bypass the protections and capture a flag,” Cloudflare said in its blog post. Cash prizes range from $100 to $10,000.

For those who don’t own a website, the PHP vulnerabilities aren’t something you can protect against by updating your passwords; it’s up to websites to stay updated to protect their visitors.

“For users, it is frustrating that they can’t fix this, as it is an issue with the site’s server, not the browser,” Radinger said. “Simply updating your browser doesn’t address the issue, and a site being up and running normally does not mean the site has been fixed.”

The usual advice applies more than ever: Be careful about clicking on unfamiliar website links, don’t share sensitive information with websites you don’t trust and be especially cautious about links that will download software to your device.

The PHP crisis is a good wake-up call about what’s happening to the World Wide Web as it approaches its fourth decade of operation. This latest security scramble is just one of the threats websites face (AI-generated breaches are a huge one). But many of the dangers to come may stem from aging digital architecture; PHP is just one of myriad tools websites use, including JavaScript, SQL databases, content management systems (like WordPress or Wix), and the software that the servers themselves run. If those aren’t regularly monitored, managed and updated across the web, you can expect a lot more vulnerabilities to emerge in the years to come.

Whenever monthly margin debt has grown above 45% year over year since 1960, there were declines in the stock market five out of six times, data from Fundstrat Global Advisors shows. June's margin debt record came in at 49%, but the S&P 500 has still added more than 13% in 2026. A swing into the red by year-end in accordance with this historic pattern would be a dramatic move — one that would be considered highly unusual for the stock market.

The firm also found that the median performance for S&P 500 forward returns following a breakout of 40% year-over-year margin debt growth has been worse off 12 months later as opposed to one month after it. To be exact, the S&P 500's median 12-month forward return is a 4.2% decline, while the median one-month return is a drop of 2.2%.

In essence, the more rapidly margin debt rises, the worse it is for S&P 500 returns over 12 months.

While margin debt doesn't necessarily cause S&P 500 declines, it can exacerbate existing moves to the downside if there's a significant amount of leverage in the market, said Hardika Singh, economic strategist at Fundstrat.

"The premise of gains beget gains, it turns into losses beget losses, and that's what's troublesome," Singh said. "You have these losses, they keep piling up, and then the stock gets sold, and everyone's starting to close out their position, and it becomes this huge train wreck that's very hard to stop, and it adds a lot of volatility to the market."

Then again, Singh sees why investors like Luu are leaning into such a risky strategy. "No one ever got rich by diversifying," she said.

Luu, who said he's been a "hobbyist" from an early age, has folded in investing as one of his many hobbies, which include activities like climbing and playing guitar. It's become such a hobby that he's created his own social media account detailing his investing journey.

It's not just Luu who views trading as a hobby.

Fundstrat's Singh explained that American traders, particularly younger ones, feel they have to take bigger risks to get ahead. She said it's part of a broader trend dubbed financial nihilism, in which young investors grappling with a tough job market and high inflation turn to less-stable investing tools.

"It makes a lot of sense why individuals, specifically retail investors, keep gravitating towards this margin debt," the economic strategist added. "This is like a drug, and I think people just can't get enough of it in this economy."

Kevin Davis, a 59-year-old retail investor who made a living as a rapper in the 1980s and '90s before becoming a stock broker, believes the risks with leveraged trading outweigh the rewards.

#3349. Junk bonds are 'flashing yellow.' Watch these warning signs

Cracks are forming in the junk bond market as investors demand higher payouts for owning the market's riskiest debt. It isn't time to ditch high-yield bonds, but investors should pay attention to the warning signs.

High-yield bonds now yield 8.1%, up from 7.22% a month ago. The increase reflects a jump in yields across the curve as investors bake in more inflation from high energy prices and other pressures, including concern about the deficit — hitting nearly $2 trillion in the fiscal year that ended Sept. 30.

The high-yield market is also showing stress on the credit side with spreads recently widening to levels not seen since April, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Credit spreads are the difference in yield between the bonds and Treasurys of similar maturities. Wider spreads mean investors are demanding higher yields for holding corporate debt, viewing it as riskier.

Spreads are at 315 basis points in the overall high-yield market, higher than a year ago but still below levels in March when they reached 346 bps. One basis point equals one one-hundredth of a percent, or 0.01%.

The high-yield market consists of bonds rated BB+ by S&P and Fitch and Ba1 and under by Moody's. The lowest-rated cohort, CCC and below, has seen the most movement with spreads climbing dramatically over the past year to roughly 1,250 bps.

Right now, the high-yield market is "flashing yellow" but is "far from red," said Michael Arone, chief investment strategist at State Street Investment Management.

It makes sense that investors are demanding more compensation for taking on additional credit risk as borrowing costs rise, he said.

Yields are elevated across the board with the 10-year Treasury reaching its highest level since 2002 earlier in the week.

"The bigger question is whether this is simply a repricing of interest rate risk, or the beginning of a more fundamental reassessment of credit quality," Arone said.

He's in a wait-and-see mode since earnings are still growing, interest-coverage ratios remain good, and while default rates have ticked up some, he believes it is not concerning.

That said, the starting point in spreads is likely weighing on investors' psyche, since they are still low by historical standards.

"There's a small margin of error here, which I also think raises the anxiety level," Arone explained. "The compensation that investors are receiving for taking on this credit risk isn't overwhelming relative to history, and therefore subtle changes in credit spreads can be concerning."

While there may be concerns about part of the lower-rated market, the overall high-yield market is in good fundamental shape.

In fact, credit quality is at a record high, with BB bonds making up over 60% of the market compared with 38% prior to the global financial crisis, said Kelley Gerrity, a fixed income strategist at Morgan Stanley Investment Management.

"We've had higher-quality companies coming in, and with higher rates now, you also have more discipline from companies that are more indebted ... just because of the higher cost of capital, so that actually is creating a bit of a healthier picture as well," she said.

The lowest tier of high yield has always been more speculative, since the bonds have a higher default risk. Therefore, it's no surprise that they are the ones seeing the most widening in spreads, said Collin Martin, head of fixed income research and strategy at the Schwab Center for Financial Research.

The overall move in high-yield spreads have been "orderly," he said. "Are there cracks forming? I think the cracks are logical cracks with the lowest-rated bonds and it's too early to say that it's spreading to the broad credit market."

In addition, fluctuations within the CCC-rated and lower spreads have been idiosyncratic, Gerrity added. Morgan Stanley recently broke down the spreads in the lowest cohort into two buckets: performing assets and non-performing assets, which are defined as spreads above 1,000 bps.

The non-performing spread to worst is 2,818 bps, while the performing bucket — the biggest piece of the CCC market — is 461 bps, she said.

"It is isolated," she said. "I'm not necessarily sure that that will go away anytime soon, but it doesn't tell the story of overall concern and flashing caution for credit markets right now."

For now, investors should remain selective within higher yield, Gerrity advised.

"We're looking for the best relative value opportunities, and we think that exists within that single-B cohort, and so we're leaning into that," she said.

Investors should be concerned if there is a steep widening in the broader high-yield market.

But there's scant evidence of stress in the BB cohort. Spreads in that group are at 194 bps, up from 179 bps a year ago — although the move hasn't been in a straight line.

"We're going to be focusing our attention on what are considered some stronger businesses," Martin said. "If we start to see the markets demand higher spreads there also, that's what we'll be looking at to see if risks are really rising."

It's also important to understand that the Federal Reserve is raising rates in a strong economic environment, said R.J. Gallo, chief investment officer of global fixed income at Federated Hermes. The central bank is concerned about an inflation problem amid high fossil fuel prices due to the Iran war, while growth has been stronger than expected, he noted.

"With growth being good as the reason that the Fed is hiking, then you wouldn't expect high yield to blow out because growth means revenues stay up, means cash flow and profitability stays up," he explained.

High yield becomes a disaster when the economy is heading into a sharp economic downturn, Gallo said.

"That's when spreads really widen out," he said. "But a recession is not the odds-on bet. Now, talk to me in six months if the Fed keeps hiking over and over, if oil prices stay high for longer, well, then maybe we will start to wonder."

#3348. If You're Worried Inflation Will Wreck Your Retirement, Here Are 3 Must-Dos

Inflation can be a serious threat to your retirement plans. And while inflation doesn't always hit people smack in the face like it does today, it can still be a problem.

Even when prices rise by relatively modest amounts, the cumulative effect over 20 or 30 years can significantly reduce the purchasing power of your savings. Expenses that seem manageable at the start of retirement, from groceries to utilities to healthcare, could consume a much larger share of your retirement income in the future.

The good news is that you don't have to accept inflation as an unavoidable threat to your retirement. Here are three important moves that could give you a leg up on inflation.

Your Social Security benefits might play a meaningful role in your retirement income. Once you turn 62, you can begin collecting benefits at any time. If you wait for your full retirement age to arrive, you'll get your monthly benefits without a reduction.

However, if you delay your Social Security claim past full retirement age, your benefits are eligible for an 8% boost for each year you wait, up to age 70. With a full retirement age of 67, which is the case if you were born in 1960 or later, you can potentially score a 24% increase to your monthly checks.

A larger monthly benefit can be particularly valuable when inflation starts to soar. Social Security benefits receive annual cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs) that are designed to help those payments keep pace with inflation. Starting with a larger monthly benefit can mean receiving larger dollar increases over time.

Of course, delaying isn't right for everyone. Your health, life expectancy, and other factors need to be considered in your Social Security claiming decision. But if you're able to cover your expenses from retirement savings or other income sources while you wait, delaying Social Security until age 70 could make it easier to manage inflation throughout your senior years.

Keeping all of your retirement savings in cash or low-yielding investments might feel like the safest move. But it could expose you to substantial purchasing-power risk over time. If inflation averages 3% annually, but your savings only grow 2% annually, you're going to fall behind.

Stocks have historically provided long-term growth that outpaces inflation. Although the stock market can be volatile, a diversified portfolio of high-quality companies or index funds could help your savings keep up without exposing you to undue risk -- especially if you limit stocks to a portion of your portfolio only.

The right percentage and investment mix depends on your risk tolerance and income needs. For some people, a 65% stock allocation might work. For others, 50% may be optimal. Either way, the goal is to balance growth potential with stability so that your portfolio can support near-term and long-term withdrawals.

Even with a strategic portfolio and a boosted monthly Social Security benefit, inflation can strain your finances if your spending plan is too rigid. It's important to be flexible when costs skyrocket because a few months of cutting back could help your savings last longer.

Start by separating essential expenses such as housing, food, utilities, and healthcare from discretionary costs such as travel and entertainment. That distinction could help you identify where to adjust your spending if prices rise faster than expected or your investments underperform.

Also revisit your budget regularly. If markets decline or essential expenses surge, temporarily reducing nonessential purchases could help preserve your savings.

Inflation has the potential to erode your buying power over time. But that doesn't mean you have to sit back and let it. By delaying Social Security, choosing the right investments, and being willing to adjust your spending as needed, you can set yourself up to thrive even as costs increase throughout your retirement.
