#5920. Review: Emirates A380 First Class, Still Spectacular After All These Years

Emirates’ A380 first class offered a fabulous experience on my flight from Mumbai to Dubai, with a comfortable suite, a delicious breakfast, an onboard shower, and outstanding service. The only problem was that the flight was far too short. Even with my stomach still recovering from India, I would happily have stayed onboard all the way to Los Angeles.

This flight was one of the reasons I planned my trip to India in the first place. I wanted to revisit Emirates’ A380 first class and then try its newer Boeing 777-300ER first class suite on my connection to Brussels.

It had been several years since my last Emirates A380 first class flight. The suite is no longer cutting edge, but I was looking forward to the experience very much and I was not disappointed.

As I explained in the introduction to this trip report, I booked Mumbai–Dubai–Brussels for 102,000 Emirates Skywards miles and approximately $700 in taxes and fees.

That included this A380 flight and the following day’s flight to Brussels on a Boeing 777-300ER featuring Emirates’ “Game Changer” first class suites.

I booked before Emirates introduced its restrictions on first class award bookings. Having about 100,000 miles already in my Skywards account, this was an attractive way to use them and compare two different first class products.

The departure time from Mumbai was hardly ideal, but I was determined to make the most of the flight.

I left the Four Seasons Mumbai around 8:00 pm and arrived at the airport well ahead of departure. Emirates’ check-in counter was not ready to accept me and I was told to return at midnight. Thankfully, I had already checked in online and had my digital boarding pass. That allowed me to proceed through security and immigration rather than wait landside for a paper copy.

I spent the time before boarding in the first class section of the Adani Lounge.

About 70 minutes before departure, I headed over to the gate where I found a gaggle of people waiting to board, with a different line for every class of service. I had the first class line to myself. We boarded a bit late at 4:24 am (the exact reason for the delay was not given).

I boarded directly onto the upper deck, turning left into the first class cabin.

First class is located at the front of the upper deck, with 14 suites arranged in a 1-2-1 configuration. The window seats are the obvious choice when traveling alone, while the center pairs make more sense for those traveling together.

Each seat is 23 inches wide, expanding to 28 inches in bed mode, and reclines into a fully flat bed measuring 78 inches long. All suites have direct aisle access and sliding privacy doors, though the doors do not extend to the ceiling.

Storage included a large area underneath the screen/table, two smaller compartments, and a shelf with a flip-up vanity mirror and a basket of snacks. There are no overhead bins in first class, which makes the cabin feel more open, but means your carry-on bag stays on the floor inside your suite.

Next to the seat is a personal minibar stocked with soft drinks and a tablet that controls the seat functions. Having drinks within reach is convenient, though with such attentive service on this flight, I did not avail myself to anything.

Each suite also has two adjustable air vents, though the cabin never got warm.

I had selected 2A, but moved back to 3A when it became clear that no one was seated in the rear half of the cabin. There were only four first class passengers on this flight, which made an already spacious cabin feel even more private (moving also made taking pictures easier without someone sitting directly across the aisle).

The woodgrain and gold trim are certainly not subtle and it does looks dated now, but the suite was well-maintained and extremely comfortable. I don’t mind a little gaudy decor when everything works and the seat is this pleasant to sit in, plus I loved the mood lighting.

After breakfast and my shower, I returned to find that the crew had prepared the suite across the aisle as a bed.

That was a lovely touch. With so few passengers onboard, I could keep my own suite for sitting and dining and use another for resting. A 4:25 am departure would normally make sleep my first priority. On this flight, though, I wanted to enjoy the seat, breakfast, bar, and shower before closing my eyes.

There simply was not enough time for everything and there wasn’t time for anything more than a quick cat nap.

Slippers and pajamas were proactively provided, though I did not change into them on this short flight (a friend had requested them so I just put them in my bag). I also forgot to request an amenity kit (silly me), which are available via request. I’ll review the amenity kit when I review my next flight.

Breakfast was served on this early-morning flight. My stomach was still recovering from Delhi Belly, so this was not going to be the occasion to sample everything Emirates had loaded onboard.

I skipped the Champagne and caviar, hoping I would feel more adventurous on my flight to Brussels the following day. The menus are now magazine-style:

The crew began mothering me almost immediately when I explained my somatic issues, I was offered herbal tea as my pre-departure beverage, served with some biscuits.

Later on before pushback, I was offered Arabic coffee and dates.

For breakfast, I ordered a cheese omelet, which seemed like a reasonably safe choice.

(While it was being prepared, I walked back to the onboard lounge. Upon returning to my suite, I enjoyed a very good breakfast).

The omelet was good, but the accompaniments stole the show. A cube-shaped cinnamon roll was rich and delicious, as was the rhubarb yogurt…delicious.

A cappuccino completed the meal, plus more freshly cut fruit after my shower.

I would have liked to try more, but I was thankful to enjoy breakfast at all after the preceding day in Mumbai. The crew was so considerate about my condition, which I will discuss below.

One of my favorite features of the Emirates A380 is the lounge at the rear of the upper deck.

I love this unique feature to the A380 and although it takes up a lot of real estate, it is one of the primary things that gives Emirates an outsized reputation of quality and luxury. When I arrived, passengers and crew were watching a Belgium–USA World Cup match live.

Belgium was leading 1-0 when I reached the bar. The USA tied in, then Belgium scored again…and then two more times. The game was moving considerably faster than I expected, though my main interest was the lounge itself.

I was offered a glass of Krug (limited only to first class passengers), but skipped that due to my stomach issues.

On a longer flight, I could see myself spending quite a bit of time here. On this flight, breakfast was waiting and I still had a shower to take.

The A380’s shower spas remain a fabulous feature. At the front of the first class cabin, the large lavatories include space to change and an actual shower. This wasn’t my first shower onboard a flight, but being able to shower at 40,000 feet still makes me smile.

The bathroom was well-stocked with shower amenities:

You receive five minutes of running water, which can be stopped and started. That does require a little attention, but it is more than enough and the pressure was acceptable.

I was the only passenger who showered so I suppose I could have asked for a second one, haha.

I emerged refreshed and returned to the cabin, where my bed was waiting. Breakfast, a shower, and then a nap…quite a way to travel between Mumbai and Dubai.

I’ll write a separate post on the shower experience.

Emirates calls its entertainment system “ICE” and the large screen in the suite offers considerably more entertainment than anyone could use on a flight this short including hundreds of movies, TV shows, games, live TV, and music. A tablet in the side console makes it easy to scroll through content.

Noise-cancelling headphones were from Bowers & Wilkins and very nice.

I just enjoyed some classical music while watching the tailcam.

Emirates offers complimentary inflight Wi-Fi to Skywards members traveling in first class, provided your membership number is attached to the reservation. While it wasn’t Starlink (that’s coming), the OnAir system worked fairly well.

The purser, Miss Donna from the Philippines, was proactive, gracious, and welcoming. Miss Hee from South Korea was also fabulous. They were concerned about my stomach troubles and rather mothered me throughout the flight, which I appreciated very much.

A crew can make or break a flight, even with an excellent seat and this crew could not have been better.

There were only four passengers in first class, which certainly helped. But a lightly booked cabin does not automatically produce attentive service. It still requires people who care about their work and pay attention to the passengers in front of them.

The thoughtful preparation of a separate bed was one example. Their concern about how I was feeling and constant attention really made me feel special.

This was an early-morning flight of only about three hours, yet they made it a first class experience and I was very grateful.

Despite the late departure from Mumbai, we landed on time on Dubai, with the sun having just risen and an army of Emirates jets present on the ground.

My Emirates A380 first class flight from Mumbai to Dubai was excellent. The suite was comfortable and well-maintained, breakfast was delicious, and I enjoyed both the onboard lounge and shower.

But the crew made the strongest impression. Donna and Hee were wonderful, particularly while I was not feeling my best, and made the short flight very special. Emirates’ A380 first class is no longer the newest or necessarily the best suite in the sky, but that did not diminish how much I enjoyed this flight.

Next time, I would like a longer flight…and a stomach ready for the Champagne and caviar.

#5917. Bilt Program Changes For 2027 Impact Bilt Cash, Credit Cards, & Hotel Redemptions

Bilt is the program known for its lucrative credit card portfolio and rewards on housing payments. I’d say Bilt can be extremely worthwhile, though also really complicated, especially in light of the changes that we saw in early 2026. Well, Bilt has now announced quite a few program updates for 2027.

Some are positive, some are negative, but I’d argue that the fundamental value proposition isn’t changing much here. If you liked the Bilt Blue Card (learn more), Bilt Obsidian Card (learn more), or Bilt Palladium Card (learn more) before, you probably won’t feel too different (Rates & Fees).

Bilt has revealed changes to how members can earn points and rewards in 2027. There are quite a few updates here, so let’s just go through them — I’ll rank them roughly in order of importance, as I see it, starting with the most significant change.

Currently, those with a Bilt credit card can earn 4% back on spending in the form of Bilt Cash, in addition to the points that they earn. Starting on your next card account anniversary, Bilt credit cards are being moved to what’s being described as a “simpler” set of benefits.

The most significant change is that by default, everyone will earn up to 1.25x Bilt Points on their rent or mortgage payment, as Bilt claims it’s simplifying the program and retiring Flexible Bilt Cash mode, which earns 4% back in Bilt Cash on everyday spend (in addition to points).

But here’s the good news — cardmembers aren’t actually being forced to move from earning 4% Bilt Cash to earning up to 1.25x points on their housing payments. Instead, if you’d like, you can keep earning in Flexible Bilt Cash mode. If that’s your preference, you just have to opt out of the new benefits in your card settings before your card anniversary date, and then you’ll keep any eligible annual credits, and won’t be able to switch between Flexible Bilt Cash and housing-only mode.

New cardmembers will also have this option through December 31, 2026, though as of January 1, 2027, the 1.25x points will be the automatic earning rate. So for the time being, not a whole lot is actually changing here, except no more flexibility to switch between earning options month to month.

Bilt is giving members more time to redeem Bilt Cash. Up until now, Bilt Cash has expired at the end of the calendar year, with the ability for $100 in Bilt Cash to roll over to the following year.

With these changes, Bilt Cash now expires at the end of the month, 12 months after it’s earned. In other words, Bilt Cash earned in October 2026 would expire on October 31, 2027. This addresses a major point of frustration that many members have had.

#5915. Tesla (TSLA) proxy shows a board getting ready for a SpaceX merger

Tesla’s proxy for its 2026 annual meeting, filed today, confirms that one of its supposedly independent directors now sits on SpaceX’s board. Ira Ehrenpreis, who chairs both Tesla’s compensation committee and its governance committee, joined SpaceX’s board in February.

That’s one of several details in the filing that line up with a Tesla-SpaceX merger. There’s a three-item ballot, an auto-voting program that kicks in for the next vote, and Musk pay terms that pay out on a deal price alone.

Tesla’s 2026 annual meeting is set for December 3 at Gigafactory Texas, for shareholders of record as of October 13.

They get three things to vote on. The first is re-electing Elon Musk, board chair Robyn Denholm and JB Straubel to three-year terms. The second is a non-binding say-on-pay vote, and the third is ratifying PwC as Tesla’s auditor, a job it has held since 2005.

That’s it. No shareholder proposals made the ballot this year. At least, not yet. Last year’s meeting featured several, including one asking the board to invest in xAI, which has since been absorbed by SpaceX.

The board also says it won’t bring back proposals to declassify itself or to drop Tesla’s supermajority voting requirements. Its 2025 attempt drew 73.6% participation but only 39.4% of outstanding shares in support, short of the bar. So the staggered board stays, and the three directors up this year would serve until 2029.

Denholm’s letter makes the pitch directly, telling shareholders that “a steady and experienced hand during this time of rapid technological change is essential to Tesla’s success.”

As chair, Denholm has long been criticized for letting Elon Musk do what he wants, unchecked by the board, but with Musk’s backing, it’s doubtful Tesla shareholders can get her out.

The say-on-pay item asks shareholders to approve Musk’s 2025 compensation, which the proxy reports at $158,359,009,867. Against a median Tesla employee who made $62,786, that’s a CEO pay ratio of 2,522,203 to 1.

None of that was cash. The figure combines $132.3 billion for the 2025 CEO Performance Award, valued as if every milestone gets hit, and $26.1 billion for the August 2025 “interim award.” Musk forfeited the interim award in April after the Delaware Supreme Court reinstated his 2018 package.

Tesla adds its own “realized compensation” table showing Musk received $0 in 2025, for a pay ratio of “0.00:1.” Accurate for 2025. But the same proxy discloses that on June 16, 2026, Musk exercised all 303,960,630 of his 2018 options, covering the $23.34 exercise price by having Tesla withhold 17,531,857 shares. That implies a share price of about $405 and a value of roughly $116 billion at exercise.

Tesla’s income statement tells a third story. The 2025 award has generated $689 million in stock-based compensation expense so far: $162 million in 2025 and $527 million in the first half of 2026. Tesla only books expense for milestones it considers “probable,” and out of 12 operational milestones it rates exactly one as probable: 20 million cumulative vehicle deliveries.

FSD subscriptions, Optimus, robotaxis and every EBITDA target sit in the “not probable” bucket, worth $105.8 billion to $120.4 billion in unrecognized expense.

All of this comes after a year where Tesla’s net income fell 46% to $3.86 billion and revenue declined for a second straight year, to $94.8 billion.

As we noted when Tesla got the SEC’s OK to auto-vote retail shares, the 2025 award works differently in a change in control. The filings lay out exactly how.

Normally, each of the 12 tranches needs a market cap milestone, measured as a trailing average over six months and 30 days, plus an operational milestone. In a change in control, operational milestones drop out. Tesla’s market cap gets measured once: shares outstanding multiplied by the higher of the last closing price or the per-share value shareholders receive in the deal. Any tranche earned that way vests immediately instead of after 7.5 to 10 years.

With 3.95 billion shares outstanding, the first $2 trillion tranche works out to about $506 per share. A deal valuing Tesla at $3 trillion, or about $759 per share, would earn Musk three tranches, or 105.9 million shares, without a single robotaxi or Optimus milestone.

The April 2026 Implementation Agreement for the 2018 package follows the same logic. The 286.4 million restricted shares Musk received from the June exercise don’t vest until January 19, 2028. A change in control while he’s still CEO, or running product or operations, vests them right away.

There are two options. The first votes your shares with the board on everything. The second votes with the board on everything except a “Special Matter,” which Tesla defines as a contested director election or a merger, acquisition or divestiture that needs shareholder approval.

There’s no option to vote against the board. Shareholders who pick “all matters” will have their shares counted for the board’s position on a merger unless they log in and override it. Tesla’s only commitment is a reminder before that meeting.

The instructions don’t apply to the December 3 vote. They start with the next shareholder meeting, which is where a SpaceX deal would land.

Travel brands are pouring more money into creators, but spend alone won’t build an edge. The advantage will come from learning faster, measuring better, and turning what works into a repeatable system.

This sponsored content was created in collaboration with a Skift partner.

Creators and social media now influence over $7 trillion in global travel spending, according to figures shared by Skift Head of Research Seth Borko at the inaugural Skift Creator Summit, which took place in New York City on September 22, 2026.

That level of influence shows how far creator marketing has moved from the margins of the travel industry, but the challenge facing brands now is turning that influence into a repeatable business function.

Sanjay Bhatia, director and head of industry, travel, auto & experiences at Meta, and Laura Troy, senior director, creative and content, U.S. and Canada at Marriott International, joined Skift Studio Content Director Alison McCarthy on stage to discuss how travel brands can move beyond one-off creator campaigns and build a more scalable, measurable system around creator marketing.

Bhatia identified three characteristics that distinguish established creator programs from more fragmented approaches: organizational ownership, a defined budget, and creative planning that begins alongside the media strategy.

“Creator strategy has to be a priority internally, with clear accountability,” he said. “There needs to be someone on the organization chart who owns the program and is responsible for it.”

According to Troy, there was “no rule book” and “no playbook” for social media when she joined Marriott’s luxury division in 2007. That early experience shaped a test-and-learn approach, but Troy said experimentation still needs a clear definition of success.

“You could have a beautiful piece of creator content, but if you weren’t aligned on the goals and what success looked like, it could fall flat,” she said.

Paid media has consequently become central to Marriott’s approach to creator content. Troy said creators need to understand not only the organic story they’re being asked to tell but also how the asset will function once media investment sits behind it.

That integration also changes the internal structure of creator marketing. Work that may once have primarily involved social or PR now requires creative, brand, media, and performance teams to agree on objectives and measurement.

Bhatia described organizational constraints as one of the biggest barriers he sees across brands. A shared definition of success can help bring those groups together. He said creator marketing was growing at four times the rate of the rest of the media. Meta’s presentation also cited forecasts putting paid creator amplification at $13 billion, up 48% year over year, while 92% of marketers said creator content outperforms their own.

Bhatia broke the creator process into four stages: discover, connect, scale, and optimize.

Discovery increasingly means looking beyond follower counts to identify creators who match a particular audience or traveler intent. For example, Meta’s Creator Marketing Hub makes more than seven million creators across Instagram and Facebook discoverable, with recommendations informed by Meta’s machine learning and first-party performance data.

The second step is creating the permissions and rights structure that allows brands to use creator content beyond an isolated organic post. Scale then becomes a matter of identifying content that’s already resonating and backing it with media.

Bhatia said Meta is seeing Partnership Ads generate two times the incremental return on ad spend and a 94% improvement in cost per lift for brand consideration. Results from those campaigns can then feed back into the discovery and planning process.

Marriott has followed a similar test-and-learn path. Troy said its creator strategy has evolved from early hotel-stay exchanges around 2015 into a broader marketing function, with creators now used both for social distribution and as talent in paid campaigns across multiple formats.

However, the starting point still depends on the business problem.

“Understand what scale means because it might mean different things to different people,” Troy said. Brands should then demonstrate impact against the chosen objective, whether that’s awareness, engagement, or attributed revenue, before expanding the model.

A recurring theme throughout the summit was the need to treat every campaign as a source of intelligence.

Troy said she works closely with Marriott’s paid media teams to understand which creative performed well, why something underperformed, and whether factors such as campaign optimization or too many competing assets affected the results.

“The worst thing you can do is decide something didn’t work and never talk about it again, because you’ll make the same mistake,” she said.

Paid amplification can make that learning more concrete. Bhatia said brands can examine metrics such as cost per acquisition, return on ad spend, and incrementality once creator content enters a measurable media environment.

The roundtable discussions that followed the session added an important caveat. Creator influence may take months to convert into a booking. One participant said partnership ads run with Booking Holdings and Tripadvisor Group had produced about a 30% improvement in conversion rate and efficiency, but many conversions did not appear until 90 days or later. Longer attribution windows can therefore give travel marketers a more accurate picture of performance.

Evan Frank, co-founder of Fora, who also spoke at the summit, made a related point. He said brands often confuse awareness with transaction, even though the content that drives discovery is not always the same content that drives a booking.

That should also shape the next round of testing. Brands should test content first, not simply creators. A creator who performs well in one context may need a different format, message, or placement elsewhere. Likewise, an organic post already generating a strong audience response can serve as an important signal before a brand commissions entirely new material.

Successful programs, therefore, do not simply replicate one winning asset. Bhatia argued that brands should scale their production models by using different creators, formats, and placements around the same underlying consumer insight.

Marriott’s portfolio gives Troy a large canvas for this strategy. The company has over 30 hotel brands, in addition to partnerships spanning sports and entertainment. That breadth helps Marriott target different interests.

“It’s not a one-size-fits-all,” Troy said, adding that younger consumers increasingly expect more personalized content. Smaller travel brands can turn the opposite characteristic into an advantage.

Bhatia said a single hotel or destination can define a much narrower audience and go deeper with a smaller group of creators. He cited research showing that over 90% of people value expert knowledge most in creators. His recommendation was to “go narrow deliberately,” finding a specific niche and building deeper relationships with creators who understand it.

Dan Holowack, founder and CEO of CrowdRiff, who also spoke at the Skift Creator Summit, made a similar case. He said nano and micro influencers are generating the highest engagement, with 73% of brands now seeking them out.

The main takeaway of the summit was that learning needs to compound. Brands planning their 2027 creator strategies should focus as much on accumulating knowledge as accumulating reach. Troy encouraged marketers to identify what’s worked, decide where the business needs to drive demand, and define what success should look like before choosing creators.

The same idea surfaced repeatedly in the roundtables. Participants stressed the value of building longer-term creator relationships, looking beyond follower counts, and giving campaigns enough time to show their full impact. Some also pointed to the importance of reusing strong creator content across channels rather than treating each activation as a standalone asset.

Bhatia’s closing point was that each campaign should leave brands better informed for the next.

“Creators earn trust, brands amplify what works, the results demonstrate value, and both sides invest more deeply,” he said.
