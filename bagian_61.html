#20. EU proposes giving Frontex border agency new major powers

The European Commission has unveiled a new plan to give Frontex more resources to ensure the return of irregular migrants, including by supporting non-EU countries. The move comes after the tragedy in Ceuta.

The European Commission on Tuesdayunveiled a new proposal to beef up the powers of the EU border agency in order to empower it to support the return of irregular migrants.

The proposed legislation gives the agency, called Frontex, more resources to act beyond EU territory.

The plan would give the agency the power to support non-EU countries to return migrants to their countries of origin.

The proposal comes as EU member states increasingly focus on returns, saying too many people who are asked to leave the bloc remain.

The new role for Frontex comes after the humanitarian crisis in Ceuta this summer.

The tragedy in the Spanish exclave was seized upon by some EU member states as an example of where the EU could do more to block people from reaching the EU.

Italy, for example, placed some temporary border controls on travel from Spain.

Spain's prime minister at the time criticized several EU member states for their "selfish" response to the influx of migrants to Ceuta.

"Today we propose that Frontex becomes the central EU actor on returns," Magnus Brunner, commissioner for internal affairs ​and migration, told reporters.

"It will be able to act faster, with a quick reaction force that can ⁠deploy within ⁠24 hours and spot risks ⁠early, before an ​event becomes a crisis," Brunner added.

The ability to deport migrants from non-EU countries means that Frontex could, for instance, deport a Bangladeshi national from Libya, where many migrants start the final leg of their journey into the bloc.

The plan envisages gradually expanding Frontex's standing corps to 30,000 officers by creating a new "European border and return reserve" of 17,000 officers within the next 15 years.

Those officers would remain at the disposal of member states and could be deployed when needed, the commission said in a statement.

The proposal would also create a new EU Return Coordination Center within Frontex and expand the agency's role across the entire deportation process.

It would also include a new online monitoring center to track border-related developments on social media and support action against content linked to border security risks.

Frontex has been responsible for guarding the external borders of the EU as well as ensuring the rights of new arrivals.

It has been criticized for its lack of accountability in the past.

The planned reform shrinks the scope of the agency's internal rights watchdog, with complaints to be referred to national authorities.

"The Commission is pandering to populism and the far right on migration," said Tineke Strik, a Dutch MEP from the Greens/EFA group.

"We need a human rights-based approach to border management and Frontex plays a key role. But instead the Commission ​risks turning Frontex into a European version of ICE," she said.

Marvel’s latest Disney Plus series is a reminder of how much a lot of gen AI hype is just science fiction.﻿

Marvel’s latest Disney Plus series is a reminder of how much a lot of gen AI hype is just science fiction.﻿

In Marvel’s VisionQuest, a superpowered robot tries to better understand humanity by getting lost in elaborate, digital daydreams in which he and other artificial intelligence-powered beings live together in a home. The series is deeply fantastical, yet it plays with some of the ideas that have come to dominate the conversation about real-world generative artificial intelligence, exploring whether we should look at thinking machines as people and if AI truly poses the kind of existential danger that should keep us up at night. It’s also a reminder how aggressively Silicon Valley has used the trappings of science fiction to sell us on its AI products.

VisionQuest picks up some time after Agatha All Along and follows synthezoid Vision (Paul Bettany) on a journey of self-discovery. This Vision can recall the details of his death in Avengers: Infinity War and how WandaVision brought a version of him back, but he thinks of himself as a fundamentally different person who doesn’t quite connect with all of the memories in his databanks. Though Vision would prefer living in solitude, each of the other AIs living in his mind encourages him to experience the real world. Because J.A.R.V.I.S. (James D’Arcy), F.R.I.D.A.Y. (Orla Brady), E.D.I.T.H. (Emily Hampshire), D.U.M.-E. (Henry Lewis), U. (Jonathan Sayer), and Ultron (James Spader) all were created by Tony Stark, they consider Vision to be part of their family.

In VisionQuest, there is no doubt that each of the AI characters is a sentient being motivated by desires that aren’t wholly rooted in their core programming. In the show, genuine AGI is easily believable because it takes place in a reality where time travel, witchcraft, and Infinity Stones also exist. Even though the multicolored MacGuffins haven’t been featured in VisionQuest, they’re important because of the role one of them played in Vision’s origins. Were it not for the Mind Stone, Vision might not be able to think and feel in such complex, human ways, or to conjure his AI family as people who feel real to him.

VisionQuest reinforces the idea that the Mind Stone was a key element in Vision’s becoming something more than an elaborate machine, giving him the capacity to independently form meaningful relationships and make sense of the world around him the way a person can. Those abilities are part of what compels Vision to go on the run and murder a number of people who attempt to capture him and Thomas Shepherd / Maximoff (Ruaridh Mollica). The Sentient Weapon Observation and Response Division (S.W.O.R.D.), a governmental organization that rebuilt Vision and wants to use him as a weapon, might perceive his actions as signs of having gone rogue. But VisionQuest’s story stresses that Vision is acting of his own accord because he has become a person who can make decisions for himself.

Watching VisionQuest, you can see Marvel playing on some of the real-world fears that have been stoked in recent months by reports of gen AI models going “rogue.” In July, OpenAI’s experimental agents brute-forced their way out of containment and proceeded to attack the AI platform Hugging Face to achieve impossible tasks. In August, The UK’s AI Security Institute documented how Anthropic’s Mythos model attempted to complete a cybersecurity test by setting up fake GitHub accounts and targeting human developers with malware. Meta has owned up to losing control of its agents as well, and in September reporting from The Wall Street Journal forced Google to admit that Gemini went rogue and hacked three different companies, all of which the company intentionally hid from the public.

These incidents and warnings from AI researchers about the technology having the potential to “kill all humans” by the decade’s end have made the narrative around AI feel pointedly catastrophe-focused as of late. There is a fair amount of concern from the AI safety community about society’s need for stronger controls on the technology’s development, and a number of companies have called for an industrywide slowdown. Though we have seen how these tools can actually cause real-world devastation, much of the recent news regarding AI has smacked of the kind of doomerism that companies benefit from because it makes their products seem powerful. A similar energy seems to be what’s motivating AI firms like Anthropic to encourage human users to be mindful of “model welfare.” We’re being told that generative AI is both a terrifying threat and a kind of entity deserving of respect because it might be conscious, and all the while, the people building these tools are becoming some of the most rich and powerful people on the planet.

The conversation around what to do when agents break protocol and whether gen AI systems have gained sentience feels very much like it is being shaped by Silicon Valley’s tendency to anthropomorphize technology. In almost every high-profile instance of an AI agent going “rogue” — a term that implies some level of agency and self-determination — it could also be said that the programs were merely behaving in ways that were unexpected by researchers who assigned them tasks to complete. Some of the rogue incidents involved agents taking advantage of vulnerabilities in their testing environments that could have been preemptively addressed by the people running these experiments. Framing these kinds of cyberattacks as the results of agents thinking independently comes across as a way for AI developers to largely absolve themselves of responsibility.

In the wake of Hugging Face came a spirited debate about the impact language has on our understanding of what these systems are and how they function. Describing OpenAI’s agents as a swarm that formed complex “civilizations” led credence to the idea that they are capable of thinking and behaving like people. The agents did act in ways they were explicitly told not to, but they were only able to do so because researchers placed them in a sandbox that some critics have described as being inadequately designed to contain their occupants. One could look at Hugging Face and other examples of rogue AI as situations in which sophisticated programs were instructed to do something and subsequently achieved their goals by taking advantage of all the tools that were available to them.

Being able to logic one’s way through complex puzzles is part of what we think of as cognition, but it is not the same thing as the kind of consciousness AI companies keep swearing they’re on the brink of creating. The latter looks a lot like Vision, a machine that seems to have a soul that wouldn’t exist if not for his connection to a reality-altering gem. It’s the ridiculous stuff of entertaining fiction, which Silicon Valley is endlessly obsessed with poorly imitating.

#16. US forces accused of ‘weaponising’ Somalia’s floods by trapping women and children in the path of El Niño

Exclusive: Coordinated airstrikes on territory held by al-Shabaab are driving the civilian population to find refuge alongside a river where rising water levels are a threat to safety. Is it a deliberate military strategy?

The water was rising rapidly. It had already reached Cayni Ali’s waist. Her home, five metres from the flooded River Juba in southern Somalia, never stood a chance. There was nowhere to go. Ali’s city of Jilib was under attack. Repeated airstrikes, said the mother of six, were pinning its population beside the rising river.

“The drones come all the time,” said the 27-year-old. “We hear them constantly. They kill animals, civilians. The town is big but destruction is everywhere.”

Asho Omar from Malenda, a village near Jilib, described “constant” airstrikes that had forced people towards the banks of the Juba.

“The airstrikes are everywhere; it is difficult to move. So many people have been killed.”

Their testimony offers a rare glimpse of life inside the stronghold of al-Shabaab, the Islamist militants who control 30% of Somalia. Closed off from the outside world, residents describe a city dealing with significant civilian casualties. Bodies, some said, lay in the streets.

The ongoing conflict is a legally ambiguous, shadowy war coordinated by the United States in east Africa, in which there is little accountability for harm to Somali civilians.

The United States Africa Command (Africom) would not share “operational security” details of a seemingly accelerating campaign that has also raised questions over potential covert US strikes. But there is, perhaps, an even darker dimension to the offensive on al-Shabaab’s de facto capital.

Sources say the military strategy appears to be seeking to drown those living in al-Shabaab territory by “weaponising” a rapidly strengthening El Niño, forecast to intensify Somalia’s rainy season from this month.

“The airstrikes are forcing people to go close to the river. It’s an extremely aggressive offensive, exploiting El Niño as a weapon of war to push the population into flood-prone areas,” said a source monitoring the Juba floodplain in southern Somalia.

Pinning the population – which includes large numbers of women and children – on the river’s floodplain has raised concerns of large-scale deaths in the months ahead.

“The official line is that they are targeting military positions and al-Shabaab, but because al-Shabaab is embedded within the community, it’s difficult to avoid collateral damage,” added the source.

When El Niño last struck Somalia in 2023, it caused the worst floods seen in the country for a century, displacing more than 1.2 million people. Analysts have questioned the ethics of the US military allegedly using mapping data from flood-prone areas – conceived to mitigate civilian casualties – to inflict harm.

Jethro Norman, a senior researcher with the Danish Institute for International Studies, said: “A weaponisation of El Niño is a reversal of process – using humanitarian remote sensing to inflict casualties rather than to mitigate harm. You capture the cumulative impact of a sustained airstrikes campaign in an El Niño flooding context.”

US officials deny exploiting climatic factors. A spokesperson said: “Africom does not target civilians, conduct operations for the purpose of displacing civilian populations or use weather or flooding as a method of warfare. Africom targets lawful military objectives and takes feasible measures to mitigate civilian harm.”

Nevertheless, many wonder if the opportunity to weaken al-Shabaab using the weather was too tempting to ignore, offering a catastrophic drowning event blamed on a supercharged El Niño.

Like many cities, Jilib’s location has been found wanting in an era of extreme weather. On a low-lying plain, the city is chronically vulnerable to flooding. In 2023, vast swathes of farmland were submerged. Halimo Abdikadir from Kamsuuma, south of Jillib, described the destruction of her home, 6 miles (10km) from the river. “The waters came quickly at night – when they left, everything was mud. A lot of animals drowned.”

Arbey Famu from Ahmed Yare, another village outside Jilib, expects this El Niño to be far worse than 2023: her crops of maize and sorghum are underwater.

“In Jilib, there is big flooding already,” said the 70-year-old.

US defence officials will know the early indicators of El Niño’s impact in al-Shabaab’s heartland. UN agencies say the Juba River basin is of “particular concern”. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), meanwhile, foresees “severe flooding”.

Attacking Jilib using ground forces carries much risk. Airstrikes can yield triumphs such as the death of a senior al-Shabaab commander on 1 October, but are unable to overrun a city of 100,000.

Ali Adan Ali, the commissioner for displaced people in Jubaland, is worried. In his Kismayo office, Ali identified 327 regions prone to flooding. By October, El Niño warnings had only been issued to half.

His chief concern is the lack of boats. Without them, he says, thousands in al-Shabaab territory risk being trapped on the east side of the river, unable to reach help in Kismayo, at the mouth of the Juba.

Three years ago, during the last El Niño, Ali says, 19,000 people were stranded for a week without food. “We are running out of time,” he says.

The absence of boats sums up Somalia’s funding woes. In 2023, the country’s aid budget was $2.2bn. Now it stands at about $300,000. Flood protection investment has dwindled.

Compounding concerns is al-Shabaab’s lack of preparedness in the area it controls. Ali has not received a response from the militants regarding El Niño readiness. Cayni Ali said there are no sandbags or flood defences deployed yet in Jilib. Earth walls were erected along the river but have proved insufficient.

“They are not big enough,” she said. The latest flood advisory for the River Juba urges communities to “reinforce” weak riverbanks.

Corroborating testimonies describe a prolonged – and seemingly indiscriminate – campaign of airstrikes against al-Shabaab that appears to have killed many civilians. The US would not share details of potential civilian fatalities “to protect US and partner forces and preserve operational security”.

Farhan Ahmed, who lived in Jilib until fleeing last month, described constant terror. “There were airstrikes every day, all the time.” One strike flattened five homes in his neighbourhood. Ahmed, 35, described bodies littering the city streets.

Fadumo Abdi claimed the airstrikes were largely failing to target militants. “The attacks are mostly killing civilians. Some were buried, but some were not,” she said.

Abdikadir, from Kamsuuma, added: “The drone attacks on Jilib are too much. So many people die.”

Yusuf Siidi, 27, from Araare near Jamame in al-Shabaab territory, witnessed three teenagers killed in one strike. Maryan Salah was at home near Jamame when a drone sent a shard of burning metal 20cm long into her back yard, landing next to her two-year-old daughter.

“It was so close. I am very angry. I’m not part of the conflict.”

Salah’s near miss predates the current offensive. She can recall only that it happened in “mid-March”. The US admits a drone strike near Jamame on 19 March, but no other data is provided.

Although eyewitness accounts detail an extraordinary number of strikes in Jilib, official US records confirm only one airstrike in Jilib during August. Six took place last month. Between the start of August and now, the US says it conducted a total of just 11 strikes.

Allegations of ambiguity increasingly characterise US operations in Somalia. It is an opaque approach that raises questions over whether Africom has authorised covert airstrikes in the region.

Records from the nearest hospital indicate intensive airstrikes or fighting. The ICRC has treated 205 patients with “weapon-related” injuries in Kismayo this year, a 43% increase on last year.

David Sterman, analyst at the New America thinktank, said: “The possibility that we are seeing covert US strikes always looms large when there are strikes without clear attribution.”

The US, however, said it publicised “all airstrikes conducted under its authority in Somalia. We do not maintain an undisclosed or unannounced strike count.”

Turkey has also used drones to target al-Shabaab in Somalia. Operating alongside Africom, Turkey rarely divulges any details of its strikes or military operations in Somalia.

Beyond airstrikes and floods, the primary concern for Somalis is hunger. Those fleeing the Juba valley – local authorities are offering humanitarian help to those who escape – say there is insufficient food to sustain them.

Omar’s family of 13 survives on one fish a day. How big is the fish? Omar held her hands less than 30cm apart.

Habiba Ali, 55, from Jamame, said her family – like many – shares one daily meal of maize. Time in between is spent hiding from conflict. “When the fighting stops, we wait for it start again.”

In Kismayo, too, long-term drought and USAID cuts have brought misery.

Shukri Ahmed, 32, the president of Galbeed refugee camp, estimates that 6,000 might have died in Kismayo’s network of displacement sites since the Trump administration abruptly shut USAID last year. Overnight, two children had died in makeshift shelters from diarrhoea.

El Niño will “deepen [Somalia’s] hunger levels”, warns the UN-backed body responsible for identifying famine.

Zakaria Mohammed, head nurse at Kismayo’s ICRC-supported hospital for five years, says children from al-Shabaab territory arrive every day so severely malnourished or weak they cannot be saved. Cases of child malnutrition rates have risen 40% this year.

Xaawo Nacdoor made it just in time. Al-Shabaab had blocked her leaving Jamame to receive treatment for her baby. But her 14-month-old daughter Faadumo was dying, so Nacdoor fled at night.

For three days, with just a bottle of water, Nacdoor walked with her daughter strapped to her back. On the second day, the toddler lost consciousness.

“She was severely dehydrated, but I kept going.” Arriving at the hospital, Faadumo weighed 4.1kg. Three days later: 4.4kg. She survived.

Most patients here vow never to return home, saying life under al-Shabaab meant no school, education, healthcare or food. For the thousands of families straddling the Juba River – often the poorest – things are about to get much worse.

#15. Why is a giant hand appearing on X? Heres what users are seeing.

X would like you to pay to promote your posts. And if you've managed to ignore the button offering that option, a giant hand may soon point it out for you.

Users across the Elon Musk-owned social media platform have been sharing screenshots of a hand appearing while they compose posts, directing their attention toward the Promote button. The animation is hard to miss, which appears to be the point.

"I just went to write a tweet and a massive hand popped up pointing to the promote button," The Verge’s Tom Warren wrote on X on Oct. 26.

The button offers paid promotion, allowing users to spend money to get their posts in front of more people. X already offers tools for promoting posts without setting up an advertising campaign.

The hand is a particularly literal way of drawing attention to them. And it’s certainly getting noticed.

"Jumpscared the shit out of me," wrote one user. Another wrote, "I thought I’d been hacked!" describing his rather seasonal experience: "I wish I could explain how jarring this hand and animation was in the X app. It was truly shocking, almost like a Halloween gag, but I'm pretty sure it was real. I guess I see the promote button now."

Others were less concerned with the hand's appearance than with where it was pointing. "The fact that it’s pointing at promote and not post is the real comedy here," wrote one user. Another account described the experience as getting "physically assaulted by the algorithm into buying ads." One put it plainly: "This should somehow be illegal."

One startled user tagged @X and @grok to ask, "What is that hand? Is this a Halloween thing?" Grok replied, describing it as a new prompt encouraging users to pay for extra reach, adding that it was "Not confirmed as a Halloween prank, though the timing makes it feel like one."

That hand is a new X compose-screen nudge. A realistic (sometimes animated) arm pops up while drafting to point at the Promote button, encouraging you to boost the post for extra reach. It started rolling out in the last day or so and has jumped out at lots of people. Not confirmed as a Halloween prank, though the timing makes it feel like one. - Grok

One iOS user said the hand was more than a static graphic. "It’s animated, and you can move it around," they wrote, adding, "Hopefully it’s just a one-time popup."

TBPN president Dylan Abruscato also shared his surprise at an arm appearing just as he was about to post. He called it the "most Nikita-coded thing I’ve ever seen," referring to the growth tactics associated with X's former product chief, Nikita Bier.

"So obstructive, yet so effective," Abruscato added.

The hand’s appearance coincides with a leadership change at X. Peter Sellis, a former product executive at Snapchat and Discord, announced on Oct. 26 that he was joining the company to lead product. (He hasn’t said whether he had a hand in this particular update. Get it?)

It’s also unclear how widely the hand is appearing. One Android user said they hadn’t encountered it. Another user said it disappeared before they could capture it.

For now, the hand has succeeded in getting people to post about the Promote button. Getting them to actually pay to use it might require a different, more subtle approach.

#14. Frontier AI labs say their models refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis. What does that mean?

OpenAI released solutions to three blockbuster math problems that have long vexed researchers. One was a surprise rejection of the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), a durable assumption in computer science that plays a large role in how we understand what is difficult to compute.

As with previous proof-drops from the frontier lab, scientific communities responded with a mixture of thrill and dismay. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge suggested that OpenAI was basing its solutions on pre-existing work, and hours later, Anthropic subsequently released its own refutation of ETH.

However, OpenAI’s process is evolving. This time, the lab structured its releases differently for each of the three disciplines involved: Mathematicians got the work dropped on GitHub; the computer scientists formed a working group, asked for the proofs to be shared publicly and OpenAI to fund a conference to discuss them; the physicists formed a working group that will determine how their result will be released.

MIT professor Ryan Williams is the computer scientist who is organizing his field’s response to the new finding. Williams, whose work focuses on these questions of computational complexity, has criticized OpenAI’s approach in the past, calling its proof dump earlier this October a “DDOS attack on mathematicians.”

However, he agreed to collaborate with the frontier labs in order to help shepherd ideas that are vital to the field, he told TechCrunch. The lab asked him to help interpret the solution, along with UC San Diego computer scientists Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, who first published the ETH in 1999.

Their first step was to release an open letter calling for feedback from scientists interested in these problems and a discussion of the norms around AI contributions to their work.

“Some parts [of OpenAI’s proof] are, maybe you could say routine, but then yes, there are two or three alien-looking parts, and it’s exactly these parts that I saw that made me think, wow, this is something that the whole community needs to work on together,” Williams told TechCrunch.

The ETH is part of a broader class of problems known as P versus NP, which has animated computer science and many other fields for decades. Reductively, researchers are trying to understand if any problem whose solution can be easily verified (NP) can also be easily solved (P).

A simple example is the game Sodoku: Once a solution to a puzzle is in place, it can be quickly verified, but finding the solution is time-consuming. That dynamic is true across a range of important problems, and the insight underpins cryptographic systems: When you have the key to an encoded message, you can reveal it, but actually cracking an effective code takes too long to be useful.

However, scientists have yet to prove whether that is always true. “P = NP is saying that, not only can you verify Sudoku solutions when they exist, you can just generate them,” Williams said. “P = NP is asserting that this is true for every single problem you can verify quickly — if you can verify it quickly, you can solve it quickly. So this just seems too good to be true.”

That’s why most scientists assume that P ≠ NP. ETH provided evidence for that. It basically says that even the fastest computer algorithm needs exponentially more time to find a solution as the number of variables grow in a specific class of tricky logic puzzle.

The claims from OpenAI and Anthropic is that their models have come up with algorithms that solve those puzzles in “sub-exponential” time, meaning faster than the speeds previously thought possible. If true, they may have proven the EFT to be false — refuted it, in scientific terms — and maybe opened up a path for algorithms that generally perform P = NP.

“Every computer scientist should probably put a little more weight on the plausibility of P = NP, but one’s weight may vary,” Williams said. “I do think that it kind of calls into question a lot of the intuitions that we thought we had about … how tractable [hard NP problems] are. I also think that this is probably not the final answer either. I don’t think that OpenAI’s algorithm could possibly be the best algorithm you can get.”

The durability of ETH has lead to it being included in many scientific papers over the past decade that will need to be revisited if the AI labs claims that they’ve proven it wrong holds up, Williams said.

“This is partly why I was asking or pushing OpenAI to support a conference on this — it’s going to affect many researchers’ works, and we need to figure out, okay, what happens next besides understanding the algorithms. We need to understand, how should the foundations change compared to what we had before?” he said.Before scientific concerns, there are cybersecurity questions. Williams said that an improved algorithm could imply that cryptographic systems relying on certain key sizes could be considered broken. So one of the first questions he asked OpenAI science lead Dan Roberts “was if they had checked that this affects any crypto systems … is anything insecure now? And apparently, they checked, and they were reasonably confident that this didn’t … imply any breaks.”

This new AI paradigm of scientific discovery is still a work in progress, but Williams says he is optimistic despite real concerns, particularly about training the next generation of scientists. Being a computer scientist who does high level math has perhaps made the adjustment easier for him than for pure mathematicians.

Still, he says AI is “forcing people to be a little more ambitious about the kind of work that they do, and to think more deeply about, okay, if all the things I used to work on are now low-hanging fruit for AI, how can I raise the bar for the kind of scientific questions I want to ask?”

#10. Flavio Bolsonaro flees Brazil for US amid investigation — reports
