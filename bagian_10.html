TL;DR: Solana DvP offers an open-source route to atomic securities settlement, with JPMorgan providing input on institutional requirements.

When a security and the money paying for it move in separate steps, one party can be exposed while waiting for the other. Solana’s newly announced delivery-versus-payment programme is designed to make those two movements part of one atomic blockchain transaction.

Released by the Solana Foundation on October 6 under the MIT licence, the tool offers isolated escrow, time limits and support for SPL Token and Token-2022 assets. It aims to provide a reusable standard for banks and other institutions rather than forcing each project to build a bespoke settlement contract.

JPMorgan provided feedback on settlement practices. That should not be described as the bank operating, certifying or guaranteeing the software: the Foundation’s own disclaimer explicitly limits the nature of its involvement. The programme has undergone external security reviews, while broader production adoption and planned privacy features remain matters for future implementation.

The opportunity is significant for tokenised securities, where the time between asset delivery and payment can create capital and counterparty costs. Whether the open-source model wins institutions over will depend on legal finality, custody integrations and how errors are handled beyond the smart contract. Fast code alone cannot replace the rest of settlement infrastructure.

#5077. Solana’s Firedancer Now Charges 1 SOL Per Bug Report

Solana’s Firedancer team now requires researchers to burn 1 SOL per bug report to combat AI-generated submissions flooding its security program.

The team behind Solana’s Firedancer software has introduced a paywall for bug hunters. Researchers must now burn 1 SOL, roughly $100, with every submission. The goal is to cut down on AI-generated spam and give engineers more time to investigate real security flaws.

Under terms updated on Monday, the burned tokens are permanently removed from circulation. The cost is nonrefundable whether a report turns out to be invalid, outside the program’s scope, or a duplicate. Reports submitted without a burn are not eligible for rewards.

Jump Crypto built Firedancer as an alternative to Agave, the main software validators use to check and process Solana transactions. A serious bug in this software can stop validators from processing payments or cause them to disagree about account balances. That could disrupt trades and transfers across the network. Every hour spent sorting through duplicate or unworkable reports is an hour not spent fixing vulnerabilities like these.

The decision follows a $1 million audit competition that ran from April 9 to May 9. It attracted 809 reports from 162 researchers, yet produced only 127 unique security findings once non-exploitable observations were excluded. Nearly half of the valid submissions were duplicates.

Jump believes AI agents helped find or write almost every submission. In an Oct. 16 review, Jump’s Ishan Bhatt argued that AI has made finding bugs cheap while judging them remains expensive. Engineers still had to reproduce each problem and check whether the proposed attack could actually work. Some reports flagged apparent weaknesses while overlooking protections elsewhere in the code.

The review backlog peaked at 299 open reports. As checks slowed, other researchers rediscovered bugs that had been submitted but not yet fixed or disclosed. About 80% of duplicates arrived more than three days after the original report.

The contest had already charged $100 per submission and capped researchers at one report a day. Jump says charging a fee discourages people from sending large volumes of speculative findings. The new platform also limits each main report section to 3,000 characters.

Alongside the new fee, Jump is raising payouts. Eligible findings receive double the standard reward through Nov. 20, and triple for bugs specific to Alpenglow, Solana’s new voting system. Critical Alpenglow findings can qualify for up to $300,000.

The move shows how AI is changing security work in crypto. Automated tools can generate reports faster than humans can review them, so teams are now using economic costs to filter the noise.

This much-awaited approval has brought Ripple’s proposed trust bank under federal supervision. At the same time, it has added another layer of regulatory oversight to its operations. This follows the OCC’s conditional approval in December 2025.

Remarking on the same, Ripple’s CEO Brad Garlinghouse said,

Now as RNTB has gained access to the playground, its key responsibilities include managing the segregated reserves backing Ripple USD (RLUSD) and acting as collateral trustee for RLUSD holders.

At the same time, it’ll also be responsible for providing fiduciary digital asset custody services that would allow institutions to hold digital assets through a federally supervised entity.

As a result, this could help Ripple serve businesses that require regulated infrastructure for payments, trading, and treasury management. However, in no sense does the charter automatically make RNTB a full-service commercial bank or authorize it to offer every banking service.

In fact, despite the OOC grant, RLUSD will continue to be issued by Standard Custody & Trust Company (SCTC), a limited-purpose trust company regulated by the New York Department of Financial Services (NYDFS).

In other words, RLUSD now benefits from an additional layer of federal oversight through the trust bank structure.

#5048. OCC Lets Crypto Firms Keep Paying Stablecoin Rewards

The OCC’s new GENIUS Act rules let exchanges like Coinbase and Robinhood keep paying interest-like stablecoin rewards, despite warnings from banks about deposit flight.

Crypto companies can keep paying interest-like rewards to customers, according to federal bank regulators. The ruling arrives despite warnings from banks that such programs could pull trillions of dollars out of the banking system.

The decision is part of the rollout of the GENIUS Act, which was signed into law last year to set rules for stablecoin issuers. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) published the new rules on Wednesday.

Under the OCC’s framework, stablecoin issuers like Circle remain barred from paying interest to people who hold their coins. Exchanges that trade and hold those stablecoins, including Coinbase and Robinhood, can pass earnings from the coins on to customers as interest-like rewards. Regulators said exchanges can do so on a case-by-case basis. Banks had wanted the practice banned outright.

Stablecoins are crypto tokens designed to hold a steady value, typically $1 per coin. They are backed by bank deposits and U.S. Treasuries, which earn interest, and that interest sits at the center of the fight between banks and crypto firms. The industry wants to turn the income from those reserves into a yield for customers.

Banking groups have pushed for tighter limits because crypto firms often offer higher yields than traditional savings accounts. Coinbase, for example, offers up to 3.5% on USDC balances. The FDIC’s latest figures put the average savings account yield at 0.38%.

If stablecoins and exchanges outcompete banks on yield, customers could shift their savings into crypto, a trend known as deposit flight. The banking industry argues this would reduce banks’ ability to lend and, in a worst-case scenario, cause some to fail. The same issue sparked intense debate when lawmakers drafted the CLARITY Act.

The American Bankers Association said the decision threatens the funds banks use to make loans that drive the U.S. economy. It argued the rule preserves the status quo for crypto platforms despite Congress’s clear intent to protect local lending.

Consumer advocates have joined banks on this issue, which is unusual. They worry that customers will see crypto rewards as equivalent to bank account interest, even though the crypto world has no equivalent of FDIC deposit insurance. Phillip Basil of Better Markets said the OCC has handed stablecoin issuers a free pass to do the work of banks without the protections that keep the banking system safe.

India’s CoinDCX Confirms $16 Million Loss Days After CEO Called It a ‘False Alarm’

Indian crypto exchange CoinDCX has admitted it lost about $16 million in an attack on its wallet infrastructure, reversing its co-founder’s earlier insistence that reports of a breach were a “false alarm.”

In a blog post, the Mumbai-based exchange said 15.99 million USDT was drained and that it has absorbed the loss itself, stressing that customer funds remain safe.

The money moved on the Tron network on Oct. 21, and the company traced the incident to a failure in an internal process meant to sweep customer deposits out of wallets it had retired after a far larger hack last year.

The disclosure caps an awkward few days for the exchange. When blockchain researchers and the Arkham tracker first flagged roughly $16 million in suspicious outflows, CEO Sumit Gupta pushed back on X, writing that CoinDCX’s wallets were “not breached” and that the flagged addresses had been compromised back in July 2025, making the activity look like “a false alarm.”

On-chain investigator ZachXBT disputed that account, noting the wallet in question had still been sweeping user deposits into CoinDCX’s current hot wallet as recently as April and May, and he urged customers to withdraw their assets.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission and the National Basketball Association have agreed to put in place standards to protect the integrity of the game amid concerns of insider trading and manipulation in prediction markets.

The CFTC and NBA signed a memorandum of understanding to share information and open lines of communication about maintaining prediction markets while preserving integrity, according to a statement from the CFTC. Both Kalshi and Polymarket will be the league's "preferred prediction market exchanges," the NBA said in a statement.

"As this category develops, we believe it is important to work constructively with regulators and operators to help ensure that appropriate safeguards are in place," said Scott Kaufman-Ross, NBA head of media distribution & partnerships, in a statement. "These partnerships represent an important step in establishing a framework that creates new opportunities to engage our fans."

The NBA marks the third sports league to sign an MOU with the CFTC. Earlier this year, the National Hockey League and Major League Baseball signed similar agreements. The National Football League has not teamed up with the CFTC and has pressed the agency for tougher rules on game integrity, consumer safety and insider trading controls.

The NFL has also diverged from the CFTC over when the federal or state government should regulate prediction markets. Who should regulate those billions of dollars worth of bets has become a major point of contention. The CFTC has said that most contracts on prediction markets are swaps and so fall under its "exclusive jurisdiction." However, states have argued that prediction market companies are operating illegally, violating their gaming laws and avoiding paying taxes.

The fight could potentially make its way up to the U.S. Supreme Court after New Jersey asked the high court to take up the issue. Earlier this month, the NFL filed an amicus brief with the U.S. Supreme Court and said that sports contracts are gambling, so they should be overseen by states.

Insider trading has also been a repeated issue in prediction markets. An active-duty U.S. Army soldier was charged in April for using confidential information to place bets on Polymarket ahead of former Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro's capture earlier this year. In another case, a former White House teleprompter agreed to pay more than $172,000 to settle charges that he used his advance access to President Trump's speeches to profit on Kalshi.

Kalshi and Polymarket have both said they have taken certain steps, including mandatory employment verification for traders in sensitive markets to deter insider trading.
