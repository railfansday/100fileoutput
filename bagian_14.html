Battlefield Studios announced a host of new changes that will be included in the same patch, which are apparently significant enough to require player testing. The developer is reviving Battlefield Labs to help it do so, but only for three two-hour sessions, split across the game’s three main server regions (EU, NA, APAC).

All tests are scheduled for today, December 2, and they remain under NDA. Battlefield Labs is also sticking with PC as its chosen platform yet again, though you will be able to join through Steam this time, alongside the EA app.

First, there’s footstep audio, an issue that was really brought into focus with the launch of Redsec Battle Royale. You can expect to hear enemy footsteps from farther away, and intuitively know the type of surface they’re moving on.

The patch also brings another pass to hit registration and netcode, which BF Studios says should improve responsiveness in close-quarter situations. Finally, there are broader changes to other combat areas that the announcement didn’t elaborate on.

All testing will take place on Eastwood, and Operation Firestorm, rotating between Conquest, and Escalation.

Going forward, Battlefield Studios said to expect Labs to be open to more players, and for the process itself to be smoother. The developer wants to have faster turnaround times, after all, so that the initiative can test smaller changes and be more responsive to player feedback.

Some of the more involved, future sessions will include experiments with map layout changes and various other non-standard compositions of player and vehicle numbers. Of course, you can also expect new maps and modes to be tested in Labs, similar to how the CTE was used in Battlefield 4’s long life.

#4719. Toyota Recalls 106K Hybrid Land Cruisers for Possible Fuel Leak

The issue stems from a potentially faulty fuel-line connection, which could waste gas and increase the risk of a fire.

Gas is expensive these days, and filling up your SUV will cost you even more if it’s leaking fuel. That’s a potential concern for nearly 106K Land Cruiser owners, whose vehicles are involved in a new recall that Toyota has reported to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA).

The recall documents state that 105,794 hybrid Land Cruisers have a plastic fuel tube on the gas tank that connects to a metal fuel tube that sends the combustible liquid to the turbocharged four-cylinder engine. However, if the retaining clip that holds these two parts together is lifted, it can cause the fuel lines to disconnect and cause a leak. Not only will such a leak lead to wasted fuel and the gas engine eventually shutting off, but it also increases the risk of a fire.

#4717. The Mercedes-AMG GLS63 is a Luxury Three-Row Crossed with a Sports Car

Behind a little-changed design is an all-new flat-plane twin-turbo V-8 and impressive dynamic focus.

At first glance, the new Mercedes-Benz GLS Class looks like a typical midcycle update: new lights, grille, wheel designs; a little nip and tuck to keep things fresh. However, some 3000 components are new or revised, more than 40 percent of the pieces that go into this 17-foot long, 6100-pound SUV.

Consider the V-8 in the AMG GLS 63. It’s still a twin-turbocharged 4.0-liter making 603 hp and 627 lb-ft of torque, the same peak figures as last year. Nothing new there, right? Well, not much beyond the block, the heads, the turbos, the camshafts, and the hybrid system’s electric motor. Not only is the crankshaft new, it’s an entirely new concept—new, at least, to the GLS. Like decades’ worth of high-winding Ferrari V-8s and the LT6 in the range-topping C8 Corvettes, Mercedes’s M177 Evo uses a flat-plane crank.

When they massaged the M177 V-8 for the last-generation AMG GT Black Series, Mercedes engineers were surprised to find it developed more torque more quickly with a flat-plane crankshaft than the cross-plane piece it was originally designed with. Not only that, but alternating power strokes evenly between the cylinder banks—rather than the necessary sequencing of adjacent cylinders in a cross-plane V-8—improved combustion efficiency and emissions. With more stringent tailpipe regulations coming into effect for Europe and China in 2027, the engineering team took note.

Flat-plane cranks are notorious for two things: shrieking exhaust notes and intense vibration. Neither would do in a luxury SUV. (It’s not just the AMG that gets the new V-8; a detuned version of the M177 Evo also powers the statelier GLS 580.) Quelling the vibrations necessitated the new engine block. A pair of balance shafts nestled low alongside the crank cancel out indecent tremors.

Balance shafts are far from novel. Mercedes’s solution for preserving the deep exhaust rumble its V-8s are known for, however, is rather innovative—innovative, but elegantly simple and refreshingly analog. Rather than cherry-picking frequencies to amplify and suppress or just playing the sound they want through the standard Burmester sound system’s 15 speakers, they turned metal. The lobes on the exhaust cams are not oriented identically for every cylinder. On the left bank, the front two cylinders open their exhaust valves 15 degrees of crank rotation earlier than the back two cylinders. On the right bank, the difference is 20 degrees of crank rotation over the front two cylinders. Powertrain development engineer Sebastian Schlegel says, “Maybe we lose half a horsepower, but that does not matter.”

This development was followed by a sort of Great German Bake-Off of different exhaust-pipe crossovers, X- and H-pipes in different locations and diameters, until the team had a flat-crank engine that sounds an awful lot like its cross-plane predecessor. It also feels an awful lot like its predecessor, with omnipresent torque and tremendous pull to the top of the tach—which, considered against other flat-crank V-8s, is a comparatively normal 6800 rpm.

This sounds like a lot of work for no gain, but perhaps landing precisely back on the M177’s output figures while futureproofing the engine against stricter emissions regulations is a point of pride for the development team. The new V-8’s heads have reoriented intake ports to facilitate what the engineers dubbed the “super high-tumble Miller cycle.” Engine nerds will note that the Miller combustion cycle’s late intake-valve closing improves thermal efficiency at the expense of horsepower. AMG cleverly compensates for this with positively heroic amounts of boost: 47.8 psi from the new turbochargers. Schlegel says that, four years ago when they first approached turbocharger suppliers, the response was, “What the hell are they doing?” He adds that the solution was to make the turbos out of a high-nickel and high-chrome alloy more commonly found in turbine engines. So, don’t blow them up. They’re probably expensive. Twin-scroll turbines and precise volute shaping allow for such monstrous boost without commensurate lag.

While the powertrain team focused on familiarity, AMG’s chassis crew sought new frontiers in engagement. With the GLS 63’s standard adjustable-height air springs, adaptive dampers, and 48-volt active anti-roll bars, they had plenty of variables to play with. They nailed it. Previously, the AMG GLS was somewhat remote, with numb steering and general disconnect between the road and the driver. The 2027 model drives like a sports car with running boards. Steering weight builds and relaxes faithfully with cornering loads, and the brake pedal is firm and progressive. The active anti-roll bars are unfazed by the AMG GLS’s six-foot height, keeping this beast of burden—7700-pound towing capacity when properly equipped—remarkably flat as the steering wheel demands more effort. And as you power out of corners, the torque-vectoring rear differential puts power where it needs to be to maintain your line.

According to the onboard accelerometer that appears on the central touchscreen in Sport mode, the GLS touched 1.0 g of lateral acceleration in several corners. No doubt that’s a peak figure, rather than sustained, but it’s still remarkable for a three-ton SUV with seats for seven. These feats take an awful lot of Michelin Pilot Sport 4 to perform, 275/50-21s up front and 315/45-21s out back. For you off-roaders, those are 32s. For the Ferrari fans in the crowd, those are the same widths as the rubber on the 12Cilindri, just with another inch and a half or so of sidewall keeping the ride comfortable even with every adjustable chassis bit set to maximum stiffness.

Because even the 603-hp AMG version is still a luxury SUV first, the GLS is equally satisfying with all systems dialed back to Comfort. The steering lightens, the dampers soften, the exhaust is muted, and the atmosphere inside the cabin settles into serenity. Also, the supremely supportive front seats include standard heating, ventilation, and massage. Heated second-row seats are also standard. Ventilated and massaging outboard middle-row seats are available, as is a heated third row. A trio of 12.3-inch displays Mercedes dubs Superscreen is also standard, and those who need still more pixels can get twin 11.6-inch screens with wireless headphones for the second-row passengers.

Mercedes also overhauled the GLS’s suite of safety tech. The brand has lagged notably behind many competitors in advanced driving aids, skipping broader availability of Level 2 hands-free eyes-on autonomy like GM’s Super Cruise and Ford’s BlueCruise in favor of its Level 3 Drive Pilot tech, currently only available in California and Nevada. That will change in the next year. Every 2027 GLS leaves the factory equipped with 10 exterior cameras, five radar sensors, and 12 ultrasonic modules. When the new Level 2 system is finalized in the next year, Mercedes will push an over-the-air update to the vehicles, and owners will wake up to a semi-autonomous Benz in their driveway. Given the tech team’s current verbosity—the self-parking system’s legal name is Digital Extra: MB.Drive Parking Assist 360—expect branding approximately as succinct as a pedigree cat name. Lady Godelieve Georgina Whillikers Whiskers, Marchioness of Lower Fenton, I presume?

Fit for human nobility with a passel of little lords and ladies but also more fun to drive than many modern sports sedans, the Mercedes-AMG GLS 63 is a magnum opus—emphasis on the magnum.

#4715. Michael Strahan Shares the Words That Helped His Daughter Through Brain Cancer Treatment

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

For years, Michael Strahan appeared in commercials for St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, delivering a message he never imagined would become so personal: “No parent should have to hear that their child has cancer.”

But in 2023, the beloved TV host and former NFL player faced that reality when his daughter, Isabella, was diagnosed with brain cancer at 19 years old.

“[That] line that I used to deliver, usually at the end of the commercial…here I am actually filling that line in my life in real time,” Strahan told Healthline.

Strahan believes his longtime connection with St. Jude was more than a coincidence. After years of advocating for families affected by childhood cancer, he found himself on the other side of that experience.

“When I needed that family…they were there for me,” he said. “I’m just appreciative, and I think things come back to you; give out good, good comes back.”

In October 2023, Isabella began experiencing vomiting, headaches, and balance issues during her freshman year at the University of Southern California (USC). Tests revealed that she had medulloblastoma, a fast-growing cancerous brain tumor located in the cerebellum. She underwent emergency brain surgery, radiation therapy, and four rounds of chemotherapy.

While medulloblastoma typically occurs in children between the ages of 3 and 9, it can also affect young adults like Isabella.

“[The] biology in adolescents and young adults can differ meaningfully from pediatric cases, requiring specialized expertise at centers of excellence for this disease,” Arnab Chakravarti, MD, director of the Brain Tumor Program at The Ohio State University, told Healthline.

Medulloblastoma is sometimes confused with glioblastoma, the most common type of malignant primary brain tumor in adults. Glioblastoma is particularly aggressive, with a median overall survival of just 15 months and a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%.

However, Chakravarti explained that medulloblastoma has a significantly higher cure rate than glioblastoma.

“[This is] a point of genuine hope that deserves to be communicated clearly,” he said.

Radiation therapy is a cornerstone of medulloblastoma treatment. Isabella received proton therapy, which uses precisely targeted radiation to treat tumors while limiting exposure to surrounding healthy tissue.

“For medulloblastoma, this physical precision is transformative,” Chakravarti said. “Proton craniospinal irradiation dramatically reduces [collateral] exposure — preserving cochlear function and hearing, reducing cardiovascular risk, protecting the thyroid, and most critically, sparing developing brain tissue in ways that translate into measurably better neurocognitive outcomes years after treatment.”

He added that multiple prospective studies have shown that children and young adults treated with proton craniospinal irradiation experience less cognitive decline and better quality of life compared to those treated with conventional radiation.

Today, Isabella is back at USC, embracing the everyday routines she once had to put on hold.

“I am back at USC, so…it’s been great, in the midst of exams, so it’s a little stressful, but I actually [am] inviting the stress in because at one point I didn’t have exams, so I’m happy to be back,” Isabella told Healthline.

Along with attending classes, Isabella is enjoying activities such as working out and cooking. She continues to undergo MRI scans of her head and spine every three months, but said that after another year, the scans will become less frequent, occurring every six months.

“But really…I don’t have a lot of doctor’s appointments,” she said.

“It feels like a brand-new life, but it’s a good one. And that’s wonderful,” Isabella said.

Isabella said her family’s support played an important role in helping her through cancer treatment. Spending so much time together in the hospital brought them closer as they watched movies and played cards and board games to pass the time.

“[It] really, really bonds you to people, and just having my dad and my whole family there was something that [you] can’t beat,” she told Healthline.

“They say you spend the most time with your kid for the first few years of their life, and then once school starts, you don’t get a chance to spend as much time as you would like with them,” he said.

#4713. Psychologist found the first corporate psychopath—a 17th century Dutch mutineer

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:48 PM EDT

The concept of a corporate psychopath is eerily recognizable, depending on your work history. It’s also notoriously misunderstood. The archetype of the manipulative, self-serving manager has regularly resurfaced in popular culture for decades, but concrete evidence supporting the wider concept is debatable. For example, a research study went viral in 2016 after estimating upwards of 1-in-5 business employees fit the “psychopath” bill, only to be retracted two years later. Additionally, psychiatrists widely agree that only about 1 percent of the general population truly can be considered clinically psychopathic.

That said, many of the people determined to claw their way to the top of the company ladder display at least some of the trademark psychopathic behaviors. Many theorists have convincingly argued this has a lot to do with corporate capitalism’s foundational principles, which leave it easily exploited by the most ruthless and manipulative among us.

Clive Boddy, one of the world’s foremost investigators of corporate psychopathy, recently took this idea to its logical historical conclusion. In a study recently published in the Asian Journal of Business Ethics, Boddy reviewed one of toxic corporate leadership’s earliest documented examples—and he makes today’s nightmare coworkers sound tame in comparison.

Meet Jeronimus Cornelisz, an all-time contender for World’s Worst Boss. According to archival accounts, the 17th century Dutchman Cornelisz embodied every core corporate psychopath characteristic over 400 years ago while working for the conglomerate that started it all: the Dutch East India Company.

Established in Amsterdam in 1602, the Dutch East India Company is arguably the world’s first multinational, shareholder-owned company. It quickly amassed enough funds and power to rival most sovereign countries through the import and export of spices, tea, luxury items, coffee, wine, and enslaved populations. The Dutch East India Company eventually exerted enough influence to wage its own wars, mint its own currency, form colonies, as well as arrest and execute convicts.

All of this was made possible through a vast fleet of ships,but even the best outfitted vessels sometimes fell afoul of nature. In June 1629, a Dutch East India Company’s flagship called Batavia collided with a reef off the Western Australian coast. Nearly 300 survivors made it to the nearby island shores, but their remote location ensured they wouldn’t survive without help. The ship’s senior officers soon set off in an open longboat on a 1,900-mile journey to present-day Jakarta, then the Dutch East Indies’ capital. Before leaving, they appointed under-merchant Jeronimus Cornelisz to the top corporate position in their absence.

Unbeknownst to Batavia’s departing senior crew, Cornelisz had already spent weeks scheming on ways to incite a mutiny. The plan was to take control of the vessel then steer its copious stores of gold and silver to a new life. Cornelisz apparently viewed getting marooned as an inconvenient detour, and quickly got to work on his hostile takeover.

According to firsthand accounts, Cornelisz immediately consolidated power by swapping fellow council members with loyalists and requiring them to pledge an oath of allegiance to him. Next, he tricked suspected opponents under the direction of a soldier named Wiebbe Hayes to search a nearby island for supplies, then left them to die.

Although his revised plan required any impending rescue ship, Cornelisz’s ultimate goal remained the same—get as far away as possible, then start his own kingdom using riches plundered from Batavia. Over the ensuing months, Cornelisz directed his gang to kill more than 100 of the shipwrecked men, women, and children. Cornelisz often justified the murders under the pretense of conserving food and water, when in actuality he was continuing to eliminate perceived threats. Meanwhile, some of the surviving women were subjected to sexual abuse.

However, Cornelisz underestimated his abandoned compatriots. As the operatically psychopathic Cornelisz terrorized his group of survivors and scrounged for supplies, Hayes’ team reportedly discovered freshwater wells and hunted local wallabies, seals, and fish. The two sides subsequently squared off on multiple occasions after learning of each others’ continued existence until a rescue ship finally arrived in October 1629. After nearly three months, only about a third of Batavia’s original passengers and crew were left.

As for Cornelisz and his closest loyalists, authorities eventually hanged them, but not before cutting off their hands.

“Perhaps the most striking finding is that another leader facing the same circumstances achieved completely different outcomes,” Boddy said. “This was effectively a natural experiment in leadership. One group prospered while the other descended into murder and abuse.”

#4706. Hormone Therapy May Help Improve Menopause Brain Fog, Mood Changes

  • Hormone therapy may affect changes in the brain that occur during menopause. 
  • A recent study has shown that hormone therapy may help improve “brain fog” symptoms during menopause.
  • Another study found that hormone therapy may improve mood changes that are often associated with menopause. 
  • Other research has alleviated long-standing concerns over a possible link between menopause hormone therapy and dementia. 

Menopause hormone therapy (MHT), also known as hormone replacement therapy (HRT), helps manage various menopause symptoms. 

Currently, MHT is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to help relieve hot flashes, night sweats, vaginal dryness, and dyspareunia (pain during sex). 

However, recent research has found that MHT may also help improve mental symptoms like “brain fog” and mood changes. 

Other research also found that MHT does not affect dementia risk. This research followed the FDA’s removal of the black box warning on these products, which suggested they may increase the risk.  

Together, these studies show that MHT may help people experiencing menopause in ways not previously realized and that it is generally safe to use. 

Hormone therapy may ease  menopause ‘brain fog’ 

Research published in Scientific Reports on September 1 suggests that an estrogen hormone treatment that combines estriol and progesterone may help ease the symptoms of brain fog during menopause. 

Brain fog may involve memory lapses, difficulty concentrating, and slower thinking. 

Just about two-thirds of women experience cognitive difficulties, like brain fog, during the menopausal transition. However, there is currently no Food and Drug Administration (FDA)- approved treatment for these symptoms. 

This study was an observational case series that followed 20 menopausal women with an average age of 53. Each participant was given a baseline assessment for cognitive symptoms prior to treatment. 

They were then treated for 12 months with a customized regimen of estriol and progesterone.

Estriol is a natural form of estrogen that occurs during pregnancy and has been used in Europe and Asia to treat other symptoms of menopause. 

After 12 months, the participants reported significant reductions in: 

  • brain fog
  • concentration 
  • working memory 
  • verbal memory
  • processing speed
  • problem solving

“Estrogen plays a well-documented role in protecting the brain, yet there are still no approved type and dose of treatment that specifically targets the cognitive symptoms so many women experience during menopause,” Rhonda Voskuhl, MD, neurologist, member of the Comprehensive Menopause Center at UCLA Health, and senior study author, said in a press release. 

“Women are often told to simply live with brain fog, when in fact there is a neuroscience basis for it, and potentially a way to address it. That gap in care is what motivated us to look more closely at estriol,” Voskuhl said. 

This was a small, preliminary case series without a placebo group or randomization. More research is needed to confirm the benefits for cognitive symptoms. 

Key points

  • Brain fog is a common symptom of menopause with no approved treatment. 
  • A recent study found that hormone therapy with estriol and progesterone may help improve cognitive symptoms during menopause. 
  • More research is needed to confirm the effects of hormone therapy on cognitive symptoms. 

Hormone therapy may help with menopause mood changes

A study published September 9 in Menopause, the journal of the Menopause Society, showed that hormone therapy may help improve mood changes in women in midlife. 

The researchers noted that mood disturbances, such as depression, irritability, anxiety, and mental exhaustion, affect up to 68% of women during menopause. 

“The erratic changes and loss of estrogen associated with this hormonal transition can lower serotonin and dopamine levels, which [are] associated with depression and cognitive changes, including poor concentration, brain fog, and memory loss,” said Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study. 

The study included 260 individuals with an average age of 52 years. Just over half of the participants had a history of anxiety or depression, and 24% were using an antidepressant medication at the start of the study. 

Each participant completed the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) both before and after initiating hormone therapy for FDA-approved indications, such as hot flashes and night sweats. 

The average follow-up during the study was 4.5 months. Between the baseline and the follow-up, there was a significant reduction in the MRS psychological scores. The percentage of participants with severe mood symptoms decreased from 62.3% to 24.6%. 

Treatment response didn’t appear to differ by age, menopausal stage, psychiatric history, or antidepressant use. These findings suggest that hormone therapy may help improve mood symptoms in midlife, especially for those with higher severity of symptoms before treatment. 

“This study supports the evidence that starting hormone therapy for the typical disruptive symptoms of menopause may bring collateral benefits, including improvement in mood,” Ross said. “Communicating with your healthcare provider is always best to manage your symptoms and prepare yourself properly for this next stage of life.” 

Key points

  • A recent study showed that hormone therapy may help improve mood changes associated with menopause. 
  • The biggest improvement was seen in those with more severe symptoms before beginning treatment. 
  • Improvement with hormone therapy was seen despite age, menopause stage, and psychiatric history. 

Menopause hormone therapy not linked to dementia

For decades, women have been warned about the serious potential risks associated with menopause hormone therapy (MHT). This included an FDA “black box” warning citing complications like cardiovascular disease, cancer, and dementia. 

However, a recent meta-analysis commissioned by the World Health Organization (WHO) that included data from more than a million women, and was published in The Lancet Healthy Longevity, found that MHT neither increased nor decreased dementia risk. 

“The World Health Organisation currently provides no guidance on MHT and dementia risk, leaving a critical gap for women, clinicians, and policymakers. Our work will help inform upcoming WHO guidelines on reducing the risk of cognitive decline and dementia,” Melissa Melville, a PhD candidate and aging researcher in the Department of Clinical Health Psychology at the University College London, and first author of the study, told Healthline in a previous interview. 

In the same interview, Melville stressed that the importance of this work is twofold: for clinicians, there is an urgent need for higher-quality research, and for patients, decisions about MHT should be guided by symptoms and well-established benefits and risks.

Key points

  • A new meta-analysis has helped to ease long-standing fears regarding the association between menopause hormone therapy and dementia. 
  • The analysis reviewed data from more than a million women and found that MHT neither raises nor decreases the risk of dementia. 
  • The researchers noted that decisions about MHT should be guided by symptoms and well-established benefits and risks.

#4680. Doctors Just Discovered a New Type of Depression

NEARLY ONE IN four cases of depression may fall into this category. A study published in the British Journal of Psychiatry identified “circadian depression,” and people with it have an internal 24-hour clock that’s out of sync with their external environment.

Like really out of sync. We’re not talking about jet lag. This is when your body’s internal timing network just can’t regulate your body’s systems.

It's important to get this out of the way upfront: Circadian depression isn't in the DSM-5—the “psychiatry bible” mental health professionals use to make diagnoses. In other words, it's not an official condition yet. But it’s important to know about. We had mental health experts break it down.

CIRCADIAN DEPRESSION IS a subtype of depression caused by circadian disruption, which is when your body’s circadian rhythm doesn't align with your external environment. People with circadian depression have disrupted sleep-wake cycles, lower levels of daytime activity, low energy, and weight gain during depression episodes, according to the study.

People who were classified in the study as having circadian depression were more likely to have severe symptoms of depression, including:

People with circadian depression also seem to be more likely than those with other forms of depression to have other conditions like ADHD, anxiety, bipolar disorder, obesity, or insulin resistance.

THE STUDY’S RESEARCHERS analyzed clinical and genetic data from more than 10,000 Australians with depression. To determine whether people had the proposed subtype, circadian depression, rather than general depression, they looked at factors like a person's social jetlag (the mismatch between their internal clock and external schedule), whether they were night owls, how tired they felt in the morning, and whether they experienced excessive daytime sleepiness. They also looked at whether people had poor responses to antidepressant treatment, and people with severe symptoms that were a mix of bipolar disorder, depression, and somatic disorders.

About 23 percent of participants fell into the category of circadian depression. This is more than a label. It’s important to recognize because, as the researchers pointed out, people with circadian depression tend to be less responsive to traditional antidepressant medications. That means treatment tailored to their subtype is especially important.

A FEW THINGS are happening here. First is people’s sleep schedules getting thrown off, possibly from chronic inflammation and a messed-up metabolism. There is prior evidence of a link between circadian rhythm and these two factors (though researchers are still on the fence on what causes what).

“The body’s internal clock helps coordinate changes in the brain throughout the day," says Hillary Ammon, PsyD, clinical psychologist at the Center for Anxiety and Women’s Emotional Wellness. That includes a feeling of alertness and motivation during conventional waking hours. When the timing is off, these coordinated changes are also affected.

“Increased tiredness, low motivation, or decreased focus can all be factors that lead to depression and also be traits of depression,” says Ammon. Having an internal clock that's off schedule also impacts your metabolism and stress hormone cortisol.

Increased cortisol release at night can lead to an increase in unease and difficulty sleeping. Changes in metabolism rates can cause weight gain. Weight gain and decreased activity are classic symptoms of depression.

The concept of circadian depression is not an entirely new idea, says Aaron P. Brinen, PsyD, assistant professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at Vanderbilt University Medical Center. “We've known that there is a link to sleep in a number of different psychiatric disorders, including depression,” he says. “When people are depressed, their sleep gets disrupted and vice-versa—when sleep is disrupted, you're more likely to get depressed.”

SINCE CIRCADIAN DEPRESSION was just discovered, there is no set treatment for it yet. But doctors who treat depression say the new discovery is a huge reminder that depression requires tailored treatments.

In the study, for example, the researchers found that people with circadian depression didn’t respond as well to standard antidepressants like SSRIs or SNRIs. In fact, people who meet the criteria for circadian depression might experience more side effects on these medications. Their suggestions? Treatments like light therapy and melatonin may reset the body's internal clock and ease symptoms.

Therapists spend a bit of time with the client anyway trying to pinpoint the drivers of their depression, says Brinen. “If it's being driven by sleep or sleep disruption, you're going to target that.”

The basics around mental health still stand. If you're having symptoms of depression, talk with a mental health professional about an evaluation and appropriate treatment. Plus, don’t be an armchair therapist and try to diagnose yourself. “I would be more mindful of the symptoms versus the diagnosis,” says Ammon.

Your provider can give you a full evaluation and recommend appropriate next steps tailored to your situation. “It may be that you need to be more thoughtful about sleep, travel, and work schedules,” Brinen says. It’s “just like when a person who is naturally hypoglycemic needs to carry snacks. You want to make sure you're prioritizing sleep.”

Because PMOS is a newer term, the researchers searched for studies using both PMOS and PCOS.

The Cornell Auction sale is officially scheduled for November 21, with an online preview and bidding slated for October 26. The Bellport, New York, auction house has not yet shared the full catalogue, but revealed in an Instagram video that “most of the items that we have in the Truman Capote auction were photographed almost exactly 50 years ago in 1976 in an Architectural Digest spread about Truman’s house here in Sagaponack on Long Island.”

It is virtually impossible to find his Long Island home in the Hamptons, but that’s exactly the way he wants it. Hidden behind scrub pine, privet hedges and rows of hydrangea bushes is Truman Capote’s two-story, weathered-gray beach house near Sagaponack on the South Fork.

He lives in the heart of the Hamptons—a stretch of rolling potato fields and lush farmlands married to the nearby Atlantic Ocean. A year-round farming community and a summer place for city people, it is here that antique farmhouses vie with modernistic glass houses for the dunes and fields. Mr. Capote once called Sagaponack “Kansas with a sea breeze.”

The unpretentious exterior of the flat-roofed, box-shaped studio beach house, surrounded by sun-baked trees, was weathered and timeless.

It has actually become a rural Montparnasse with the likes of James Jones, Willem de Kooning, Marya Mannes, Jean Stafford, Joseph Heller and many other artists and writers living in the area. It is also the kind of sleepy sentimental town where the local bar—Truman Capote often lunches here or has a drink with fellow writers John Knowles or Willie Morris—runs an ad in the biweekly newspaper wishing him happy birthday. The best way to find Mr. Capote’s secluded house is with him at the wheel of his deep-green Buick. Wildflowers are still blooming, and the smell of the potatoes, almost ready for harvest, is pungent on the salty Atlantic Ocean air.

The two-story living room communicated a mood of oceanside ease tinged with intelligence and humor. Highly personal objects, memorabilia and books—which included copies of his own works in several languages—were placed casually among the simple furnishings.

“Home is where I hang my hats,” he laughs. “However, I keep leaving them when I go visit people. I don’t have as many as I used to because I don’t recover them all. People always promise to send them back, but they don’t.”

#4665. Why volunteers are gluing seeds to clam shells in Connecticut

Published Oct 24, 2026 11:18 AM EDT

Environmental restoration sometimes requires out-of-the-box thinking. Along the waters of Long Island Sound in New York and Connecticut, experts are tackling the challenge of restoring crucial eelgrass habitats by gluing the plant’s seeds to clams.

Eelgrass (Zostera marina) is an important member of the ecosystem. It provides foraging areas and shelter for young fish and invertebrates, diminishes coastal erosion, draws in excess nutrients, filters water, and releases oxygen, for instance. Compared to the 1930s, eelgrass meadows in and around Long Island Sound have decreased by more than 90 percent, according to regional environmental action group Save the Sound.

To make matters worse, eelgrass is difficult to restore and there have been a lot of failed attempts, Bill Lucey, a Long Island Soundkeeper from the organization, tells Popular Science. In 2025, the team tried a different approach—recruiting clams as underwater gardeners. The strategy was originally ideated by Robert Vasiluth, the founder of Submerged Aquatic Vegetation EngineersEnvironmental (SAVE) .

The approach consists of attaching eelgrass seeds to the clam’s shells. When the clams are released into the sound, they bury into the sediment, taking the seeds down to the bottom and effectively planting them. When they conducted drone imagery to check in on their results this year, they found that the eelgrass meadow coverage had increased by 178 percent.

“It needs to survive for three years before we can really trumpet our horns and say it’s working, but the actual method of putting clams out and seeds attached to them really worked. The generation of the plants was incredible,” Lucey explains.

During their 2025 planting, the team used glue typically used in coral reef restoration to attach seeds to the clams. But then Connecticut College biologist Maria Rosa yielded better germination rates when she used wet pieces of rice paper (the kind you make spring rolls with) to seal the seeds against the shell. The Save the Sound team asked its volunteers to do the same earlier this week.

“The problem with the spring roll wrappers is yesterday [Monday, October 19], when we deployed them for the first time in scale, they weren’t all staying on,” says Lucey. “So what we’ve started doing today [Tuesday, October 20] is putting a little pocket of seeds, 10 to 20 eelgrass seeds on the top of the clam, putting a little circle of this [coral] glue around it, and then putting the spring roll wrapper stamp over that and wetting it. And so it keeps the seeds moist, and the glue holds the spring roll wrapper to the clam till it dissolves, and then it’ll release the seeds next to the clam.”

Save the Sound is aiming to release 10,000 claims and 300,000 seeds this season.

Adenocarcinoma is the most common subtype of lung cancer globally. It accounts for around 40% of all lung cancer diagnoses. 

Adenocarcinoma is a type of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which accounts for about 80–85% of all lung cancers. 

Lung adenocarcinoma begins in the epithelial cells that produce mucus. It is the most common type of NSCLC.

  • targeted therapy

#4658. 6 ways bats are surprisingly human

Published Oct 24, 2026 1:30 PM EDT

Bat Week officially begins today. It’s the one week where Earth’s only winged mammals get top billing. Often associated with disease, vampires, and other generally unpleasant things, bats are actually important pollinators and play critical roles in our ecosystem.

“They’re just so cool and so interesting. During first forays into bat studies, it was just one question after another,” Rachel Page, an evolutionary biologist at the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute in Panama, tells Popular Science. “The more I sort of scratched the surface, the more interesting it became—which is the fun part.”

One reason bats are so interesting to scientists like Page is that they are surprisingly similar to humans. Here’s how.

Sharing a meal with good company brings immense joy for us humans. For many vampire bat species (subfamily Desmodontinae), sharing food is a matter of survival. They survive exclusively on blood and can only go about two nights without a blood meal before they die. To get around this, they rely on their social networks.

“They have the equivalent of friends. Basically, if a bat comes back from a night of foraging and has not been successful, other members of that social group will share food,” Page explains. “They’ll regurgitate blood for the bat that came back hungry.”

If that donor bat has difficulty getting food later, the one recipient will often become the donor. “So there’s this very reciprocal relationship,” says Page.

According to Page, bats will have their distinctive personalities just like people. It’s present across species, but also individual bats as well.

“You have some species that are very feisty, and they’ll go out of their way to pick a fight, and then other ones that are much more passive, calm, and very gentle,” she says.

At the edge of Panama’s Soberanía National Park, tiny, fruit-eating bats will take over the roost of larger, carnivorous bats. The Seba’s short-tailed bat (Carollia perspicillata) weighs roughly the same as a strawberry. By comparison, the frog-eating, fringed-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) weighs roughly as much as an apricot and twice as much as the short-tailed bat.

Page and her colleagues in Panama have witnessed fights between the two species from roosts set up throughout the forest.

“Basically, it was the male Seba’s short-tailed bat who was instigating the fight. He was coming over and was kind of boxing and threatening much larger bats with wide open mouths and lots of teeth,” Page recalls. “And he just kept at it, and then finally, this group of fringed-lipped bats left.”

Even in the thick jungles of Panama, bats can find motionless insects on leaves. Initially, scientists believed that the thick tree cover would give small insects an “acoustic camouflage” that would confuse a bat’s echolocation system. But they were wrong.

The bats can see through this acoustic camouflage by changing the angle at which they approach the still insect.

“If you approach a flat leaf at just the right angle, you can actually get an echolocation signature,” Page explains. “You can get an echolocation signal that jumps back at you, but only if you direct your echolocation call at just the right angle.”

That’s exactly what the bats were doing in order to find insects who tried to hide in leaves.

When it’s time for the babies to wean or leave the roost, mother tent-making bats (Uroderma bilobatum) will physically prod their young with their forearms.

“There is this interesting tug that you see across so many different species. For birds, when they’re ready to fledge, there’s this sort of uncertainty.”

When sick, humans and even non-human primates change their behavior. We isolate so that the disease does not spread, and will look to plants and other sources of medicine for relief. Bats appear to change their diet to less nutritious leaves. While this may sound backwards, it’s in the hope that they have medicinal properties.

In an ongoing study with a team from the Ukrainian Bat Rehabilitation Center, the Smithsonian team is looking at the bats from Barro Colorado Island in Panama.

“We have fecal samples from these bats, so we were able to see exactly what they are eating,” says Page.

In addition to changing their diets, vampire bats will self-isolate when they are sick and others will act in a way familiar to any human parent—continuing to care for our young, even when sick.

“You don’t do as many of the normal behaviors unless it’s taking care of your babies,” says Page. “And again, it’s like a human relationship. If your little kid has a fever, you’re not going to shut them in a room and leave them. You’re not going to desert them.”

To find dinner, the fringe-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) will listen in on their prey’s mating calls. These frog-eating bats eavesdrop on the mating calls that the male frogs use to attract females. They then use the calls to assess if the frog is edible or poisonous before going in for the kill.

“They’ve really cracked this communication of another entire group,” says Page.

According to Page, it’s similar to being in a quiet room and hearing someone open up a bag of potato chips and is loudly eating. The noise makes everyone pay attention. “Bats attend to those acoustic signals of other bats, and that attention then helps them learn,” Page says. “They can learn those distinctions of palatable versus poisonous, and they can learn that socially as well from observing the other behaviors with bats.”

To learn more about bats and how to help protect them, visit Bat Week’s website.

#4652. Ultra-Processed Food Intake May Not Be Linked to Colorectal Cancer

  • Researchers report that consumption of ultra-processed foods doesn’t necessarily increase a person’s risk of developing colorectal cancer.
  • They note that there is a wide range of foods considered ultra-processed, and each may carry different risks.
  • Experts say people should adopt a healthy diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and calcium-rich foods to lower their risk of colorectal cancer.

Ultra-processed foods as a group do not increase the risk of colorectal cancer, according to a new study.

Researchers from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) report that they found no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer incidence among the more than 400,000 adults they studied.

They said their findings are important because ultra-processed foods now account for more than half of adults’ energy intake in the United States. They published their research on October 15 in PLOS Medicine.

The study comes amid recent research that has connected ultra-processed foods to an increased risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity.

However, the link between colorectal cancer and ultra-processed foods has been less clear.

A 2025 study reported that ultra-processed foods may increase the risk of colorectal cancer in females under 50.

A 2022 study found that males who consumed high rates of ultra-processed foods had a higher risk of developing colorectal cancer.

NCI scientists noted in their new study that ultra-processed products represent a wide range of foods.

They pointed out that foods such as whole grains and dairy products were associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer among study participants, while foods such as processed meats were associated with a higher risk.

“We did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence,” said Erikka Loftfield, PhD, a former investigator with the NCI, in a news release. “However, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention.”

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the NCI scientists appear to be on the right track.

“While the study did not find that overall ultra-processed food intake was associated with colorectal cancer risk, the findings suggest that simply classifying foods by their level of processing may not tell the whole story,” said Marissa Shams-White, PhD, a senior principal scientist in population science at the American Cancer Society.

“One of the challenges with studying ultra-processed foods is that the category includes a very wide range of foods and beverages, and we don’t yet know whether they all relate to colorectal cancer risk in the same way,” she added.

Ketan Thanki, MD, a colorectal surgeon specializing in benign and malignant diseases of the colon, rectum, and anus at the MemorialCare Todd Cancer Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California, agrees.

“Just because it is ultra-processed doesn’t mean it is inherently bad or good for you,” he said. “The source of the food and the additives like preservatives, flavorings, sugars, and pigments that are added to increase palatability probably play a much larger role in increasing cancer risk.”

“A whole grain cereal and a hot dog are both UPF, but they push risk in opposite directions,” Thanki noted.

Ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer risk

In their study, the National Cancer Institute researchers analyzed data from 461,682 adults aged 50 to 71 years who enrolled in the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study between 1995 and 1996.

The participants recorded their eating habits using a food frequency questionnaire. Researchers then used that data to estimate each person’s intake of ultra-processed foods. The median consumption of ultra-processed foods among participants was 43% of daily median intake.

The participants were then followed until 2018, for a median of nearly 19 years. The scientists reported that 10,075 cases of colorectal adenocarcinoma were diagnosed during that period.

The researchers reported no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and the incidence of colorectal cancer.

However, they said their findings varied by food type. Higher intake of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium derived from UPF sources was associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence, while meat from UPF sources was associated with higher incidence.

Researchers also found that replacing 10% of gram weight from UPF intake with minimally processed foods was associated with a 2% lower incidence of CRC.

The researchers acknowledged limitations to their study.

They noted that the participants were primarily older, white Americans. In addition, UPF intake was only measured once at the beginning of the study period, so the findings cannot account for changes in eating habits or the increasing availability of ultra-processed foods.

Nonetheless, Ashkan Farhadi, MD, a gastroenterologist at MemorialCare Orange Coast Medical Center in California, who wasn’t involved in the study, said he found the results “very interesting.”

“The definition of ultra-processed foods is so broad,” he told Healthline. “It’s important to designate what type of ultra-processed foods you’re talking about.”

“You shouldn’t just go by whether a food is designated as ultra-processed,” Farhadi added. “You should go by what’s on the [ingredient] label.”

Shams-White shared a similar approach. “When it comes to diet, it is important to follow an overall healthy dietary pattern,” she told Healthline. “Emphasize a variety of fruits and vegetables and whole grains and limit red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, ultra-processed foods, and refined grain products.”

“Eating a diet rich in fiber and whole grains, dairy and calcium, and limiting processed and red meat and alcohol is what really reduces your risk of developing cancer,” added Thanki.

Risk factors for colorectal cancer

The American Cancer Society estimates there will be about 108,000 new cases of colon cancer and nearly 50,000 new cases of rectal cancer in the United States in 2026.

The incidence rate is slightly higher in men than in females.

The organization reports that the rate of colorectal cancer cases in the United States has declined since the mid-1980s, but they note that most of that decrease has occurred in older adults.

The incidence rate among people under 50 increased by nearly 3% from 2013 to 2022. Colorectal cancer is now the leading cause of death from cancer for U.S. adults under the age of 50.

Colorectal cancer occurs when cells in the large intestine grow out of control. About 70% of cases begin in the colon.

There are a number of symptoms of colorectal cancer. Some of the common ones include:

  • constipation
  • diarrhea
  • blood in the stool
  • bleeding in the rectum
  • abdominal pain

Diet is considered one of the risk factors for colorectal cancer. However, there are other considerations.

Farhadi said genetics, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and a sedentary lifestyle can also increase a person’s risk of developing colorectal cancer.

Shams-White noted that the American Cancer Society Guideline for Diet and Physical Activity for Cancer Prevention recommended achieving and maintaining a healthy weight, being physically active, following a healthy eating pattern, and avoiding alcohol and tobacco.

Thanki points out that there are risk factors for colorectal cancer that can be modified and others that can’t.

“Among non-modifiable factors, the biggest are age, a personal or family history of colorectal cancer or advanced polyps, inherited syndromes such as Lynch syndrome, and longstanding inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s colitis),” he told Healthline.

“On the modifiable side, the evidence is strongest for obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, alcohol, and diets high in red and processed meat and low in fiber, whole grains, and calcium,” he added.

Colorectal cancer treatment outcomes improve if the disease is caught at an early stage. The 5-year relative survival for localized colorectal cancer is 91.3%. The 5-year relative survival rate for colorectal cancer caught at various stages, including cancer that has spread, is 65.4%.

Shams-White points out that the American Cancer Society Colorectal Cancer Guidelines recommend that people at average risk of colorectal cancer start getting screened at age 45.

“Screening can help prevent cancer by finding abnormal cells and polyps so they can be removed before they can become colorectal cancer, as well as detect cancer at an earlier stage,” she said.

Thanki had similar advice. “Get screened starting at 45, or earlier if you have a family history or other risk factors, because colonoscopies can prevent cancer by removing polyps,” he said. “And never ignore symptoms like rectal bleeding or a change in bowel habits at any age.”

#4648. Why do dead bodies move and make noise?

Published Oct 25, 2026 7:54 AM EDT

Death is supposed to be the end of the road. The lights are out, the curtain has fallen. No breathing, no heartbeat—end of discussion, right? Not quite.

In some cases, corpses twitch, flex, shift, change positions, or even make noises that could easily be mistaken for messages from beyond the grave. But before you call the Ghostbusters, take a deep breath. The explanation is less zombie apocalypse and more basic biology.

Even after the heart stops beating, the body still has a few tricks left up its sleeve. The results can be creepy, sure, but experts say they’re perfectly natural. Here’s why corpses sometimes move and make noise.

Our bodies can continue moving long after death. When researchers in Australia used time-lapse cameras to keep tabs on a decomposing human body, they discovered that it continued to shift for at least 16 months, with some parts moving as much as 20 total inches over the course of the study period.

Researchers call this “post-mortem movement”—and it’s a lot less dramatic than you might imagine.

“A body can move, but not necessarily in the way that I think most people envision,” Derek Boyd, a biological anthropologist who co-directs the Forensic Anthropology and Bioarchaeology Laboratory at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, tells Popular Science.

“The movements are very subtle—you’re not going to see a body sit up or turn its head.”

After death, the human body moves through a pretty predictable series of decomposition stages. One of the earliest is rigor mortis, when the muscles stiffen and the body becomes rigid. As this process unfolds, a corpse may shift, creating the appearance of movement—but it’s easy to miss, Boyd says.

“You’re not necessarily going to see [the movement] unless you’re laser-focused on the body for the first 48 hours—and even then it’s just going to look like tension, just the body stiffening,” he says.

The next stages in which movement might occur are boat and post-bloat, says Boyd. When someone dies, the body’s own enzymes begin breaking down its cells, a process known as autolysis.

Meanwhile, bacteria and microbes start spreading throughout the body, digesting tissues and producing gases. This process, known as putrefaction, can “cause the body to become distended or start to bloat, and that bloating can result in a dramatic expansion,” he says. “The body inflates in various places. But, again, this is something that happens gradually.”

Eventually, those gases escape—and the body gradually deflates, sometimes causing small shifts in its position.

Finally, in the advanced stages of decomposition, the body’s soft tissues begin to dry out, a process known as desiccation. “We have ligaments that are attaching bones together, tendons connecting muscle to bone, and in these later stages of decomposition, those connective tissues can shrink and tighten—and, in doing so, they can pull on our skeleton in various ways,” he says.

Of course, not all postmortem movement comes from decomposition itself. Outside forces can also cause a body to shift or change position. Scavengers may drag or disturb remains while feeding, for example, while insects can move small body parts or contribute to the breakdown of tissues. Environmental factors, such as wind, flowing water, or shifting soil, can also displace remains over time.

While we’re alive, our bodies are constantly making noise—burps, farts, stomach gurgles, and growls, creaks, and cracks. Considering how noisy our living bodies can be, it shouldn’t come as much of a surprise that they can continue making noises after death.

As gases build up during putrefaction, for instance, the body can produce gurgles, bubbles, groans, or even noises that sound like sighs, Sarah Chavez, executive director of the Order of the Good Death, a nonprofit that aims to change how people think about death and make end-of-life care and funeral options more accessible and equitable, tells Popular Science. Fluids and gases can also get pushed through the mouth or nose, which can sometimes create surprising sounds, she says.

“It can be startling if you’re not expecting it, but there’s nothing supernatural happening,” Chavez adds. “The body is just continuing to do what bodies do—break things down, move fluids and gases around, and occasionally make unintentional noises.”

Ultimately, the fact that bodies can move or make noises after death is a reminder that the human body is “a beautiful ecosystem of its own,” says Boyd.

In life, our bodies are home to countless microorganisms that interact with one another and help keep us alive. After death, those same microorganisms continue to play a role in the body’s breakdown, creating the conditions that can cause a twitch, a shift in position, or an unexpected sound.

The body no longer has thoughts, feelings, or aspirations, but it remains a complex biological system in its own right. “We are walking ecosystems, and we remain ecosystems even after death,” says Boyd.

And scientists are still learning what these postmortem ecosystems can tell us. For example, Boyd says researchers are exploring whether changes to the body’s microbial communities could help forensic investigators estimate how much time has passed since someone died—a “microbial clock” of sorts. In other words, the very same processes that can make a corpse move or make noises may also offer valuable clues about the biology of death itself.

#4643. Letter from the Editor-in-Chief: What Breast Cancer Patients Need When Treatment Ends

  • Breast cancer awareness has never been higher, but public knowledge and support after treatment haven’t kept pace, said Marisa Weiss, MD, founder and Chief Medical Officer at Breastcancer.org.
  • The widest gap is the “quiet phase” after initial treatment ends, when patients are left with questions about side effects, work, and family. Some turn to GLP-1s, supplements, and cannabis without telling their doctors, often fearing judgment, even though some can interact with cancer medications.
  • To mark this Breast Cancer Awareness Month, Breastcancer.org has released a Breast Health Playbook providing 9 ways to lower your risk of breast cancer.
  • Healthline’s Editor-in-Chief Robin Hough sits down with Dr. Weiss, a breast cancer survivor herself, to talk advocacy, awareness, and care.

Breast cancer awareness has never been higher. Every October, the world turns pink and the key messages—that early diagnosis saves lives and that knowing the risk factors reduces the risk—are hard to miss. 

Breast cancer remains the leading cause of cancer death amongst women in most countries, with an estimated 694,000 deaths annually and around 8.2 million women globally living with a breast cancer diagnosis, according to 2024 data from the World Health Organization (WHO)’s Global Health Observatory. 

But when October ends, and the wall-to-wall campaigns move on, what has awareness actually changed for those impacted by breast cancer?

For Marisa Weiss, MD, the answer is that there is always more to be done, and she’s uniquely placed to hold that view. 

Dr. Weiss has treated breast cancer patients for more than 30 years. In 2000, she founded Breastcancer.org, a not-for-profit that provides expert-reviewed medical and lifestyle information on breast health and breast cancer to millions of people globally.

Sixteen years ago, she was diagnosed with stage 1 breast cancer, becoming, in her own words, a “dual citizen” in that moment, with lived experience on both sides of the doctor’s desk. 

I had the pleasure of sitting down with Dr. Weiss as part of Healthline’s Breast Cancer Awareness Month coverage to talk about how awareness has changed care for the better for millions of people around the world, but why critical gaps remain, and how access to trusted information and community can help close those gaps. 

“It’s good that awareness is high, but knowledge remains low,” she said. The misconception she sees most is the belief that breast cancer is something that happens to other families.

“Most people think they’re off the hook because it doesn’t run in their family,” Dr. Weiss explained. In fact, 85%-90% of people who get breast cancer have no known family history and no known gene in the family, “so we’re all at risk,” she warns. 

For most, a diagnosis arrives as a shock. “They’re completely surprised, flattened by it. Their life is upside down, and they scramble to find what they need to know,” Dr. Weiss noted.

Breast cancer care: Be your own advocate

That aftershock caused by a diagnosis is at the core of why Breastcancer.org exists. Because the person whose life is at stake is increasingly expected to direct their own care and advocate for their needs, access to accurate and empowering information is critical in that process. “Decisions aren’t being made around the patient and for the patient in a bubble,” Dr. Weiss said. “Decision-making is supposed to be shared.”

With appointments hard to get and clinicians stretched, she said patients have to do more of the work.

In practice, Dr. Weiss says that means preparing by writing down your family history on both sides and making sure your care team knows who you are. “What’s important to you? Who’s in your life? Do you want more children? Are you planning a vacation? All those decisions can have an impact on the care you receive.”

Gaps remain even with the best care

Where Dr. Weiss sees the widest gap is after treatment ends. Speaking from personal experience, she said: “I had the best care possible, and there were still gaps. You can find yourself alone with lots of questions and fears.”

Understandably, a great deal of focus and attention is centered on diagnosis and treatment, she said. Then it stops. “When your life is supposed to go back to normal, it never does. You have to find a new normal. That quiet phase can be really lonely and difficult.”

For Dr. Weiss, the questions that fill that silence are often as practical as they are emotional: “How do I know my treatment is working? How do I tell leftover side effects from new ones? How do I go back to work, and who do I tell? How do I talk to my kids? What about that new treatment I heard about on TV last night?”

That post-treatment period can also be the first time anyone has really had a chance to stop, take stock, and start asking “what next?” 

“You might think, what happened to me? Who do I want to be moving forward? How do I have a long life full of wonderful things, and remain as independent as possible?” 

What breast cancer patients don’t say in the clinic

Some of what patients do post-care never reaches their doctor at all, with Dr. Weiss pointing to a growing amount of personal care happening outside the hospital: GLP-1s, peptides, hormonal therapies, supplements, medical cannabis, to name a few. Breastcancer.org’s annual Pulse survey shows that people reported being interested in all of those things and more, and that they tend to learn about them online rather than risk the stigma of bringing them up with their doctors.

“They don’t really trust that their doctor knows much about these things,” she said, “and they don’t want a conversation that makes them feel judged. Some patients worry about what ends up in their medical record and then everyone’s going to know.”

Dr. Weiss says the stakes are real, because some of those choices can interact with cancer medications with potential for adverse or unexpected side effects. “That is why people turn to Breastcancer.org with questions that are personal, private, and important, including “what do I need to share with my doctor, and who’s on a need-to-know basis?”

The power of community, and its limitations

Peer communities fill part of that gap. “Breastcancer.org’s members share medical information, tips, and practical advice with people who have just had the call, or are about to start treatment and don’t know what to expect,” Dr. Weiss shared, and said that while she values that kind of support system hugely, it does come with a caveat:

The same community can also be intrusive. Dr. Weiss describes standing at a party, drink in hand, when someone asks what kind of cancer you had, what you did, and whether it came back. Her advice to patients, which she took herself: “Put up your hand and say, ‘thank you so much for caring, but I cannot hear another story right now.’”

Information you can trust in the age of AI 

On the topic of AI and its impact on treatment and care, Dr. Weiss is cautiously optimistic. On the treatment front, she points to tools that help radiologists read mammograms more thoroughly and assess future risk as game changers in the fight against breast cancer. 

Breastcancer.org also uses AI for tasks such as finding citations and interpreting pathology reports. But like many, she is wary of misinformation. “When you’re fearful, you look for reassurance, and you don’t want to be reassured by misinformation. When it comes to health information, every word matters.”

Her final advice is for the appointment itself, which she says she has even struggled with. “When they start speaking quickly in complex medical language, you can get lost really fast. The risk is you kind of fake it. That must not happen.” 

“Do your research in advance. Write things down. Bring someone you trust as a second set of ears. Ask direct questions. Don’t worry if it sounds funny or silly. It’s always a smart question when you are dealing with your health.” Just be prepared for the questions to multiply from there. “Once you get a certain set of questions answered, that raises more questions. So just write it all down.”

If breast cancer has touched your life, know that you are not alone. Healthline’s Bezzy Breast Cancer Community is a space for peer support, feel-good stories, and advice in a welcoming, stigma-free environment when your lived experience is seen, heard and valued.

A new international study found that women with autoimmune diseases often experience longer delays in getting a correct diagnosis compared to men.

The research, published in eClinicalMedicine on October 12, also shows that females report higher rates of being misdiagnosed, especially with mental health or psychosomatic conditions.

These findings highlight challenges in diagnosing autoimmune diseases and suggest that sex differences affect patients’ experiences in healthcare.

This is significant given that autoimmune diseases, conditions in which the body’s immune system attacks its own healthy cells, are estimated to affect around 4.6% of the U.S. population. Additionally, despite the observed disparities in diagnoses, women are almost two times as likely as men to be diagnosed with an autoimmune disease.

Researchers conducted an online survey between December 7, 2024, and March 7, 2025, inviting adults ages 18 and older who had a confirmed autoimmune or rheumatic disease.

The survey was carefully designed and tested with input from patients, charities, and medical experts to ensure it captured important information about patients’ diagnostic journeys.

Participants were asked about the time it took from when they first noticed symptoms to when they received their autoimmune diagnosis.

They also reported how many healthcare visits they made before diagnosis and whether they felt their symptoms were ever misdiagnosed as mental health issues, psychosomatic problems (physical symptoms caused or worsened by psychological factors), or another physical disease.

The survey was widely shared on social media, patient support groups, and community organizations to reach a broad audience.

Special efforts were made to include underrepresented groups such as males, younger individuals, and people from minority ethnic backgrounds.

The survey was available in five languages, and participants could complete it with help from researchers if needed.

To analyze the data, the team used statistical methods, adjusting for factors like age, type of autoimmune disease, continent of residence, and financial situation.

These methods helped compare the experiences of males and females while accounting for differences in disease types and other factors

The study included 6,607 participants, with 91.2% identifying as female and most living in the United Kingdom. On average, women were younger than men in the study.

Autoimmune thyroid disease was the most common condition reported. Non-autoimmune conditions like anxiety were more commonly reported by women, while men reported higher rates of hypertension.

One of the main findings was that women reported longer delays before getting a diagnosis. Nearly 32% of females experienced delays of four years or more from symptom onset to diagnosis, compared to about 26% of males.

On the other hand, men were more likely to be diagnosed within a year of symptom onset (nearly 50% of males versus 40% of females).

Women also made more visits to healthcare providers before diagnosis, with 26.2% having 11 or more visits compared to 18.4% of men.

When it came to misdiagnoses, women were significantly more likely to report that their symptoms had been mistaken for mental health issues (43.3% of females vs. 32.7% of males) or psychosomatic causes (33.9% of females vs. 21.5% of males).

However, there was no significant difference between men and women in being misdiagnosed with another physical disease.

The type of autoimmune disease also influenced misdiagnosis rates. Diseases with clear physical symptoms or definitive lab tests, like type 1 diabetes or rheumatoid arthritis, had lower rates of mental health and psychosomatic misdiagnoses.

In contrast, conditions such as autoimmune encephalitis or undifferentiated connective tissue disease had higher rates of these misdiagnoses (over 50%).

After adjusting for disease type and other factors, women still had higher odds of reporting mental health or psychosomatic misdiagnoses compared to men.

Other factors affected misdiagnosis rates, too. People living in continents outside the United Kingdom, those with lower household income, and certain disease groups were more likely to report psychiatric or psychosomatic misdiagnoses. Age, however, was not linked to the risk of being misdiagnosed.

Muhammad Qasim, MD, a pulmonologist and medical writer with MySleepApneaTeam, told Healthline that autoimmune diseases are often difficult to diagnose. Qasim wasn’t involved in the study.

“Early symptoms can be vague, fluctuate over time, and affect several parts of the body,” he said. “Fatigue, pain, sleep problems, brain fog, and mood changes do not immediately point to one diagnosis.”

Qasim echoed the study’s findings, explaining that similar symptoms may be interpreted differently in women versus men, with complaints being attributed to anxiety, stress, or psychosomatic causes.

“Differences in disease presentation, gaps in research, fragmented care, and unconscious bias can all contribute,” said Qasim. “Once a psychiatric explanation enters the medical record, there is also a risk that later clinicians view new symptoms through that same lens.”

This review of 12 clinical trials involving over 1,000 people ages 40 to 65 found that both aerobic and mind-body exercises may be associated with reduced depressive symptoms and improved well-being.

It is common for individuals to experience psychological symptoms during menopause, including depression, anxiety, low mood, stress, and difficulty with thinking and memory. These symptoms can have a significant effect on quality of life, with an estimated 1 in 10 women leaving work due to menopause symptoms.

This study found that regular physical activity may help improve certain psychological symptoms and overall quality of life during menopause.

The types of physical activity in the review were broken into two categories: aerobic-based exercise (ABIs) and mind-body activities (MBAs).

The researchers found that ABIs showed the strongest effects on reducing depressive symptoms and improving overall quality of life. This may be particularly relevant during perimenopause when women may be more vulnerable to mood instability due to estrogen fluctuations.

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, pointed out that there are various ways that ABIs can have positive effects.

Ross explained that aerobic exercise may help improve menopausal symptoms through several mechanisms, including the release of endorphins, reduced inflammation, improved cholesterol levels, and better regulation of body temperature and sleep cycles.

MBAs were shown to have a greater effect on improving psychological quality of life. These exercises involve mindfulness, body awareness, and breathwork. They may help improve emotional regulation.

ABIs tended to require fewer sessions. This may mean these types of exercises are easier to fit in, especially if you are facing time or energy constraints. For example, in one trial, participants were instructed to walk for 30 minutes, three times per week. In another trial, participants attended three hour-long Zumba classes per week.

In contrast, one trial using MBAs instructed participants to attend two 75-minute yoga classes each week, and another had participants attend daily one-hour yoga classes.

Stephanie Faubion, MD, Director for the Mayo Clinic Center for Women’s Health and Medical Director for The Menopause Society, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings are encouraging.

However, she also cautioned that this was a relatively small body of evidence. “While physical activity is an important component of a healthy lifestyle during midlife, I would be cautious about interpreting these findings to mean that exercise is a treatment for depression related specifically to menopause,” she said.

Research has shown that physical activity can positively affect mental health. Studies have also found that regular exercise can help improve symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Faubion told Healthline that, broadly speaking, regular physical activity may support mood through multiple pathways, including better sleep, improved physical function, and overall well-being.

However, she also noted that this study cannot tell us precisely why the improvements occurred or whether aerobic exercise is superior to other forms of activity.

Faubion said that recommending physical activity during and after menopause extends well beyond mood.

“Physical activity is important for cardiovascular and metabolic health, maintaining muscle mass and physical function, and supporting bone health,” she said.

“These become particularly important at midlife as women experience age- and menopause-related changes in body composition, bone, and cardiometabolic health,” Faubion added.

Ross agreed that physical activity is important for more than just mood and mental health improvement. She also noted that there are other types of exercise beyond ABIs and MBAs that can benefit people during menopause:

“These types of activities help with weight loss or allow women to maintain a healthy weight. If you are not ready for aerobic exercises, stretching and simple exercises help with balance and stability,” Ross said.

Faubion told Healthline that physical activity is an important part of healthy aging and should be encouraged during the menopause transition, but it should not be suggested that women can simply exercise their way out of significant depression or other bothersome menopause symptoms.

“Women experiencing persistent depression, anxiety, or symptoms that interfere with their quality of life should seek evaluation and discuss the full range of evidence-based treatment options with their healthcare clinician,” Faubion said.

#4624. In 1934, a doctor brought a dog back to life. He tried to do the same with humans.

Published Oct 26, 2026 9:02 AM EDT

Under the harsh glare of overhead lights at a laboratory at the University of California at Berkeley, Lazarus II lay motionless.

Dr. Robert E. Cornish, who’d asphyxiated the rangy white fox terrier with ether and nitrogen, watched the clock. Six minutes after the final heartbeat, the scientist gathered up the dog and strapped him to a teeterboard, a see-saw-like invention whose back-and-forth, up-and-down motion Cornish believed was key to restoring the blood’s circulation.

He pushed a saline solution primed with oxygen, heart-stimulating adrenalin, and the liver extract heparin into a vein in Lazarus’ thigh, then lowered his head over the patient. As his assistant rocked the teeterboard and rubbed the little body vigorously, Cornish exhaled deep breaths into the dog’s mouth and lungs.

Long seconds turned to minutes and then, suddenly, Lazarus gasped and jerked his leg. Slowly, his heart began to beat. Lazarus II had returned from the dead.

With the invention of modern cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) still a quarter century off, resurrecting Lazarus after his heart stopped was something of a miracle in 1934. But while the fox terrier lived for another eight hours and 13 minutes, he did not live well, stuck in a restless zombie state punctuated by the occasional whine, bark, and panting.

“Irreversible damage occurs to either the heart or the brain after a period of time, making a good recovery impossible,” Samuel Tisherman, professor of surgery and director for the Center for Critical Care and Trauma Education at the University of Maryland, Baltimore, tells Popular Science.

Lazarus II wasn’t Cornish’s first attempt to revive dying patients using his teeterboard. He wasn’t even the first Lazarus. The young scientist, a former child prodigy who’d earned his doctorate from UC Berkeley at age 22, became obsessed with resuscitation sometime around 1933.

Before working on dogs, Cornish began his reanimating work on people. A 62-year-old printer who died of cardiac arrest was the first to receive Cornish’s reanimation treatment. Four-and-a-half hours after his death, Cornish arrived at the hospital to strap the man’s heating pad-swaddled body to a teeterboard. After see-sawing the patient up and down for 90 minutes, Cornish failed to revive the subject—although Cornish noted a point at which the printer’s “face seemed to warm up suddenly, sparkle returned to the eyes, and soft pulsations were observed in the soft tissue between windpipe and sternum,” according to science journalist Frank Swain’s How to Make a Zombie: The Life (and Death) Science of Reanimation and Mind Control.

Two weeks later, Cornish tried the same treatment on a 22-year-old machinist who’d been pulled dead from the San Francisco Bay three hours before, working his body on the teeterboard for a full four hours. It, too, failed, along with a third attempt the following day in which Cornish submerged an electrocuted cadaver in a warm bath instead of using heating pads before placing the body on the teeterboard.

Dissatisfied with the outcomes, Cornish decided it would be wise to perfect his methodology on fox terriers before returning to human subjects. He named each one Lazarus after the biblical man Jesus brought back to life.

Cornish’s biggest breakthrough came with Lazarus IV. Though blind and unable to stand on its own, within 13 days the dog could crawl, bark, and sit up. Its appetite, too, had returned. Over the few months of his life after death, Lazarus IV went through almost a pound of meat each day.

The next patient, Lazarus V, recovered even more quickly. It took just four days for the dog to resume some normal behaviors despite suffering blindness and brain damage. Like his predecessor, he too lived only a few months.

Despite their mixed outcome, Cornish was praised for his Lazarus experiments. In the press, the scientist who’d often been considered an oddball by his peers for pursuing projects like underwater reading glasses was hailed as a celebrity. A movie about his work, Life Returns, was released in 1935.

But Cornish’s resuscitation experiments were not without controversy. Dogged by animal rights protests and other outcries from the public, UC Berkeley’s administrators had him removed from campus. Cornish continued his work from home, turning a garden shed into a laboratory that neighbors claimed stunk up the street.

The science behind resuscitating a dying patient has come a long way since the Lazarus experiments. These days, “how to restore a pulse depends upon the cause of the cardiac arrest,” says Tisherman.

“The first, most important intervention is assuring good chest compressions,” he continues.

How quickly intervention begins also plays a role. “It’s generally been considered that if the period of no blood flow at all, before someone starts CPR, is greater than five minutes, the outcome will likely be poor,” says Tisherman—though that idea has been challenged and “no one knows for sure.”

While drugs and medications similar to those used by Cornish to improve blood flow and return heart rhythms to normal in his Lazarus experiments play a part in modern resuscitation protocol, emergency medical practitioners have never adopted the teeterboard he believed was key to restoring life.

Cornish never had the opportunity to prove that his teeterboard worked, despite appealing to the governors of Nevada, Arizona, and Colorado for permission to revive criminals executed by the gas chamber shortly after completing his Lazarus experiments. Their refusal didn’t stop Cornish from making one final attempt in 1948, sending multiple requests to the warden at San Quentin State Prison to resuscitate kidnapper and murderer Thomas McMonigle following his upcoming execution by gas chamber—all of which were rebuffed.

Once news of Cornish’s plan for McMonigle became public, more than 50 other “mostly single men” volunteered to be brought back to life by his teeterboard but by then, it seems, Cornish had moved on to something a little less extraordinary: formulating and marketing toothpaste to the American public.
