#4019. The GameMaker engine series behind Undertale, Hotline Miami and Spelunky is going free for commercial use on PC

Here's some upbeat news to end the day on (unless one of the other awful people working here posts something diabolical, once I'm done) - you'll soon be able to publish games created using the latest GameMaker tools for free on PC alongside web and mobile.

Hitherto, game developers have needed a Professional license to sell or monetise GameMaker creations. That changes on 1st January 2027. You'll still need a paid license if you plan to release a GameMaker game on console, but pish posh, those things are going extinct anyway.

In case you're unfamiliar, GameMaker is a series of game creation tools that dates back around 26 years. Current developers YoYo Games – who are now owned by browser company Opera - announced that they would make the tools free for non-commercial use on PC in November 2023, bringing a rare grimace of affection to former RPS archchancellor Graham Smith's time-poisoned visage. I can only imagine his happily beaming mug right now.

GameMaker has been used by such indie noteworthies as Heart Machine (Hyper Light Drifter), Toby Fox (Undertale), Derek Yu (Spelunky, UFO 50), Vlambeer (Nuclear Throne), and Dennaton Games (Hotline Miami). And also by Graham, apparently, though I'm not sure what he was using it for. It's historically been aimed more at novice programmers (see also: Graham), with a focus on 2D visuals, though the latest incarnation, GameMaker Runtime, aims to beef up the toolset's 3D capabilities.

Community dev Ross Manthorpe detailed the change in a post on the official site, explaining the implications for people who currently have a Pro license. "Developers who currently own the Professional license will still be able to commercially distribute games made on the existing GMS2 runtime, and will additionally benefit from access to GMS2 runtime source code," he wrote. "Regardless of runtime, developers will still need an Enterprise license and to be an approved developer to be able to export to consoles. There will be no changes to existing tier pricing."

Baldur’s Gate 1 was the first game I truly loved.

#4014. FBI investigating theft of more than 6000 PSA-graded trading cards, including almost 3000 Pokémon cards

"Recovering every stolen item and returning it to its owner is our top priority."

The FBI and Santa Ana police are investigating the theft of 6451 PSA-graded cards, including nearly 3000 Pokémon cards, from a FedEx truck in California.

PSA confirmed the investigation, saying the orders were stolen on 13th October from a FedEx truck after pickup at its Santa Ana facility.

The company says that "recovering every stolen item and returning it to its owner is our top priority", and that it has given investigators video footage, shipment manifests, and item-level records.

In total, 6451 items were taken. PSA has published a list of every stolen card, including its certification number and, for serialised cards, the serial number. It deactivated the certification numbers and shared the list with marketplace sellers, auction houses, and digital commerce platforms, so the cards can't be easily resold.

According to TheGamer, which broke down the list by category, Pokémon cards account for 2978 of the stolen items, followed by 898 One Piece cards, 880 basketball cards, 640 baseball cards, and 433 football cards. The New York Times reports that the haul is collectively worth more than $963,000.

PSA has refunded the grading fees on all affected orders, and says these refunds are separate from compensation for the cards themselves. Orders sent with a PSA return label are covered by its insurance, with payments already going out. Customers who used their own label will work with a dedicated PSA team, and those who bought through a dealer should contact them directly. Compensation is based on fair market value, up to each item's declared maximum insured value, and customers can choose to return the item if it is recovered.

Anyone with information is asked to contact the FBI's Los Angeles office or Santa Ana police.

#4010. How I'm Using the Time Change to Kickstart My Morning Exercise Routine

I look forward to the fall time change every year, because I have plans for that extra morning hour. Turning back the clock is a perfect opportunity to kick-start a morning routine, since you can get up an hour early without it feeling like you're getting up an hour early.

We are, unfortunately, still doing clock changes twice a year. But I will take advantage of that fact while I can, because the fall time change gifts us with an extra hour in the morning, even if it is cruelly ripped from our evening routines. I'm not a morning person by nature, but my day always goes better when I get my workout in early, so I'm going to take that extra morning hour and use it for a workout instead of recalibrating my body clock to wake up later. I recommend all my fellow night owls do the same.

I mean, you could use your extra hour to sleep in—take it if you need it. But personally, I'm going to set my alarm an hour earlier than I get up, starting on Nov. 1, the first day of standard time for 2026. An hour is enough time for a simple strength workout or for a short run and a quick shower before officially starting your day. I reserve one day a week for sleeping in, which for me is Saturday. On days I'm resting from exercise, I'll still wake up on schedule but use my morning hour for something fun or relaxing, like reading a book.

If you want to fit a full workout in and need more time than the hour gives you, this is still a good way to soften the blow. For example, you can wake up 90 minutes earlier (according to the clock) with it only feeling 30 minutes earlier than usual (according to your circadian rhythms). I've been using my extra hour a little differently each year, depending on how committed I am to those morning workouts. This year, I may split it between extra sleep and a mini workout.

On the first day you wake up "early," things will be much as they always are. But now that you're becoming a morning exerciser, you'll have to come to terms with the winter darkness. It will creep in, sunrise getting a few minutes later each day, until your morning jog is, most likely, fully in the dark.

If you are running in the early mornings, definitely get yourself a light to help you see and be seen. I have a Petzl Tikkina, which I bought a few years back because Petzl has a reputation for reliability, and the Tikkina is one of their more affordable models. It's worked so well that the second winter I used it, I sprang for the rechargeable battery that fits in place of its three AAA cells. The rechargeable is designed to work well even in cold conditions, which is a nice plus for winter runs. Also consider wearing bright colors, a reflective vest, or even something like the Noxgear Tracer to give you good visibility on the side of the road. I have a neon pink jacket that comes out on cold dark mornings.

At the gym, be aware that the rhythm of the place will change. There will be a different group of regulars, and it may be more or less busy than you're used to. But even if everything is the same, there’s something luxurious about being able to take your time, when normally you have to squeeze your last few exercises together and rush to work. Savor that extra hour.

#4001. How to Prepare Now so the End of Daylight Saving Time Is Less Jarring

Sadly, we did not manage to abolish Daylight Saving time changes during that brief, hopeful moment a few years back, so in just a few weeks we will all be going ahead with our usual ritual: Wake up early, forgetting that the time changed during the night, then get confused when the time on our phone doesn’t match the one on the microwave. Here’s how to be a little more prepared.

Daylight saving time is what we call the time in summer, and yes, it’s daylight “saving” time, singular. This time actually gets its own time zone abbreviation: EDT (Eastern Daylight Time) instead of EST (Eastern Standard Time) on the east coast, with corresponding CDT, MDT, and PDT across the country. So in November we change from daylight time to standard time.

Specifically, daylight saving time ends at 2 a.m. on Sunday, Nov. 1, 2026. That’s when the clocks “fall back.” (In the spring, they "spring forward"—get it?) If you’re changing your clocks manually, 2 a.m. becomes 1 a.m. This means that if you’re used to waking up at 7 a.m., you’ll open your eyes after sleeping the usual amount of time to find that it is now 6 a.m.

The fall time change is a great moment for me, a night owl. When I was younger I would be thrilled to stay up an extra hour and still not feel excessively tired in the morning. These days, I’m more likely to use the time change as an assist to my morning routine. I’ll be up at 6:30 (for example) instead of 7:30, and I can get in an early workout or do some journaling.

If you’re more of a morning person, this may be a bit less pleasant. You’re going to fall asleep watching TV in the evening—earlier than you usually do, anyway. Maybe you should try to stay up a bit, to get a head start on shifting your routine an hour later.

For the creatures in your house who are blissfully ignorant of the concept of standardized time, routines are about to get a little weird. Most importantly: They’ll get hungry (and everything else) an hour earlier than usual.

If you think ahead, you can start moving breakfast time later, before the time change arrives. Instead of feeding your dog at 7, start feeding him at 7:15 now, and you’ll be less likely to get a cold nose in the face at 6 a.m. (Since we have about two weeks from this writing, you can shift the time to 7:30 after a few days, and then 7:45. You get the idea.)

Same goes for kids: Expect them to wake up an hour before the clock says they should, and to get tired (and overtired) an hour earlier than they normally would. A gradual shifting of routines will help here as well, and you may want to explain the situation ahead of time to kids who are old enough to understand. And if you expect your own tummy to be grumbling on cue? Consider a gradual change in mealtimes for yourself, as well, or at least have a snack on hand in case you need it.

When daylight saving time ends, we stop “saving” that extra hour of sunlight in the evening. Your evening workout or your evening commute will be darker and colder than before. On the bright side, we’ll see light earlier in the morning, which can be a good excuse to take a morning walk you might not otherwise take. (Morning light and morning exercise both help to set your body clock and may put you in a better mood for the day to come.)

If you head outside for exercise in the evening, check out our guide to how to layer for an outdoor run, depending on the temperature. You may also want to bring a flashlight or wear a headlamp. Here's the one I use.

It may be easier to wake up after the time change, but the flip side is that it may be harder to go to bed. This is a good time to take a look at your bedtime routine, or even to create a bedtime routine if you haven’t really had one.

I have a guide to good sleep hygiene here, but one of the most important tips is to give yourself a wind-down time in the evening—30 minutes minimum—where the lights are dim and you aren’t staring at a screen. It’s a good time to take a bath, read a book, or do something else you find relaxing. Soon you’ll be locked in to your new schedule—at least until our next time change in the spring.

Clean as many sectors as possible with the toothbrush without spending any more money. While you can get a better tool, like the nail brush or the hand brush, it’s better to save them once you have cleared at least 15 sectors.

Now, let’s look at all the upgrades. There are five different categories available for you to pick from.

To produce Moon Sand, you’ll need to purchase the Pressure Chamber. This will convert 5 Kg of Lunar Dust into Moon Sand every 12 seconds. It works automatically, so you don’t have to do anything yourself.

Once you have Moon Sand, you’ll need to get a Stone Press. This will consume 1 Kg of Lunar Dust and 1 Kg of Moon Sand to produce 2 kg of Moonstone Block every six seconds.

#3957. Virtue and a Sledgehammer review – a haunting sci-fi drama about breaking walls, AI faces, and your heart

Outwardly about hitting things for stress relief, Virtue and a Sledgehammer is really an affecting family drama that confronts pain but doesn't give in to despair.

It’s been well over a week since my final swing of Virtue and a Sledgehammer’s righteous whacking stick, and I’m still finding it difficult to shift the game from my thoughtspace. Having tempted me in with promises of cathartic, base-instinct vengeance against a town of roboticised arseholes, it’s since had me picking through the rubble of my own demolition spree, not so much looking for meaning as trying to parse the abundance of it. I’d gladly invite you to join me in the wreckage.

Virtue’s developers, Selkie Harbour and the apparent nominative determinists at Deconstructeam – previously of The Cosmic Wheel Sisterhood – say it’s a "narrative exploration" game. Which is accurate, on both counts. As pictured sledgewielder Pratelle, your most urgent interactions involve breaking shit, be it the walls of your childhood hometown or the android copies of its former inhabitants. Really though, you’re bashing with purpose beyond your (or Pratelle’s) own satisfaction: hammering away masonry will open up new nooks and paths to explore, which in turn leads to more flashback story scenes, better contextualising a rampage that might otherwise seem lacking in justification.

This is not a swift process. Pratelle’s swings are slow and deliberate, each one needing at least some charging-up, and end on a similarly lengthy recovery animation. While this imbues encounters against the more aggressive bots with some timing-based challenge, the game’s sluggishness also serves to lend some emotional weight – a means of holding your head towards the crumbling homes and the clattering humanoids. Nothing dies here unless you absolutely, intentionally will it, and once you commit, there’s scant chance to do anything but watch as it hits the dirt.

On an initial playthrough, the techniques of fellow low-polyist Mouthwashing may come to mind: that game conveyed the cruelties of its main playable character through similarly slow, purposeful interactions. As Virtue gives up more of its secrets, though, it becomes clear that while it wants you to feel Pratelle’s rage, it also considers her crashout entirely legitimate. To explain fully would mean practising in spoilers, but those narrative shards seriously are worth tracking down, each scene laying on depth and tragic complexity to Pratelle and her family – the latter being the reason she’s back in town to begin with.

Non-chronological, but coherently written and arranged flashbacks run an impressively wide gamut of wholesomeness, unease, and horror. And they wisely take place before the sci-fi element becomes prevalent in the present-day storyline, keeping the darker moments more compellingly, uncomfortably grounded. Even with a tendency towards quip-speak, especially from the mouth of Nina, Pratelle’s smug genius of a sister, the mixing of fond memories with traumatic ones heightens the impact of both. It’s naturally awful to watch Nina coldly kick Pratelle out of their mother’s hospital room, or to see that mother inflict physical abuse on the sisters, but the hurt hits different when a later scene shows all three warmly chatting at home. Based on the chatter of their robot avatars, many of the family’s neighbours are/were variously racist, homophobic, or outright murderous, but it’s the pain and betrayal seeded in those past moments that drive Pratelle’s stomping, bludgeoning campaign of violence.

Crucially, Virtue does not introduce these humanising moments to manipulate you into manufactured, Far Cry 3-ass, you-are-the-real-monster guilt, and in-story attempts to play this trick on Pratelle herself are quickly called out as such. The wrongheaded people are wrong, the game knows it, and there’s no ulterior motive beyond the suggestion that those we love (and are loved by) can be corrupted by ego, ambition, or inherited trauma. Still, that central, lamentable family dynamic, and how skillfully its members are built up and broken apart in such short vignettes, are largely why I’m still pondering the game so much. I also confess to having replayed the entire thing, in far less brutal fashion, in the hope – proved vain – that it might help change some of their fates.

For better or worse, fury is the only language this town understands, and Pratelle is a fluent speaker. The long windup of attacks is at least met by an equal and opposite reaction of object-mangling: walls blast open with an appreciably exaggerated shower of debris, and taking out a building’s corners will cause a thunderous collapse of the entire structure. Thwacking robots isn’t quite as satisfying, in part because of the underwhelmingy dull 'thunk' of the impact sound, but a solid hit will still send them tumbling backwards with a deserved lack of grace. In some delightfully fortuitous melees, a clobbered bot would ragdoll hard enough into an adjacent wall to crack it open, revealing a memory shared I could have otherwise missed.

I briefly wondered if having me collect all these was necessary, given the game has no problem auto-triggering regular cutscenes at points. Except, it kind of is. Pratelle’s journey is cathartic, but it’s also a labour, the weight and sorrow of what she must do mirrored in her leaden footsteps and determined but joyless sledgehammering. It’s entirely appropriate, then, that uncovering her full story takes some extra effort, just as that effort is entirely rewarded.

Virtue and a Sledgehammer stands against much. It’s anti-prejudice, anti-ambivalence, and in not very subtle terms, anti-AI: its depiction of a soulless (albeit human-driven) machine takeover, complete with whining about how hard life was before, throws up gladly smashable parallels to the chatbot infestation of creative and public spaces.

For all its violent opposition, though, it’s also earnestly, maybe even optimistically pro-human. While drawing a line at intentional and misguided evils, it’s accepting of our imperfections and the role they have in shaping us – remember you’re fighting about 600 identical automatons – and only plumbs the depths of true despair when humanity isn’t adequately defended. Rage is vented and steam is let off, but not in a purely spiteful, fuck-the-world manner, and even Pratelle is only tearing her past down so she can move on from it. Maybe one day I will too, if I can ever get her stupid, fascinating game out of my head.

#3937. How to Lock Your Phone Number Against eSIM Hijacking on Verizon, T-Mobile, and AT&T

Your phone number is the skeleton key to your digital life, and carriers have spent years handing out copies to anyone with a decent script. Resetting a bank password, recovering an email account, approving a crypto withdrawal: All of it can run through a text message sent to a number that a call-center rep can reassign in minutes.

The good news is that all three major U.S. carriers now let you lock your number with a toggle. Here's how the attacks work and where to find each switch.

A SIM swap starts with research. Attackers collect your name, address, and account details from data breaches or phishing, then contact your carrier pretending to be you. If they convince someone to move your number onto a SIM they control, your calls, texts, and two-factor codes go to them instead. A close cousin is port-out fraud, where the attacker opens an account with a different carrier and arranges for your number to be transferred there.

The scale is hard to pin down, but the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center logged 982 SIM swap complaints and about $26 million in losses in 2024, down from 2,026 complaints and $72.7 million in 2022. That decline looks encouraging, but the figures only include crimes that victims bothered to report to one federal portal.

eSIMs removed the plastic card, not the weak link. Researchers at the Russian firm F.A.C.C.T. found that attackers now break into a victim's carrier account with stolen or leaked credentials, generate a QR code, and scan it on their own device. Your legitimate SIM is deactivated at the same moment. There's no store visit and no phone call to a rep.

Intel 471 also flagged a provider that let the activation code go to email instead of SMS, which gave attackers a second inbox to compromise. That provider has since closed the loophole, but the lesson stands: Every shortcut for locked-out customers is also a shortcut for criminals.

Carriers now offer three overlapping defenses. A SIM lock blocks SIM or eSIM changes and device upgrades; a port-out lock stops your number from moving to another carrier; and a PIN adds a password check when you contact customer service or request a transfer.

I'd treat the PIN as the weakest of the three. PINs aren’t bulletproof because company reps can still override them. A lock that grays out the option entirely is much harder to talk your way around.

In its 2023 order, the FCC instructed wireless providers to offer customers a free account lock and to notify them of SIM change and port-out requests. Compliance deadlines were pushed back until federal paperwork review finishes, though, so don't assume every carrier has implemented these features fully.

AT&T bundles everything into a single toggle called Wireless Account Lock. According to AT&T's support page, it blocks device upgrades, SIM and eSIM swaps, IMEI changes, number changes, transfers to another carrier, and edits to authorized users and billing info. You can still pay your bill, change plans, and unlock a device.

Open the AT&T app on a device that's active on your wireless account, and sign in.

Prepaid customers follow a different path: Sign in, choose your number, tap Profile & Settings, then Account Info & Preferences, and select Turn on under Wireless Account Lock. AT&T texts a one-time code to confirm.

Only users with primary or secondary access can flip the switch, and AT&T emails the account holder and texts every active number whenever the lock changes. I like that. A silent change is the whole point of these attacks, so noisy notifications are half the defense.

Verizon splits the job in two. SIM Protection blocks unauthorized SIM and device changes, while Number Lock blocks your number from being transferred to another carrier. Verizon's own documentation notes that Number Lock alone doesn't prevent SIM card changes, so you want both switched on.

Tap the Me tab, then Edit profile and settings.

Find SIM Protection and toggle it on for each line you want covered.

After you disable SIM Protection, SIM changes and upgrade orders stay blocked for another 15 minutes. And if you ever port out, Number Lock must be off before you can generate a Number Transfer PIN. Two toggles is slightly more homework than AT&T's single switch, but it lets you lock the number while leaving device upgrades open, which is handy if you swap phones often.

T-Mobile also uses two features. SIM Protection blocks SIM and eSIM changes, and Port Out Protection blocks transfers to other carriers. Both are free.

To enable SIM Protection on a postpaid account in the T-Life app:

Toggle protection for the whole account or for individual lines.

Port Out Protection works differently since it's an add-on you apply to each line separately. In T-Life, go to Manage, then See Plans, then Manage add-ons, and check the box next to Port Out Protection. Repeat for every line on the account.

On T-Mobile's side, authorized users can add either feature, but only the Primary Account Holder can remove them. The per-line setup is tedious on a big family plan, though it's worth the ten minutes. T-Mobile also recommends setting an account PIN of 6 to 15 digits, which you can only change from the primary holder's phone while on T-Mobile's network with wifi off.

A lock protects against changes made through your carrier—it doesn't protect against someone who already has your carrier login, who might be able to switch it off.

AT&T at least requires its app on a device active on your account, which raises the bar. Still, researchers recommend unique passwords and two-factor authentication on the carrier account too.

Remember to unlock before you upgrade your phone or move an eSIM, because every carrier will block you otherwise. Flip it back on afterward.

The bigger fix is to stop letting your phone number guard anything valuable. Switch your bank, email, and crypto accounts to an authenticator app or a passkey where you can. Think of the carrier lock as a seatbelt: You'll rarely need it, and you'll be glad it was buckled the one time you do.

#3932. Taika Waititi’s ‘Klara and the Sun’ Doesn’t Understand Kazuo Ishiguro’s Book

An age when many people are convinced robots will eventually destroy us all probably isn’t the ideal moment for Taika Waititi’s feel-good adaptation of Klara and the Sun, wherein an Artificial Friend (Jenna Ortega) helps her grief-stricken owners realize, “It’s okay to be broken.” True, Ishiguro’s 2021 sci-fi novel was prescient in anticipating a loneliness epidemic that would put us under the thrall of machines programmed to approximate human conversation. But Waititi, who wrote and directed the film, forgoes any attempt to capture the book’s existential depth, offering a manic, lighthearted robot romp instead.

Apparently, the awkward marriage between solemn source material and Waititi’s quirky tone was intentional. When Vanity Fair caught up with Ishiguro last week, he described his own book as “bleak.” For the film, however, he wanted something different: He sought out Waititi’s “kind of wacky, bordering on surreal humor.” The Nobel Prize laureate compared this adaptation, which hits theaters October 23, to an artist covering a song and making it their own. Unfortunately, not all covers are Whitney Houston’s “I Will Always Love You.” To get a sense of how very different these tunes are, read on to learn more about how the novel and film diverge—with commentary from Ishiguro himself.

Ishiguro’s first-person narrative takes place inside the mind of Klara, a keenly observant Artificial Friend, or AF—a robot companion designed for socially isolated teens. In Ishiguro’s dystopia, many children are “lifted,” meaning their genes have been edited for superior traits (though cruelty and selfishness remain). They receive remote learning from experts instead of attending school. This prepares them for the universities that, with one exception, accept only lifted students. Klara serves as a beguiling guide to this dark world, slowly piecing together what motivates humans. As Ishiguro puts it, “Klara’s story is a kind of expedited version of a typical human life. We see her going from virtually a baby to an elderly person…. It’s kind of a distorted mirror.”

Klara appears programmed to lack most negative emotions: She never demonstrates cruelty, jealousy, or anger, though she does seem to experience love, fear (for the well-being of others), gratitude, and happiness. Her perspective—consistently serene and largely without self-interest—lends the novel a restrained and gently contemplative tone. Klara sees people more clearly because her view isn’t filtered through aggression or egotism. But even if it makes her more capable of love, that limitation also makes her less human. Ishiguro says the novel interrogates what we mean when we talk about love: “We tend to put a rather loose term on a whole bunch of emotions, and call it love, as though nothing needs to be said after that about this rather mystical thing.”

In the film, though, viewers don’t have access to Klara’s endlessly curious interiority or the philosophical questions it raises. Instead, Ortega’s Klara conveys the broad, plucky winsomeness of a YA heroine. Eager to please and endowed with the clumsiness of a toddler lacking spatial awareness, she pratfalls her way through the film’s early scenes, tumbling off the store’s window display and later slithering down Josie’s stairs. Those visual gags feel like an odd fit for the material; Ishiguro is known more for low-grade existential sadness than slapstick. That’s not to say his novel lacks whimsy—but it’s subtle and mostly driven by solar-powered Klara secretly making earnest appeals to the sun, i.e., praying. Ortega’s Klara, on the other hand, is made fun of for mawkishly thanking the sun for its “super-duper nourishment.”

Amy Adams and Jenna Ortega, Klara and the Sun, 2026.

Waititi’s film also changes a key element of the novel’s plot, drastically lowering the stakes. In Ishiguro’s novel, parents must decide when their children are young whether to put them through the gene-editing operation that makes them “lifted.” The procedure is dangerous; it can lead to debilitating sickness or even death. Klara learns Josie’s older sister, Sal, was lifted, got sick, then died. Yet even in the aftermath of her terrible grief, Josie’s mother, Chrissie, doubles down and chooses to put Josie through the operation as well. Josie also gets sick, and Klara is presumably purchased as her AF to help care for Josie.

The novel forces readers to wrestle with the uncomfortable fact that Chrissie would rather have two dead children than one who isn’t part of the dominant class. One could read it as an allegory for the current debate over how parents should educate their kids. Yet the novel doesn’t portray Chrissie as a monster, but as a regular mom facing monstrous choices. Ishiguro tells me he’s asking us to confront the nature of love: “Is the mother showing love towards her daughter, or is it something else?” he asks.

Amy Adams’s Chrissie is more sympathetic and less interesting. For one, she’s artsy—an interior designer with a tastefully bohemian home. (In the book’s vaguely outlined bureaucratic world, Chrissie holds some nebulous “high-ranking” position.) Most crucially, she doesn’t make the tortured choice to have Josie gene-edited in the wake of Sal dying from the same procedure—because, in the film, Sal has died of natural causes. That convenient revision shields Adams’s character from the critique of being so status-obsessed that she knowingly risks harming her child. Instead of gambling with her kid’s life, Adams’s Chrissie reveals her steeliness when she attempts to drown Klara, which doesn’t happen in the book. While a shot of Klara vomiting large quantities of water might get an easy laugh, it’s a switch that makes Chrissie much less provocative.

And it’s not just Chrissie who gets a Hollywood flattening. Newcomer Mia Tharia’s Josie is a sickly genius who’s invariably kind to Klara and precociously self-aware in the vein of YA teen heroes everywhere. In Ishiguro’s novel, she’s often kind—but also cruel, dismissive, selfish, insecure, and in denial. The novel’s Josie seems to have an inkling that her mother plans to replace her with a robot in the event of her death, but she never allows herself to fully grapple with that painful truth. In Waititi’s telling, Josie has figured out her mother’s plan. The two of them work through their resentments, with Adams’s character telling her daughter, “I was never trying to replace you; I just didn’t want to lose you.” Which is not only cloying, but confusing: She definitely was trying to replace her!

What we know about the contours of the film’s dystopia we mostly learn from Natasha Lyonne’s Store Manager, who regales her robots with bedtime stories about a pre-WWIII world where something called the internet existed before it got taken away. (There are humanoid androids in this world, but no internet?) But in the novel, we’re drip-fed other information about Ishiguro’s universe. For instance, Josie’s father, Paul (absent from the film), was a brilliant engineer who now lives off the grid in a “community” with other ex-white-collar workers, where they fashion weapons to defend themselves in tribal conflicts. He’s something of a doomsday prepper, but he credibly tells Josie that he’s living more authentically now than in his old life.

If Chrissie represents someone who opts into the ruling system, Paul’s character shows that there are choices even within this world’s oppressive parameters. The book doesn’t weigh in on which path is better; either includes decent people complicit in evil systems. “Most of us lack perspective,” says Ishiguro. “There’s something tragic about it…. Decent people trying really hard to contribute, yet because of this lack of perspective, they might be contributing to something bad.”

The other character in the novel who represents an alternative way of life is Rick, Josie’s best friend and love interest who isn’t “lifted.” Aran Murphy’s Rick gets short shrift in the film, but in the novel, he’s Ishiguro’s most decent (human) character: someone who plays the hand he’s dealt gracefully, teaching himself robotics and caring for his sick mother and Josie. He and Josie formed a “plan” when they were children to spend their lives together, though one senses Josie only holds onto it to keep her despair over her illness at bay. And once she miraculously recovers from her illness, both Klara and Rick fade to the background as she prepares for college. Meanwhile, Rick makes new friends, presumably with other non-lifted kids. Their separation doesn’t feel like Romeo and Juliet so much as the normal, bittersweet way of things: a youthful attachment that gradually dissolves as both kids find their footing in their respective social classes. In Waititi’s film, Rick isn’t given a future outside of Josie; he literally disappears from the film once she recovers.

The film’s last scene shows Klara at a beach that resembles a Florida RV retirement community, where she and other AFs listen to Lyonne’s Store Manager tell them the story of the Little Mermaid. Waititi depicts death as literally a day at the beach. That’s a far cry from the novel’s somber ending: Klara has been dumped at a junkyard where AFs go to wait out their “slow fade.” We’re to understand this is a kinder option for Klara, who could’ve been taken apart for research. Her mind is glitching as she goes over her memories, trying to put them in the right order. Suddenly, she sees Store Manager, who sits down to chat. It seems Manager has an interest in historical technology and has come to the yard to collect souvenirs and look for her old AFs.

Ishiguro’s Klara gives Store Manager a brief report of her life. After some pat reassurances, Store Manager gets up and says goodbye. Then we get the novel’s last lines: “She stopped and turned, and I thought she might look back one last time at me. But she was gazing at the far distance, in the direction of the construction crane on the horizon. Then she continued to walk away.”

It’s a gut punch. For a second, we think we’re getting a cinematic ending: Manager gazing one last time at Klara from a distance. But even though that conclusion would be many times less schmaltzy than the actual film’s ending, Ishiguro won’t give it to us. To the manager, Klara—who’s lived an extraordinary life—is a technological curiosity, no more interesting than a piece of construction equipment. As Ishiguro told us, the book is “bleak.”

Steam's Personal Calendar, a feature Valve has been testing for nearly a year, has finally left beta and is now available for everyone.

Personal Calendar, which initially launched as a Steam Labs experiment last October, works by highlighting upcoming games and recent releases - tailored to your interests - and arranging them by release date. The feature has its own section on the store homepage, and also has a dedicated page showing the games Steam's algorithm believes you're going to like, meaning what's included is different for everyone.

While Steam has always had several ways of recommending games to players based on their tastes, Personal Calendar takes it a step further by having a stricter recommendation criteria and arranging the results in an intuitive, month-based way that's less likely to overwhelm.

Valve says its recommendation engine relies on three key metrics: it looks at your recently played games, finds other players with similar playtime profiles, then looks at the games those players have been playing or adding to their wishlists. In a blog post, Valve explains only games "you play the most" are considered, meaning titles you try for a few minutes won't factor into your recommendations.

Crucially, the Personal Calendar's recommendations are not static, and Valve notes they're refreshed daily to incorporate new data where available. "In reality you aren't likely to see your recommendations change all that much from day to day," it writes, "but as time goes by you will obviously see new things pop up as the eight-week horizon of the calendar marches forward, or as games lock in their release dates."

Incidentally, Valve is aware the feature could end up having the opposite effect, i.e., limiting the number of games a player potentially sees because they fall outside their filters, essentially trapping them inside a bubble. The developer doesn't have a clear solution for that potential problem, but says it wants to keep things varied.

"With the Personal Calendar, it's unlikely that every day has a new game launching that is the same genre/theme as games you've been playing and is also a good recommendation for you," Valve explains. These are in addition to other films that have stayed part of cinema’s collective memory, such as West Side Story (1961) and Gypsy (1962).

Her personal life was intertwined with Robert Wagner’s. They married in 1957, when she was 19, and divorced in 1962. She later married British producer Richard Gregson, the father of her daughter Natasha, born in 1970. In 1972, Wood and Wagner remarried, and in 1974 their daughter Courtney was born. It was one of Hollywood’s most celebrated second chances: a home always filled with friends, animals, and children, frequented by the likes of Fred Astaire, Bette Davis, Gene Kelly, and Audrey Hepburn. It’s also where Wagner raised Natasha, as if she were his own daughter.

Natalie Wood with her daughters Natasha and Courtney.

It all came to an end on November 29, 1981. Wood, at age 43, drowned off the coast of Santa Catalina during a weekend aboard the family yacht, the Splendour. The official investigation concluded it was an accident. But in 2011, the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Office reopened the case. In 2012, the medical examiner changed the cause of death to “drowning and other undetermined factors,” and in 2018, investigators designated Wagner as a “person of interest.” But he has never been charged with any crime, and none of these reviews have led the justice system to accuse him of anything.

While the courts have not charged Wagner, headlines have debated his involvement for nearly 45 years. Sensationalist books, television specials, true-crime documentaries, podcasts, and talk shows have judged and condemned him without conclusive evidence, without a trial, and without an opportunity to defend himself. Every anniversary, every reopening of the case, and every new statement from a witness from four decades ago has reignited the same insinuation—sold as a revelation—dredging up the family’s pain.

Those who carry the least blame have paid the price. Natasha and Courtney lost their mother when they were 11 and 7 years old, respectively, and they grew up watching her death being written about on magazine covers and seeing the man who raised them—the very same man who supported them through their grief and kept Natalie’s memory alive at home—being singled out. Today Wagner is 96, and Natasha doesn’t want the last thing the world remembers about him to be an accusation that no court ever formally made. “I think it’s reckless and dangerous when people make up lies or assumptions, and then they become the truth that’s passed around,” she says. “It’s just so absurd that somebody as kind and generous and loving as my stepdad has been maligned in this way.”

Natasha Gregson Wagner has chosen another way to tell that story. In 2020, she coproduced the documentary directed by Laurent Bouzereau, Natalie Wood: What Remains Behind, an intimate portrait of her mother as an actor, wife, and mother—not as a victim. And through scent, Natasha has found a more personal way to keep her mother’s memory alive. In 2016, she launched “Natalie,” a perfume inspired by Jungle Gardenia, the fragrance her mother wore. Today, that first creation is at the heart of L’Amour Mère, a perfume line dedicated to the love between grandmothers, mothers, and daughters. It’s complemented by C-Love—inspired by her daughter Clover, Natalie Wood’s granddaughter and one of the faces of the campaign—and Lyublyu, which means “love” in Russian and pays tribute to her grandmother. Gardenia, jasmine, and tuberose: It’s the scent of Natalie Wood, bottled by her daughter.

Vanity Fair España spoke with Natasha about the scent of her childhood home, the stars who visited her, James Dean, and her biological father, as well as her daughter Clover and the letters she’s compiling for a book. Everything except the tragedy.

Vanity Fair España: Your mother was an icon, but you were very young when she died. What is your earliest memory of her?

Gregson Wagner: Well, luckily I have so many memories of my mom, and we were so close that, even though she was only alive for 11 years of my life, I feel like she gave me a lifetime of memories, enough to power me through my own life… One of my first memories was when my mom and my daddy, Gregson, my real father, when they broke up, my mom took me and my Aunt Olga and her best friend, Mark Crowley, on a ship, the SS Rafaelo, to Sardinia.

We were in a hotel, and it was a dark room. My godfather, Mark, was feeding me spaghetti. My mom was resting. I just remember feeling like she was sad and I needed to, like, take care of her in some way. I remember feeling like I had to be a good child for her.

If you close your eyes and try to remember the smell of your home when you were little, what’s the first thing that comes to mind?

I love that question. Two things. My mom burned Rigaud candles, the green Cyprès ones made of wax. So that smell, which is like cypress, and then her gardenia perfume, and then roses. We had lots of roses in the garden, and so she put them in the house a lot. Those are the scents that I remember. And food. My mom wasn't a cook, but the woman that took care of us, Willie Mae, she was an amazing cook. And she was from the South. There was a lot of happiness in my house growing up. There were lots of people. They always had friends staying with us, and we had lots of animals. It was very happy.

The best of Hollywood used to pass through her house: Fred Astaire, Frank Sinatra, Gene Kelly. What memories do you have of them?

Oh, my gosh. Well, our house was like a hub. My parents loved to entertain. Gene Kelly lived down the street, and Fred Astaire lived nearby. Sir Lawrence Olivier was also a good friend of theirs. Ruth Gordon was my godmother. So my house was always full of these kinds of people. Betty Davis, Audrey Hepburn. My parents told me that they were extremely famous, but I just experienced them as being very down to earth, and they always smelled really good, whatever cologne or perfume they wore. And they were always dressed extremely beautifully. I remember Fred Astaire dressed exquisitely. David Niven, Ruth Gordon, she always wore the most beautiful dresses and pearls and just exquisite jewelry. A lot of dignity and elegance. And they would give my sister Courtney and I attention. They would talk to us, you know? We weren't banished. We were allowed to be around everybody. It's such a good memory.

Her mother started working when she was just four years old; America watched her grow up. Do you think she had a happy childhood?

I think that there were parts of her childhood that were very happy. My grandma loved my mom so much, but she was overprotective and over-controlling. She really wanted my mom to be an actress. Luckily, my mom liked it, so it worked out. What I’ve read and remember is that my mom didn't feel that she had a normal childhood. And so she really wanted that for my sister and me. She created this very magical childhood for us and really loved being a mom. We were so lucky in that way. I think she had a complicated childhood, but parts of it were really wonderful.

You’ve said that the photos of your mother belong to the world, but that her scent belongs to you. What perfume did she wear?

She wore this perfume called Jungle Gardenia by Tuvaché. It's no longer in production. She found it from Barbara Stanwyck when they did a movie together called The Bride Wore Boots, and she loved it. She was a little girl, but she loved it. That became her signature scent, and she wore it all of her life. It smells a bit like Fracas. It's tuberose, jasmine, and gardenia—that kind of scent.

You’ve created a perfume in her honor, Natalie. What are its main scents?

The main scents in Natalie are gardenia, jasmine, tuberose, vanilla, orange blossom, neroli, and bergamot. It has more citrus notes than the one that she wore because it’s a combination of what I love and what she loved.

What is the biggest misconception about Natalie Wood?

That she was tragic or doomed or overly vulnerable. She had an open heart and was a humanitarian. You felt her heart when you watched her. She was very much in control of her life. She was extremely powerful as a businesswoman, as a wife, a mother, a friend. She ran the show. And she was strong, really, really strong. So I think maybe the misconception that she was tragic or doomed…I didn't experience that with her.

There has been a lot of speculation about the circumstances of her death. How has that affected your family?

I think grief is such a private feeling. It's such a private experience. But coming from a famous family, it was so public. And so I felt extremely protective of my family, especially my stepdad, because he has been such a force of love and protection for me. He raised me like I was his own daughter, and he's loved me like I'm his blood. I know that my dad would've done anything to protect my mom, and I think it's reckless and dangerous when people make up lies or assumptions, and they become the truth that's passed around. My dad is 96 and a half years old, and I don't want his legacy to be tarnished with these mistruths or untruths.

But I think that all the speculation and recklessness of that brought my family closer. We are so protective of each other. And my dad is so proud of the way that I take care of my mom's legacy. He wants me to do that. He allowed her legacy to be alive during my childhood after she died, for my sister and me. He always spoke about her. He gave us gifts he had given to her, pieces of jewelry, and he would tell us why he bought the piece or where he bought it, and he engraved them. And it's just so absurd that somebody as kind and generous and loving as my stepdad has been maligned in this way.

What was your mother’s relationship with James Dean like?

She called him Jimmy. And I remember she spoke very lovingly and almost maternally toward him, you know? That was the first big loss in her life. It really shook and rocked her. I was young, but she did speak to me about him. I think she felt a lot of regret that his life ended so... That he didn't have enough time on this earth to do all the things that he probably would've done.

Do you think your father, Richard Gregson, regretted how his relationship with your mother ended?

Oh, for sure. My daddy Gregson, he had a difficult childhood and didn’t have a very warm mother. I think that when I was born, my mom's attention went so drastically from him to me that he didn't have the words to articulate his feelings of abandonment or loss of her, and so he misbehaved in a way that was extremely devastating and humiliating to my mom. But it was actually my stepdad, my daddy Wagner, who encouraged my mom to let Gregson back into our lives, because Wagner knew how important it was for me to also be connected to my real dad. My memory of my real dad and my mom is that they were friends. He would come to the house before we'd go out for dinner, and they would have a drink at the bar, and talk. They had a nice friendship. And when my mom died, Gregson was so devastated, mostly for me, because he knew how close I was to my mom. But yes, he definitely regretted it. How could he not?

Now you’re a mother. Do you tell your daughter about her grandmother?

Yep. The minute my daughter was born, it was important for me that she felt a connection to her grandma. I know my mom would’ve loved to be a grandma because she loved children. And Clover, my daughter, looks a lot like my mom when she was younger. When my mom was younger, the studio dyed her hair blonde, and my daughter is blonde. She has the same profile. So yeah, I showed her Miracle on 34th Street and West Side Story. She recently watched Slender in the Grass and Rebel Without a Cause. She's very interested in film. I don’t know that she’s interested in acting, but she loves film. She’s taking film classes, and she has jewelry now that was my mom's. I made these T-shirts with my mom’s face on them, and when I was packing Clover’s tennis bag, she gave me a T-shirt to bring with my mom’s picture. She wears it all the time. It’s sort of woven into the fabric of her childhood, and I hope that she feels a connection to her.

Oh, yeah. I feel her so much. In fact, the older I get, the more I feel her because I’m more stable in my life. I’m more peaceful. I’ve been through so much, and now I’m at a place where I feel grounded. In the quiet, we can connect to the other side. So when I’m with my dogs, or with my birds, or on a walk, or when I meditate, I feel her so much.

Have you been back to Santa Catalina Island?

It’s such a beautiful place. My daughter went there on a field trip with her school. I’ve thought about it, but I don’t really see the point in going there again. I spent a lot of amazing years on Catalina Island with my family, and I have so many incredible memories. So I think I’ll keep those and leave the island for other people to enjoy.

If you could talk to her for five minutes, what would you say?

My gosh, that makes me wanna cry. I would say, “I’ve missed you so much.” And I would say, “Thank you for making me feel so loved.” I would say that I have tried to be a good daughter to her and to take care of my dad, my stepdad, and my sister, and that I wish she didn’t die. Yeah.

You are so much more than just the heir to a legacy: you've acted, made a magnificent documentary about your mother. What are your next projects?

Well, I’m continuing to grow my fragrance brand, which I really enjoy. I’m in the middle of working on a new book of letters. When my real father passed away in 2019, he died of Parkinson’s, and my stepmom sent me all the letters that he had kept between him and me, and also him and my mom.

They’re unbelievable. I started going through them while I was working on the documentary, and also this summer, when we put together an exhibition about my mother’s life here in Orange County. She wrote amazing letters, she received amazing letters, and the art of writing letters has sort of gone away. So this is going to be a book about all the letters that she wrote to people, and people wrote to her. Hopefully it will make people want to write letters again—not just emails.

I am completely honored to be her daughter. And, of course, I’m so sad that she died young, but I do feel that she had a wonderful life.
