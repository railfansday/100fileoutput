Arrowhead Game Studios will only make a Helldivers 3 if it's lightyears apart in style from Helldivers 2, or if Helldivers 2's playercount falls through the floor. This is my takeaway, at least, from a recent chat with Johan Pilestedt, chief creative officer, in which Pilestedt touched briefly on the studio's next unannounced game, while assuring me that there are still no plans for a Helldiver threequel.

According to Pilestedt, 95% of the studio is focused on Helldivers 2, with a "small team" now prototyping Arrowhead's next game. They currently have around 150 people on the books. "Studio-wise, we want to grow on our own terms and know when to stop," Pilestedt commented over email, noting that "plenty of teams hit big and immediately double their size".

As for the prospect of Helldivers 3, Pilestedt is wary of dividing the audience while Helldivers 2 still has a following, commenting that "for now, my view is that a sequel resets everything and forces players to start over", whereas "a continuation turns the game into a lifelong hobby, like LEGO or Warhammer". In what probably isn't a sign that he's getting David Lynch to direct the next Warbonds video, he framed Helldivers 2 "as a 'third space,' a community where people can spend a huge amount of their time, but equally one that players can go away from for months or years and still find fun and community when they come back." For context, Helldivers 2's Steam live playercount as of writing is 47,097, which isn't chart-topping but seems fairly respectable for a two-year-old game; this doesn't include console players, of course.

"Some studios can treat players like a short-term cash grab," Pilestedt went on. "We see players as the actual engine of the game: they're the ones bringing in friends, making memes, and keeping the game alive. When you have an active community doing that, you don’t need a sequel.

"The industry is panicking because the old live-service tricks don’t work anymore," he said. "And the biggest competitor to a Helldivers 3 would be Helldivers 2. It only makes sense to make a distinct sequel if it’s so different that both games can exist side by side. For now, making Helldivers 2 better every day makes way more sense than throwing away its momentum."

Pilestedt didn't spell out what he meant by the industry panicking, but I assume he's referring to all the live service game cancellations of late. 'Live service' in general – aka, turning games into platforms from which 'rents' can be extracted, be it an optional cosmetic purchase or a subscription - has become something of a cussword among players who'd rather their games were less online, habit-forming and, well, landlordy.

The intrigue here, for me, is just how different Helldivers 3 would need to be to stand apart from its predecessor. Helldivers 2 is a very different game from the original Helldivers, swapping the old top-down view for third-person gunnery with a greater emphasis on physics and credible weapon handling. Where should they go next?

I'm not sure Helldivers would be entertaining as a first-person shooter – so much of the game's beguiling daftness depends on seeing your character. I could well imagine a Helldivers RTS, but that'd probably be a spin-off project. There are other directions they could take within the third-person shooter genre that might change the feel while supporting the game's brash and callous dystopian humour. Sound familiar? It's supposed to.

"The key concept was the 'third place' - a spot where you might hang out with mates after work, just as you would in Fortnite or Destiny," Origami shared, "which were cited as sources of inspiration. You played as a blank character, tagging along on the adventures of the heroes of Beyond Good & Evil, in the passenger seat."

But since then, the project "evolved", from smaller matchmade groups to more open-ended play, then to team-based play in groups of four and finally, to pairs. "The aim now is to offer a 'solo-first' adventure, with no mandatory online connection and featuring a strong narrative that can also be played in co-op," Origami stated.

"A few years ago, some people thought it bore a resemblance to Zelda in space; today, its combat is very reminiscent of Insomniac's Spider-Man games."

Yet despite its incredible length of time in development - I was at the Paris-based UbiDays event in 2008 when Ubisoft revealed the first CGI trailer for Beyond Good and Evil 2 - the game is apparently yet to enter full production. #4212. Popular cannabis sleep gummies may have a hidden dark side

Cannabinol (CBN) has become a popular ingredient in sleep supplements, often promoted as a natural, gentle way to relax and get a better night's rest. However, new research from the University of Connecticut suggests that this widely available cannabis compound may have risks that consumers should know about.

Scientists found that repeated exposure to CBN caused mice to develop signs of physical dependence. The findings, published in the November issue of the journal Drug and Alcohol Dependence, raise questions about the safety of using CBN regularly, particularly over long periods.

"CBN is everywhere right now. Gummies, tinctures, capsules, and it's marketed as a safe, herbal sleep supplement," says Steven Kinsey, professor in UConn's Elisabeth DeLuca School of Nursing. "Our findings suggest consumers should be aware repeated use may carry risks."

Popular Sleep Aid Shows Effects Similar to THC

CBN is a naturally occurring compound in cannabis, along with THC (tetrahydrocannabinol) and CBD (cannabidiol). Although CBN is generally considered less psychoactive than THC, the researchers discovered that it affects some of the same biological systems and can produce several key signs associated with drug dependence.

In the experiments, mice received CBN twice a day for three consecutive days. As treatment continued, the animals became less responsive to the compound, indicating that they had developed tolerance. When CBN was withdrawn, the mice also displayed physical symptoms resembling withdrawal, including head twitching and paw fluttering. These movements are comparable to the shaking that people can experience during drug withdrawal.

Another experiment revealed a surprising connection between CBN and THC. Mice previously trained to recognize and respond to THC's psychoactive effects reacted similarly when given CBN. This suggests that the two compounds produce overlapping subjective effects, at least in mice.

Together, the results indicate that CBN, despite its reputation as a milder cannabis compound, interacts with some of the same receptor systems that contribute to THC dependence.

Scientists Raise Concerns About CBN Sleep Supplements

The findings are particularly relevant because CBN products have become increasingly common, even though scientists still have limited information about their long-term effects.

The lead author of the study, Olivia Vanegas '26 Ph.D., notes "The marketing of minor cannabinoids is pretty wild right now. There are so many therapeutic claims about these drugs, but we really don't know much about their potential risks, especially with repeated use."

CBN is now widely promoted as a sleep aid, yet important questions remain unanswered. Researchers do not fully understand how CBN affects sleep quality or whether taking it repeatedly at doses commonly found in supplements could lead to addiction in humans.

There is also a historical reason for caution. Over the past century, several medications initially promoted as helpful sleep treatments were eventually found to carry serious risks of dependence and habit formation.

Potential Benefits Come With Unanswered Safety Questions

The researchers also identified potentially useful properties of CBN. The compound demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects, including at lower doses that did not produce psychoactive effects.

However, the experiments revealed another possible concern. During a simulated bacterial challenge, CBN reduced the body's ability to defend itself. This weakening of immune protection could make it easier for an infection to develop, similar to how bacteria can enter through a cut on the foot and cause illness.

These findings suggest that CBN may have medical applications, but scientists need a clearer understanding of its benefits, potential side effects, and appropriate doses before determining how it can be used safely.

Importantly, the experiments were conducted in mice, so the findings do not establish that CBN causes dependence or withdrawal in people. Additional studies are needed to understand how the compound affects humans, especially when taken frequently, at higher doses, or over extended periods.

"CBN may still have therapeutic value," he says. "But until we know more about dosing and long-term effects, the message for consumers is simple: buyer beware."

#4203. Scientists overcome a hidden obstacle to more powerful quantum computers

Silicon spin qubits could play a major role in the future of quantum computing because they can be produced using the same semiconductor manufacturing methods that make today's computer chips. However, researchers have faced a significant challenge. When multiple silicon qubits are controlled at the same time, their operations tend to become less accurate. Building practical, large quantum computers will require scientists to control many qubits simultaneously while keeping errors to a minimum.

Researchers at the RIKEN Center for Emergent Matter Science and their collaborators have now made important progress toward solving this problem. In a study published in Nature Physics, the team demonstrated highly accurate control of several silicon spin qubits operating simultaneously. Their experiments used an array of five qubits connected to a shared microwave control line. By identifying a major cause of errors during simultaneous operation, the scientists developed a technique to reduce those errors and maintain precision.

Leon Camenzind, a RIKEN research scientist and co-first author who supervised the experiments, explains the significance of the results: "This shows that high-fidelity control can be maintained even when several qubits are operated at the same time and represents an important step toward scaling up silicon-based quantum processors."

Scientists Identify a Hidden Source of Quantum Computing Errors

Unlike conventional computers, which store information as bits representing either 0 or 1, quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, that can exist in combinations of both states. Silicon spin qubits store this information using the spin of individual electrons confined within silicon. Performing reliable quantum calculations requires extremely precise control over these electron spins.

Microwave signals provide that control, but operating multiple qubits simultaneously can create unwanted effects that interfere with their accuracy. The researchers traced much of this problem to a phenomenon called the AC Stark shift.

This effect occurs when microwave control signals alter a qubit's effective energy levels, producing changes in the phase of its quantum state. Those phase shifts can disrupt the intended operation of individual qubits, particularly when several are being controlled at once.

To counteract the problem, the team created a calibration method that compensates for these unwanted shifts. Rather than separately calibrating every possible combination of qubits, the technique relies on calibrations performed between pairs of qubits. This makes the approach more practical for systems containing larger numbers of quantum bits.

"Rather than requiring an entirely separate calibration procedure for every possible combination of qubits, our method uses calibrations between pairs of qubits to compensate for the unwanted phase shifts," explains Yi-Hsien Wu, the first author who carried out the experiments as part of his PhD research.

Quantum Qubits Achieve Accuracy Above 99.99%

The researchers combined their calibration technique with an improved device design and carefully adjusted microwave control pulses. Their results showed that exceptionally high accuracy could be maintained even when multiple qubits were operating simultaneously.

When the five qubits were tested individually, each achieved single-qubit gate fidelity well above 99.99%, with the highest approaching 99.999%. Fidelity describes how closely a quantum operation matches its intended result.

The team then tested how well the system performed when several qubits were active together. With as many as three qubits operating simultaneously, fidelity remained above 99.99%. Even when all five qubits were controlled at once, accuracy stayed at approximately 99.9%, approaching the levels needed for practical fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Although the calibration method greatly reduced errors, it did not eliminate them entirely. The researchers found that the remaining loss of accuracy was linked to drive-induced decoherence, an effect caused by the greater microwave power required to operate multiple qubits simultaneously.

Unlike the unwanted phase shifts corrected by the new technique, decoherence involves the loss of the delicate quantum coherence needed for reliable operations.

According to Camenzind, "This is a limitation, but further improvements to device design could help mitigate this source of error."

A New Path Toward Larger Quantum Processors

The findings show that controlling several silicon spin qubits at the same time does not necessarily require a major sacrifice in accuracy. By correcting a key source of errors, the researchers have demonstrated a promising approach for operating more qubits through shared microwave control lines.

This is especially important for the development of large quantum processors. Giving every qubit its own independent control system would make increasingly complex devices difficult to manage. Shared control lines could offer a more practical way to expand these systems while maintaining the precision needed for quantum calculations.

"By addressing a key source of control errors through pairwise calibration," says Camenzind. "Our study supports the use of shared control lines as a practical approach to scaling up quantum processors. Combined with silicon's compatibility with established semiconductor manufacturing, we believe that this advance could help pave the way toward larger quantum processors capable of performing increasingly complex computations."

The broader objective is to develop quantum computers that can reliably perform calculations despite the errors and disturbances that affect quantum systems. Such machines, known as fault-tolerant quantum computers, will require precise, dependable control over large numbers of qubits.

"Our long-term goal is to build a fault-tolerant quantum computer based on silicon spin qubits, and reliable parallel control is an essential step toward that goal," adds Seigo Tarucha, project manager of a silicon quantum computing project under JST's Moonshot Goal 6.

#4193. Scientists reveal the hidden danger of missing blood pressure medication

Missing doses of blood pressure medication could significantly reduce its ability to protect against heart attacks, strokes, and other serious cardiovascular problems, according to findings presented at ESC Congress 2026.

An estimated 1.4 billion adults worldwide between the ages of 30 and 79 have high blood pressure (hypertension), a major contributor to illness and death. Although medications that lower blood pressure are proven to reduce the risk of cardiovascular complications, approximately half of patients do not take their prescriptions as directed.

"We have evidence that poor adherence to antihypertensive medication is associated with worse outcomes," explained presenter, Miss Qianqian Yang from the University of Oxford, UK. "However, previous data has come from observational studies and could be confounded by other factors. We undertook an analysis of randomized trial data to assess the true impact of adherence on the benefits of antihypertensive treatment."

Major Study Examines the Impact of Missed Blood Pressure Medication

To better understand how consistently taking medication affects its benefits, researchers analyzed individual patient data from 91,339 participants across nine clinical trials. Each trial compared a blood pressure treatment (intervention group) with an alternative approach (comparator group: either placebo or a less-intensive regimen).

The researchers divided participants into two categories based on how closely they followed their prescribed treatment. Those who took at least 80% of their assigned medication were considered to have higher adherence, while those who took less than 80% were placed in the lower adherence group.

Even with the regular monitoring and structured conditions of clinical trials, patients became less consistent about taking their medication as time passed.

"A decline in adherence over time was apparent, even in the structured setting of trials," noted Miss Yang. Overall, 88% of participants met the higher adherence threshold during the first year, but that figure had dropped to just 79% by the fifth year.

Skipping Blood Pressure Pills Reduces Treatment Benefits

The difference in medication adherence was reflected in how much patients' blood pressure improved.

Among participants with higher adherence, systolic blood pressure, the top number in a blood pressure reading, was 5.2 mmHg lower in the intervention group than in the comparator group. For those with lower adherence, the difference was considerably smaller at just 3.0 mmHg.

Researchers then examined whether these differences translated into protection against major cardiovascular problems. The outcomes included strokes, heart attacks (myocardial infarction), ischemic heart disease, and heart failure that resulted in hospitalization or death.

The findings revealed a striking contrast. Among patients who consistently followed their treatment plans, the intervention group experienced an 11% lower incidence of major cardiovascular events compared with the comparator group. However, among patients with lower adherence, there was no statistically significant reduction in these events.

"Notably, our results reinforce the saying that 'drugs don't work in patients who don't take them.' Patients with lower adherence derived little or no cardiovascular benefit, whereas those with higher adherence had greater reductions in blood pressure and prevention of cardiovascular events," said Miss Yang.

Helping Patients Get Lifesaving Heart Protection

The researchers believe that helping people take their medication consistently should become a greater priority in the treatment of high blood pressure. They recommend more routine assessments of medication adherence, along with better patient education and practical strategies to make treatment easier to follow.

Possible approaches include simplifying medication schedules, combining multiple treatments into a single pill, and prescribing longer-acting drugs that can help limit the consequences of an occasional missed dose.

Professor Felix Mahfoud, Chair of the ESC Communication Committee, emphasized that missed medication has received too little attention as a contributor to poorly controlled blood pressure.

"For years, we have neglected non-adherence to medication as a cause for uncontrolled hypertension. Assessment of adherence should become a routine part of hypertension care so patients do not miss out on life-saving benefits. As healthcare providers, we are here to have open, non-judgmental conversations with our patients about any barriers they may have to taking medication, to help improve adherence."

#4184. Just two drinks a day may be far riskier than you think

Even alcohol consumption that many Americans would describe as moderate may carry significant health risks, including a greater likelihood of premature death, disability, cancer, and heart disease. That is the conclusion of a new study published in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs, a journal published at Rutgers University.

The research, known as the Alcohol Intake and Health Study, was originally commissioned by the U.S. federal government to help shape updated national dietary recommendations. Researchers estimated that people who consume an average of 14 alcoholic drinks per week face a 1 in 25 lifetime risk of dying from alcohol-related causes. By comparison, consuming up to seven drinks weekly was linked to only small increases in risk for most health conditions.

"Even low levels of alcohol use come with health risks," says lead study author Kevin Shield, Ph.D., an associate professor at the University of Toronto and a senior scientist who leads the World Health Organization (WHO)/Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) Collaborating Centre in Addiction and Mental Health. "And that risk continues to increase the more someone drinks."

How Scientists Measured the Health Risks of Alcohol

To better understand the long-term consequences of drinking, Shield and his colleagues in the United States and Canada investigated how alcohol consumption over a lifetime contributes to disease and premature death among Americans.

Medical experts first examined more than 7,200 scientific publications covering illnesses and injuries associated with alcohol. Using this evidence, they estimated the risks for individual health conditions and combined those findings with large national health data sets. Statistical models then allowed the team to calculate how different levels of alcohol consumption affect the likelihood of developing serious health problems or dying prematurely.

The findings provide specific estimates that are missing from the latest U.S. Dietary Guidelines. The updated recommendations simply encourage Americans to "limit alcoholic beverages" without identifying a particular consumption threshold. Earlier versions advised men to drink no more than two alcoholic beverages per day and women no more than one.

"While the new U.S. Dietary Guidelines contain a useful 'less-is-best' message, they provide no quantitative framework. Our study was designed to do just that across the drinking spectrum," says study co-author Timothy Naimi, M.D., M.P.H., director of the University of Victoria's Canadian Institute for Substance Use Research and an adjunct professor at Boston University.

"It turns out that two drinks per day, which might be considered 'moderate' from a social standpoint, is associated with a substantially elevated risk of a premature death caused by alcohol," explains Naimi.

Moderate Drinking Offers No Significant Overall Health Benefits

The researchers looked beyond the risk of premature death to examine alcohol's connection to a wide range of immediate and long-term health problems. These included cancers (e.g., esophageal, oral, and breast), cardiovascular disease, liver disease, and injuries.

One of the study's most important findings challenges the longstanding belief that consuming small amounts of alcohol can improve health, particularly heart health.

"We did not observe a significant protective effect of alcohol on health at any level of consumption," says Shield. "At low levels, alcohol may be associated with a reduced risk of ischemic heart disease and stroke. But when you look across the full range of health outcomes, including cancer and other chronic diseases, those potential benefits are outweighed by the risks even at 7 drinks per week."

In other words, while some studies have suggested that light drinking may benefit certain aspects of cardiovascular health, those possible advantages do not translate into an overall health benefit once alcohol's links to cancer and other diseases are taken into account.

Although the analysis drew on extensive scientific evidence, the researchers emphasize that its estimates describe risks across populations rather than predicting precisely what will happen to any particular person.

Shield says the statistical calculations relied on "the best possible data." However, he cautions that individual circumstances can substantially influence the results.

"However, we can't assume that means one person's individual health risk is the same as what is reported here -- that depends on other factors like lifestyle, genetics, drinking patterns, and other choices that differ person to person."

To estimate alcohol's overall impact, the researchers calculated risks for health conditions already known to be caused by alcohol and combined them into a broader assessment. However, scientists are continuing to investigate additional possible consequences of drinking, including emerging evidence connecting alcohol consumption to pancreatic cancer.

"Understanding those relationships, and how much alcohol contributes to those risks, is an area that still needs further work," says Shield.

What the Findings Mean for U.S. Drinking Guidelines

The results suggest that alcohol-related health risks become more pronounced when consumption exceeds one drink per day, regardless of whether the person is male or female. This provides a more specific reference point for people trying to understand how their drinking habits might affect their long-term health.

Rather than suggesting that any particular amount is completely safe, the findings show how risks increase with greater consumption and why drinking less generally reduces exposure to alcohol-related harm.

"Having a clearer threshold helps people better understand what level of drinking is associated with increased risk and make more informed decisions when drinking," says Shield.

The study has also raised questions about how scientific evidence was incorporated into federal dietary recommendations.

In an accompanying editorial, Robert M. Vincent, a former Associate Administrator for the U.S. Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, offers his perspective on the circumstances surrounding the research and its role in the development of national guidance.

"The Alcohol Intake and Health report was explicitly invited to inform alcohol guidance during development of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030," he writes. "Despite the study's adherence to its mandate, its findings were sidelined."

#4172. AI detects “invisible” signs of pancreatic cancer years before diagnosis

An artificial intelligence model called REDMOD can identify extremely subtle changes in pancreatic tissue that may signal cancer long before the disease becomes visible to doctors or conventional imaging technology. According to research published online in the journal Gut, the system can detect early signs of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, the most common type of pancreatic cancer.

Researchers believe this technology could help transform pancreatic cancer detection by identifying the disease at a much earlier stage (stage 0), when treatment is more likely to succeed. Instead of receiving a diagnosis after the cancer has progressed to an advanced, potentially terminal stage, patients might have an opportunity to begin treatment while the disease is still treatable.

Although REDMOD outperformed experienced radiologists in the study, more research is necessary before it can be introduced into routine medical care. In particular, the scientists emphasize the need to test the system in patients at high risk, including those experiencing unexplained weight loss or recently diagnosed diabetes.

Why Pancreatic Cancer Is So Difficult to Detect Early

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma has a low survival rate, largely because it is often discovered only after the disease has advanced. Early pancreatic cancer typically produces few noticeable symptoms, and the changes it causes in pancreatic tissue can be too subtle to detect through standard medical imaging. The cancer can also progress rapidly, making timely diagnosis especially important.

To address these challenges, researchers created an artificial intelligence framework known as Radiomics-based Early Detection MODel (REDMOD). The system analyzes extremely fine patterns in the texture of pancreatic tissue, known as radiomics (radiomics), that are not readily visible on conventional computed tomography (CT) scans.

REDMOD also automatically identifies and outlines the pancreas, separating it from nearby organs and tissues. This process eliminates the need for specialists to manually trace the organ's boundaries, reducing the inconsistencies that can arise when different people perform the task.

AI Finds Hidden Cancer Signals Years Before Diagnosis

To evaluate the system, the researchers examined abdominal CT scans from 219 patients treated at multiple hospitals. At the time of their scans, experienced radiologists had found no evidence of pancreatic cancer. However, every patient in this group was subsequently diagnosed with the disease.

The scans had been taken at different intervals before diagnosis. For 87 patients (40%), the images were obtained 3 to 12 months earlier. Another 76 patients (35%) had been scanned 12 to 24 months before diagnosis, while 56 (25%) had undergone imaging more than 24 months earlier (up to around 3 years). In nearly two thirds (64%) of the patients, the cancer developed in the head of the pancreas.

The researchers compared these images with CT scans from 1243 people who remained free of pancreatic cancer for up to three years afterward. The comparison group was matched according to age, sex, and the dates of their scans.

Patients who eventually developed pancreatic cancer had an average age of 69, with ages ranging from 34 to 88. Those in the comparison group were 64 years old on average, with the same age range of 34 to 88.

REDMOD successfully identified subtle imaging signatures associated with pancreatic cancer an average of 475 days before patients received their clinical diagnoses. These early warning signs had previously gone undetected during routine radiological assessments.

"This temporal window holds profound significance, as attaining such early detection would substantially augment the probability of cure and improved survival," highlight the researchers.

"In fact, modelling studies indicate that increasing the proportion of localized [pancreatic ductal carcinomas] from 10% to 50% would more than double survival rates, thereby underscoring that the timing of diagnosis is the single most critical determinant of survival outcomes," they add.

The results also showed that REDMOD was substantially better than experienced radiologists at recognizing early cancer signals.

The AI achieved a sensitivity of 73%, compared with 39% for radiologists. Sensitivity measures how effectively a diagnostic method identifies people who actually have the disease, rather than overlooking genuine cases.

The difference was even more pronounced when researchers examined scans taken more than two years before the patients were diagnosed. In these cases, REDMOD achieved a detection rate of 68%, nearly three times the 23% recorded by radiologists.

The researchers also assessed how well the system could recognize scans from people without pancreatic cancer. In an independent group of 539 patients from several hospitals, REDMOD correctly classified just over 81% of scans as cancer free. It performed even better on the publicly available US National Institutes of Health NIH-PCT dataset (80 patients), correctly identifying 87.5% as free of pancreatic cancer.

Additional testing suggested that the early warning signs detected by REDMOD were consistent over time. When the researchers analyzed scans taken from the same patients several months apart, the system produced matching results in 90 to 92% of cases. This consistency strengthened the evidence that the AI was identifying meaningful changes associated with the disease.

Could AI Transform Early Pancreatic Cancer Detection?

Despite the promising findings, the researchers caution that several questions remain unanswered. One limitation is that the study population did not include a sufficiently diverse range of ethnic backgrounds, which could affect how well the technology performs across different populations.

The system must also undergo prospective testing, particularly among people at increased risk of pancreatic cancer, before researchers can determine whether it will be effective in everyday clinical practice.

Even with these limitations, the findings suggest that AI could eventually help doctors identify pancreatic cancer while it is still at a much more treatable stage, potentially improving survival for patients who would otherwise receive a late diagnosis.

The researchers conclude: "This study validates REDMOD as a fully automated AI framework capable of identifying the imaging signatures of stage 0 [pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma] in normal pancreas, achieving this with substantial lead times and performance superior to expert radiologists."

They add: "While prospective validation is paramount to confirm clinical utility, the REDMOD framework represents a significant advance towards shifting the paradigm for sporadic [pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma] from a late-stage symptomatic diagnosis to proactive pre-clinical interception, offering tangible hope for improving outcomes in this challenging disease."

#4164. The surprisingly simple exercise that could lower your blood pressure

New research suggests that exercising just once or twice a week may be enough to maintain some of the blood pressure benefits gained from regular physical activity, at least over a short period. However, people who continue exercising three times weekly may experience larger and more sustained improvements.

The findings, published in BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine, suggest that even a single weekly session of isometric exercise could help people preserve improvements in blood pressure when they cannot commit to a more frequent workout schedule.

Isometric exercise involves tightening muscles while holding the body in a fixed position for several minutes. Common examples include wall squats, planks, and handgrip exercises.

Earlier research has found that isometric training can lower blood pressure more effectively than other types of exercise, sometimes producing reductions comparable to or greater than those achieved with certain medications. However, researchers have not fully understood how often these exercises need to be performed to maintain their cardiovascular benefits.

To investigate, researchers enrolled 100 adults whose average age was 37.4 years. Participants had systolic blood pressure ranging from normal to high-normal levels (120-140 mmHg) and did not regularly participate in structured exercise programs.

Under expert guidelines, systolic blood pressure between 130 and 139 mmHg falls into the "high-normal" category, which warrants specific lifestyle recommendations from healthcare professionals. None of the participants had been diagnosed with hypertension.

To qualify, volunteers had to be free of injuries and illnesses, have no current or previous use of blood pressure medication, and be nonsmokers. They also had to consume no more than 14 units of alcohol per week.

Participants were randomly assigned to five groups. One served as a control group, while the other four began the same four-week exercise program at home, focusing on isometric movements involving the lower body.

The training consisted of four wall squat holds per session, with two minutes of seated recovery between each hold. Participants completed this routine three times weekly during the first four weeks.

After this initial training period, the four exercise groups followed different schedules for another four weeks. One continued with three weekly sessions, another reduced its frequency to twice weekly, and a third performed the exercises only once a week. The fourth group stopped training altogether.

Researchers evaluated blood pressure, heart rate, and additional measures of cardiovascular function at the beginning of the experiment and again after four and eight weeks.

Even One Weekly Workout Helped Preserve Blood Pressure Benefits

After the first four weeks, all four exercise groups showed significant improvements compared with the control group. These included reductions in systolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart contracts, and diastolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart relaxes. Mean arterial pressure, a measure of average blood pressure throughout the cardiac cycle, also decreased significantly.

These initial results were consistent with earlier studies demonstrating the blood pressure benefits of isometric exercise. However, after participants switched to their assigned maintenance schedules, the groups no longer showed statistically significant differences in diastolic blood pressure.

By the end of the eight-week study, participants exercising just once or twice weekly had maintained significant reductions in systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure compared with the control group. Neither schedule maintained a statistically significant improvement in diastolic blood pressure.

The greatest benefits appeared among participants who continued performing wall squats three times per week. Their systolic blood pressure fell by an average of 13.73 mmHg (-13.73 mmHg) from the beginning of the study.

By comparison, the twice-weekly group experienced an average reduction of 6.95 mmHg, while participants exercising once a week recorded a decrease of 6.56 mmHg.

Those who maintained the three-session schedule also preserved improvements in other cardiovascular measurements, including total peripheral resistance (an indication that blood vessels have narrowed) and heart rate variability.

Stopping Exercise Caused Blood Pressure Benefits to Fade

The results also revealed what happened when participants stopped exercising completely.

Those who discontinued wall squats after the initial four weeks experienced a reversal of their earlier blood pressure improvements. By the end of the study, their readings were no longer statistically different from those of the control group.

This finding suggests that maintaining some level of regular isometric exercise may be important for preserving the cardiovascular improvements gained during training.

However, the researchers emphasized that the maintenance phase lasted only four weeks. Additional studies following participants for longer periods will be needed to determine whether less frequent training can preserve these benefits over months or years.

"The study demonstrates that while lower frequency isometric exercise training may be sufficient to maintain blood pressure reductions over the short term, higher frequencies are required to preserve these benefits and the associated vascular and cardiac autonomic adaptations," the researchers write.

"The study offers evidence that lower frequency isometric exercise training can help maintain short-term blood pressure reductions, providing a pragmatic option for individuals with limited time or adherence challenges."

#4154. A tiny laser beam could make computer memory 1,000 times faster

Scientists have developed a new way to control magnetism using light, a breakthrough that could eventually make digital data storage faster, more compact, and more energy-efficient.

The research, conducted by engineers at the University of California San Diego, was published in the journal Nature Communications.

Modern hard drives and other storage systems rely on tiny magnetic regions to encode digital information as 1s and 0s. Writing new data usually involves applying an external magnetic field to change the orientation of these regions. However, this process consumes considerable energy and places limits on how quickly information can be recorded.

The UC San Diego team has demonstrated an alternative that uses light to change these magnetic states, a technique known as optical switching. Light can deliver energy in extremely short bursts and can be focused onto remarkably small areas, potentially allowing magnetic information to be written much faster and with less energy. According to the researchers, optical switching could achieve speeds more than 1,000 times greater than conventional methods that depend on external magnetic fields.

Shrinking Laser Light To Improve Data Storage

To make this possible, the engineers used a specialized ultrafast laser and carefully modified the shape and size of its beam. The resulting beam was dramatically smaller than those employed in earlier experiments, with the researchers describing a reduction of tens of orders of magnitude.

Such precise control over light could also make it possible to store significantly more information within a limited amount of space. By directing laser energy onto smaller magnetic regions, future memory devices could potentially hold more data without increasing their physical size.

"The smaller the size of the beam, the smaller and more dense the optical memory," said study senior author Abdoulaye Ndao, professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the UC San Diego Jacobs School of Engineering.

Until now, optical switching faced two important restrictions. Earlier experiments had succeeded only with extremely thin magnetic structures containing no more than three magnetic layers. The process also required light with a particular polarization, meaning its electromagnetic waves had to be oriented in a specific way.

"Past experiments had found that increasing the material's thickness beyond three layers suppressed optical switching," Ndao said. "That put a constraint on the overall thickness of the magnetic material and thus, its ability to retain memory in the long term."

These restrictions limited the potential usefulness of optical memory, particularly because thicker magnetic structures can help preserve stored information over extended periods.

Scientists Overcome a Major Magnetic Memory Barrier

By carefully reshaping and reducing the size of the laser beam, Ndao and his colleagues found a way around both obstacles.

They successfully demonstrated optical switching in a substantially thicker magnetic structure containing nine alternating layers of platinum and cobalt. The researchers also showed that the process worked without requiring a particular polarization of light.

"We've optically engineered the light to change the physics that's happening in the material at the micro- and nanoscale," said study first author Muhammad Waleed Khalid, an electrical and computer engineering Ph.D. student in Ndao's research group.

The technique works by concentrating laser energy into an extremely small region and delivering a sequence of ultrafast pulses.

The first pulses briefly heat a localized area of the magnetic material, causing a small region to reverse its magnetic orientation. Additional pulses gradually enlarge that reversed region until the new magnetic state becomes stable.

This carefully controlled sequence allows the researchers to manipulate magnetism in ways that had not been achieved using conventional laser beams.

"Working with a specialized laser allowed us to optically engineer the beam to have a certain shape and size," Khalid said. "That gave us the room to explore more fundamental physics, which cannot be done with conventional beam lasers."

An Unexpected Discovery That Required Repeated Testing

The unusual results initially proved difficult to establish within the scientific community. Because the effects differed so greatly from what earlier experiments had suggested was possible, the researchers needed extensive additional testing to confirm their observations.

"The effects we were seeing were so new and unusual that we had a difficult time convincing others in the field that our discovery was not a one-time fluke. We spent a lot of time and effort repeating and verifying our experiments to substantiate our work to the optics community," Khalid added.

The breakthrough emerged from a collaboration between researchers specializing in two areas that do not often intersect: optics, which focuses on light, and magnetic materials.

Ndao's optics research team worked with Eric Fullerton, an expert in thin-film magnetic materials at UC San Diego. Fullerton is a professor of electrical and computer engineering and of chemical and nano engineering, as well as the Endowed Chair Professor of the university's Center for Memory and Recording Research.

Combining these areas of expertise allowed the team to investigate an established problem in magnetic memory from a different perspective. Rather than developing a magnetic material with new properties, the researchers focused on changing how light interacts with an existing material.

"Instead of designing a new material to enable optical switching, we redesigned the light itself and showed new properties that were not previously thought to be possible," Ndao said.

The Next Challenge for Ultrafast Computer Memory

Although the results point toward promising possibilities for future storage technologies, significant technical obstacles remain before optical switching could be used in everyday electronic devices.

One major challenge is the specialized ultrafast laser required for the experiments. Current versions are not easily incorporated into computer chips, making practical integration difficult.

The researchers are considering other ways to achieve similar results, including identifying magnetic materials that could respond to laser systems more suitable for integration into electronic hardware.

They are also attempting to reduce the laser beam's size even further, with a target of just a few hundred nanometers. To accomplish this, the team is exploring optical structures capable of concentrating light into progressively smaller regions.

These efforts are aimed at understanding how precisely engineered light can manipulate magnetism at extremely small scales. If the remaining engineering challenges can be overcome, the approach could help enable a new generation of compact, high-density memory devices capable of writing digital information at much greater speeds.

#4144. New 3D thermal invisibility cloak makes objects disappear from infrared cameras

Scientists have developed and tested the first three-dimensional thermal invisibility cloak, a device that can conceal objects from infrared cameras while protecting them from extreme temperatures. The breakthrough could lead to new ways to cool microchips, safeguard delicate electronics, and prevent equipment from being detected through its thermal signature.

The cloak can conceal objects with a wide variety of shapes, regardless of the direction from which heat approaches. Earlier thermal cloaks were limited to two dimensions or worked only when heat traveled in a particular direction. The new design overcomes these restrictions by directing heat around a hidden object rather than simply blocking it. As a result, infrared imaging shows a temperature pattern that makes the object appear to be absent.

The research was conducted by University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign civil and environmental engineering professor Shelly Zhang, postdoctoral researcher Weichen Li, and graduate student Yibo Wang, in collaboration with professor Ole Sigmund of the Technical University of Denmark. Their findings were published in Nature Communications.

"A real thermal cloak should work no matter where the heat comes from," Zhang said. "Our device can hide a complex 3D object in an infinite number of directions while keeping the temperature inside stable and protected."

A New Material Makes 3D Thermal Invisibility Possible

Creating a thermal cloak that functions in three dimensions has been a longstanding challenge. Previous experimental devices could redirect heat only across a flat surface or along one direction of heat flow, leaving them far short of the capabilities needed for a fully three-dimensional cloak.

To overcome these limitations, the researchers revisited the foundational principles of transformation thermotics, a field that explores how heat can be redirected through specially designed materials. They focused on a central question: "What kind of material structure could cover almost all the thermal properties needed for a perfect cloak?"

The solution was a new lattice-based material whose structure can be independently adjusted along three directions. These changes allow the researchers to control how efficiently heat passes through different parts of the cloak. Compared with previous designs, the material can achieve a much broader range of thermal conductivities, closely approaching the properties predicted to be necessary for ideal thermal cloaking.

Importantly, the researchers went beyond computer simulations and constructed a working device. They used 3D metal printing to produce an intricate aluminum lattice, which provides pathways for efficient heat conduction. They then used mold casting to fill the remaining spaces with a rubber-like material that conducts heat poorly. Together, these materials create a structure that can carefully control the movement of heat.

Infrared Camera Tests Confirm the Cloak Works

To evaluate the device, the team positioned it between hot and cold regions, establishing a temperature difference that caused heat to flow through the surrounding environment. They monitored the resulting temperature patterns with an infrared camera to determine whether the cloak could successfully redirect heat around the concealed area.

The experiments showed that the device could guide heat around the hidden region while preserving the surrounding temperature pattern. To an infrared camera, the area appeared much as it would if no object were present. At the same time, temperatures inside the cloak remained uniform, shielding the concealed space from the extreme conditions outside.

The researchers also tested whether the cloak could accommodate unusually complicated objects. They successfully concealed intricate three-dimensional geometries, including detailed shapes resembling human heads. According to the team, earlier experimental thermal cloaks have not demonstrated a comparable combination of geometric complexity and cloaking performance.

Potential Applications in Electronics and Stealth Technology

The ability to control heat in three dimensions could have important implications across several industries. In electronics, the technology could help engineers direct heat away from sensitive components, improve thermal management in microchips, and protect devices operating under demanding conditions.

The researchers also see potential applications in security and defense. Future versions of the technology might help conceal people or equipment from thermal detection systems or shield valuable assets from severe temperature conditions. These uses would build on the device's demonstrated ability to redirect external heat while keeping the protected region thermally stable.

"Any field that needs precise control of heat or needs to protect something from being detected thermally could benefit from this work," Zhang said. "But we also see it more broadly: it's about hiding and protecting information that is carried by heat."

The Next Challenge Is Hiding Objects That Generate Heat

Although the current cloak represents a major advance, the researchers want to develop more sophisticated versions capable of performing additional functions. One particularly important goal is to conceal objects that produce heat themselves, rather than only protecting them from heat originating outside the cloak.

Achieving this would require materials that can actively control heat within the concealed region. Instead of simply redirecting incoming heat, a future cloak would need to concentrate, distribute, or guide thermal energy as conditions change. Such capabilities could expand the technology's usefulness in electronics, thermal protection, and applications involving infrared detection.

"We've shown that a true 3D omnidirectional thermal cloak is possible," Zhang said. "The next step is to make cloaks that don't just hide and protect, but also actively manipulate heat in useful ways."

The research received support from the National Science Foundation, the Villum Foundation, and the Air Force Office of Scientific Research. Zhang is also affiliated with mechanical science and engineering and the National Center for Supercomputing Applications at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

Perhaps with time, this will be a better device, but at this point, I’m more inclined to watch for improvements than to recommend it as is.

#4131. A simple liquid can stop cavities without drilling, major study confirms

A cavity doesn't always have to mean drilling, injections, or an uncomfortable visit to the dentist. A major clinical trial has confirmed that a simple, inexpensive liquid can stop tooth decay in young children, potentially preventing painful infections and the need for dental surgery.

The treatment, called silver diamine fluoride (SDF), takes only seconds to apply to each affected tooth. Rather than removing damaged tooth material and placing a filling, a dentist uses a tiny sponge tipped applicator to paint the liquid directly onto the cavity. The process requires no drilling, injections, or sedation.

The findings could have enormous implications for childhood dental care. Every year, thousands of young children in the United States visit emergency departments because of untreated cavities. Emergency physicians generally cannot provide the dental procedures needed to address the underlying problem, leaving some children with ongoing pain and infections. Others ultimately need dental surgery under general anesthesia, which makes them completely unconscious during the procedure.

Now, researchers led by the University of Michigan have produced compelling evidence that a much simpler approach can stop many cavities from getting worse.

Major Clinical Trial Confirms Silver Diamine Fluoride Works

Published in JAMA Pediatrics, the Phase III clinical trial included 830 children younger than 6. Participants were recruited through dental clinics, pediatric medical practices, and Head Start and Early Head Start programs in Michigan, New York, and Iowa.

Head Start and Early Head Start are federally supported programs that provide early childhood education and related services to eligible families.

The results showed that a solution containing 38% silver diamine fluoride successfully stopped decay in more than half of the affected baby teeth when treatment was provided at six month intervals.

Dentists refer to this as arresting tooth decay, meaning the damaged area stops deteriorating. Unlike a traditional filling, the treatment does not replace lost tooth structure. Instead, it helps prevent existing decay from progressing.

SDF works through the combined effects of silver and fluoride. Silver helps combat bacteria associated with tooth decay, while fluoride helps strengthen the mineral structure of teeth and resist further damage.

"This is a very effective and safe treatment -- even in children as young as 1," said Margherita Fontana, professor of dentistry at the University of Michigan School of Dentistry and the study's lead investigator.

A Simple Treatment Could Help Millions Avoid Dental Pain

Tooth decay is the most common chronic disease among children, affecting more than 40% of American children. Although cavities may initially cause few noticeable symptoms, untreated decay can eventually lead to severe toothaches, infections, problems eating and sleeping, missed school days, and repeated medical appointments.

For young children, conventional dental treatment can also be particularly challenging. Some are too young to remain still throughout a procedure, while others experience significant fear or anxiety.

SDF offers an alternative for many of these patients because it can be applied quickly without the equipment and procedures required for a traditional filling.

Fontana said the benefits could extend well beyond young children. Older adults, people with developmental or physical disabilities, individuals with severe dental anxiety, and patients who have difficulty obtaining conventional dental care could also benefit.

There is, however, one important tradeoff. SDF permanently turns the decayed portion of a treated tooth dark, which can be especially noticeable on front teeth. The discoloration is primarily cosmetic, but it may influence whether patients and families choose the treatment.

For many people, avoiding pain, drilling, or more invasive procedures may outweigh that drawback.

Why This Decades-Old Treatment Could Soon Reach More Patients

Silver diamine fluoride is not a newly invented substance. Dentists have successfully used it to manage cavities in numerous countries for decades.

In the United States, the FDA approved SDF as a medical device for treating tooth sensitivity in 2014. Dentists have also used it off-label to stop cavities, meaning they apply an authorized product for a purpose beyond its original approved indication.

What has been missing is large-scale clinical evidence from U.S. populations establishing its safety and effectiveness for treating tooth decay, a key step toward obtaining FDA approval for that specific use.

The University of Michigan trial was designed to help fill that gap.

"If we want more children and families to benefit from this treatment, we need rigorous evidence showing both that it works and that it's safe. From a public health perspective, if we want broader implementation across the United States, including in medical settings, we need carefully collected data in U.S. populations, and we now have that," Fontana said.

The investigation began in 2018 and continued despite the major disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic.

The researchers believe stronger clinical evidence could make SDF more widely available, including in pediatric medical settings where children often receive care long before their first dental appointment.

"In medicine, clinicians want high-quality evidence before changing practice," Fontana said. "It is important to have data they can refer to because young children often see pediatricians years before they ever visit a dentist, broader acceptance could allow many more cavities to be treated while a referral to a dental home is successful, and before they become painful, infected or require surgery."

A dental home refers to an ongoing relationship with a dental care provider who can deliver preventive care, treatment, and follow-up services.

Expanding access to SDF could allow more children to receive early intervention before relatively manageable cavities develop into serious dental problems.

FDA Approval Could Expand Access to Cavity Treatment

The trial brought together researchers from the University of Michigan, New York University, the University of Iowa, and Indiana University. The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research, part of the National Institutes of Health, provided more than $12 million in funding.

The product tested was Advantage Arrest 38% SDF, supplied by Elevate Oral Care.

The findings provide clinical trial evidence that the manufacturer can use in an application seeking FDA approval of SDF as a drug for arresting dental caries, the medical term for tooth decay.

Amr Moursi, a professor of pediatric dentistry at New York University College of Dentistry and a co-principal investigator, emphasized the potential importance of that regulatory change.

"Our results support FDA approval of SDF for managing arrest of tooth decay in young children. Removing SDF from off-label status would be an important innovation which could lead to increased utilization by providers, enhanced payments by insurers and more consistent product quality," said Moursi, a co-principal investigator on the study.

For some children, receiving another application of SDF every few months could be sufficient to keep a cavity under control until the affected baby tooth naturally falls out.

Adults could also benefit, either by using SDF to manage decay over a longer period or as a temporary solution until a filling or another restorative procedure becomes affordable or practical.

The treatment is not suitable for every cavity, and stopping decay does not necessarily eliminate the need for future dental work. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that a procedure requiring only seconds to perform could help many patients avoid more complicated treatment.

"For almost anyone, this can arrest the decay and stop the infection and the pain it causes," Fontana said. "This could benefit many people."

#4123. The secret to slower aging may be hiding in your gut

Scientists have uncovered a surprising connection between the bacteria living in the human gut and the speed at which the body ages. Researchers at the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa found that certain gut microbes are associated with slower biological aging, while others are linked to faster aging.

The study, published in Scientific Reports, found that differences in gut bacterial communities accounted for approximately 15% of the variation in participants' biological aging rates. The results suggest that the gut microbiome, the vast community of microorganisms living in the digestive system, may provide valuable clues about aging at the molecular level.

"This study advances our understanding of the relationship between the gut microbiome and biological aging," said senior author Alika K. Maunakea of the UH Mānoa John A. Burns School of Medicine. "We found that microbial patterns in the gut were significantly associated with molecular measures of aging, even independent of chronological age. These findings open important new directions for understanding how modifiable biological systems influence long-term health trajectories and longevity."

Scientists Identify Gut Bacteria Linked to Faster and Slower Aging

To investigate the relationship between gut bacteria and aging, researchers examined stool and blood samples collected from 123 adults across a wide range of ages. They identified the bacteria present in each participant's gut and compared those findings with blood-based molecular markers used to estimate the body's rate of aging.

These biological measurements differ from chronological age, which simply reflects how many years someone has been alive. Instead, they are designed to assess how quickly aging-related changes are occurring within the body.

The researchers found a notable association between the composition of the gut microbiome and DunedinPACE, an advanced biomarker that measures the pace of biological aging.

Some bacterial species stood out for their particularly strong relationships with aging. Bifidobacterium adolescentis was among the microbes most strongly associated with slower biological aging. In contrast, Succinivibrio dextrinosolvens showed one of the strongest associations with faster aging.

These differences suggest that particular patterns of gut bacteria may offer important information about how rapidly the body is aging.

How Diet, Lifestyle, and Environment May Influence Gut Health

Lead author Braden P. Kunihiro said the discovery adds to growing evidence that the gut microbiome could play an important role in precision health research, which aims to better understand individual differences in health and disease.

Unlike chronological age, the composition of the gut microbiome can change in response to everyday influences, including diet, lifestyle, and environmental conditions. This makes it a particularly interesting area of investigation for scientists studying healthy aging.

"The gut microbiome is highly responsive to lifestyle, diet, environment and other everyday exposures," Kunihiro said. "Our findings suggest that the microbiome may reflect important aspects of biological resilience and aging in ways that could eventually help inform future wellness and healthy aging strategies."

The research also included Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander participants, groups that have historically received limited representation in studies of aging and gut microorganisms.

Co-author Ruben Juarez explained that including these populations could help scientists investigate how social circumstances and environmental conditions leave lasting biological effects over the course of a person's life.

"The microbiome exists at the intersection of behavior, environment and human biology," Juarez said. "Studies like this help illuminate potential pathways through which lived experiences and social factors influence long-term health outcomes."

Could Changing Gut Bacteria Influence How We Age?

Although the findings reveal a meaningful relationship between gut bacteria and biological aging, researchers stress that the study does not establish cause and effect. In other words, finding certain microbes in people who age more slowly does not necessarily mean those bacteria are responsible for slowing the aging process.

Further studies will be needed to determine whether deliberately changing the gut microbiome can influence biological aging or improve long-term health.

Still, the findings offer a promising starting point for exploring how the microorganisms living inside us relate to the aging process and whether they could eventually help guide more personalized approaches to healthy aging.

"This is an important early step toward understanding the gut microbiome's relationship with healthy aging," Maunakea said. "Future research will help clarify the mechanisms involved and whether these microbial signatures may eventually contribute to precision approaches for promoting long-term health and wellness."
