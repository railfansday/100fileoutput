#5808. I bought a $199 Walmart e-bike, and what arrived shocked me

I’ve tested e-bikes that cost $10,000. I’ve tested e-bikes that cost $500. But until recently, I’d never tested a brand-new e-bike that cost less than a nice set of bicycle tires.

So when I stumbled across a $199 e-bike for sale through Walmart’s website, my curiosity got the better of me.

To be clear, I wasn’t expecting it to be good. I wasn’t even really expecting it to be acceptable. This was more of a morbid curiosity experiment: What exactly does $199 buy you in the world of e-bikes?

Apparently, the answer is: more than I expected, but also fewer pedals than I expected.

The bike in question is from a brand called “Bropath”, and it’s a tiny folding e-bike with 14-inch wheels, a 350/500W rear hub motor (disparity based on which marketing blurb you read), and a surprisingly large 576Wh battery.

On paper, that’s actually fairly impressive, especially for the rock-bottom price. The bike claims a top speed of 20 mph (32 km/h) and around 20 miles (32 km) of range. There’s a throttle, pedal assist, a suspension fork, mechanical disc brakes, a rear rack/basket that also houses the battery, and even a folding frame.

Things started getting interesting as soon as I pulled it out of the box, which you’ll definitely want to check out in my unboxing and testing video.

The bike was mostly assembled, but the battery basket wasn’t actually attached to the rack. There were holes where bolts clearly should have gone, but no bolts. I checked the box. I checked the packaging. I checked the instructions.

Eventually I resorted to temporarily ratchet-strapping the entire battery basket to the bike, which is not generally a step included in professional bicycle assembly.

Then, just as I thought I was ready for a test ride, I noticed another small problem: the bike didn’t have pedals.

Not “the pedals weren’t installed.” There simply weren’t any pedals in the box.

My guess is that an accessory box containing the pedals and battery mounting hardware was forgotten during packaging, or perhaps I received a re-packaged bike and they were left out. Fortunately, I live, eat, and breathe bikes, so my garage contains enough miscellaneous bicycle parts to make a small bike shop jealous. I eventually found two long bolts that fit the battery basket and a spare pair of pedals.

Before that, though, I did what any reasonable person would do: I rode it like a scooter with my feet resting on the bare cranks.

And that’s where things got surprising: the darn thing actually worked.

The little e-bike pulled away under throttle and moderately quickly got me cruising at nearly 20 mph (32 km/h). The brakes, which look like they were manufactured from approximately the same grade of metal as a soup can, actually stopped the bike reasonably well. The motor had decent power, the throttle worked, and once I installed pedals, the pedal assist worked too. On that note though: the single-speed design and small 14″ wheels meant that once the pedal assist kicked in, you’d quickly be going faster than your feet could keep up on the pedals. With that ghost pedaling in effect, the pedal assist quickly became useless, so don’t expect to ride this as a pedal-assist bike.

To test out the cargo capacity of the little battery basket, I took it on a shopping trip and managed to haul home several bags of groceries. I wouldn’t load it down with several cases of beer, but light duty grocery runs seems possible.

Is it a good e-bike? I wouldn’t go that far. But for $199, it was shockingly adequate. And I mean that as a compliment.

Yes, there are plenty of compromises. The suspension fork feels like a wet noodle, to the point that I’d probably rather have a rigid fork. The tiny 14-inch wheels combined with the cheap suspension make rough pavement and gravel paths fairly unpleasant. The seatpost is so short that even I can’t get a particularly good pedaling position, and I’m not exactly an NBA center.

And like I mentioned, the single-speed drivetrain also means that once the pedal assist gets you moving quickly, your legs can’t really keep up with the gearing. You wind up ghost pedaling while the motor does most of the work.

I don’t know what cells are inside that 576Wh battery, and I certainly wouldn’t assume the battery has the sort of third-party safety certification I’d prefer to see on an e-bike. I don’t know how many years – or months – the suspension fork will last. I don’t know how the folding mechanism will hold up after hundreds of cycles, because it definitely doesn’t look robust.

And if something does break, you’re probably largely on your own. At this price, you’re not paying for a nationwide dealer network or a robust customer service department. This is basically a white-labeled budget e-bike, and part of the $199 experience is accepting that you will likely become your own mechanic.

That’s why I wouldn’t recommend this bike to most people.

If you can afford to spend more on a quality e-bike from an established company with better components, a certified battery, replacement parts, and real customer support, I think you should. When people ask me what the most affordable e-bike is that is still good quality, I generally send them to a $799 Lectric XP Lite-series e-bike. By the time you start upgrading parts on a bike like this $199 Walmart bike, adding likes better fork, brakes, drivetrain, or other components, you could have simply bought a better e-bike in the first place.

For someone who genuinely only has $200 to spend and needs transportation, this thing is transportation.

It got me to the store. It got me to lunch. It carried groceries. It cruised at nearly 20 mph. Once I supplied the missing parts myself, everything basically worked (and I assume most people will be luckier than me and get all the parts in the box).

Good enough is better then nothing. It fills a gap. Most of China is riding around on sub $300 ebikes. Those folding bikes are getting very cheap I've seen them for around $50 fob. It's amazing they have brought the cost down for folding bikes so much 15 years ago they were several times more expensive then a regular bike. Now a folding ebike for less then the fancy brands and stores were trying to sell a folding bike. As long as a it last a few years and doesn't blow up it's a win for personal transportation.

I went into this experiment expecting a $199 e-bike to be almost comically terrible. Instead, I found something that was undeniably cheap, questionable in several places, and certainly not something I’d choose as my everyday e-bike – yet was also far more functional than it had any right to be at this price.

There’s a big difference between “good” and “good for $199.”

But somehow, against all odds, it might actually be the latter.

Set for a key procedural vote on the legislative vehicle when senators return on Nov. 9, the measure would make several politically dicey changes to bedrock environmental laws intended to make it easier to get more infrastructure projects built faster — from electric grid upgrades to oil drilling rigs.

That’s created a powerful alliance of clean energy and fossil fuel industry backers, a rarity in Washington that’s put some major lobbying muscle behind the yearslong effort culminating in last month’s deal between ordinarily strange political bedfellows.

The vast majority of power-generating facilities waiting years to be connected to the grid are wind and solar farms and battery storage systems, about 8,200 projects capable of over 2,000 gigawatts worth of generation and storage, according to the Energy Department’s Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory.

Senate Energy and Natural Resources ranking member Martin Heinrich, D-N.M., one of the key negotiators, often cites the SunZia Wind and Transmission Project in his home state as motivation for the bill. The project, with 916 wind turbines and 550 miles of transmission lines, took 17 years to complete.

Such facilities are typically built in remote areas away from population centers, requiring more land and a need to navigate a substantial state-federal permitting bureaucracy. By contrast, traditional coal- and natural gas-fired power plants were mainly built in the 20th century with decades of transmission infrastructure in place around them.

Even some environmental critics of the broader bill support the measure’s transmission title. One is the Natural Resources Defense Council, which said that part of the bill would “produce substantial and durable benefits, updating an aging power grid by allowing new investments.”

Large electric utilities, however, aren’t so sure about upending a regulatory process for new transmission lines that’s mostly been handled at the state and local level where they’ve enjoyed more sway. But the industry appears divided ahead of next month’s vote, which gives the bill’s supporters an opening.

Pressure to act is also coming from the midterm campaign trail. A Hart Research survey of battleground House districts in early October, commissioned by environmental groups, found that heating and electric bills were the third-most important issue for voters after food costs and gas prices.

In territory governed by PJM Interconnection, the regional transmission organization responsible for 13 states — including those with key congressional seats in play, like Michigan, Ohio and Pennsylvania — prices are up 41 percent on average.

States could still enact their own rights of first refusal, but codifying the federal ban is viewed as a step backward.

It was the summer of 1978. The Senate was holding hearings on whether to extend the deadline to ratify the Equal Rights Amendment, which was set to expire the following year. And Tina Smith, who had just finished her sophomore year at Stanford University, tumbled down the steps of the United States Capitol.

“I didn’t hurt myself, but I was absolutely mortified — and truly, every time I walk up and down the steps now, I think about that, and I say, “Be careful! Don’t fall!” the Minnesota Democrat says.

Smith was interning for California Sen. Alan Cranston at the time, running papers and manning the autopen to sign letters to constituents. Four decades later, she was appointed to the Senate herself and later secured her first full term in the 2020 elections.

Last year, Smith announced she would not seek another term. She said her decision hinged on spending more time with her family, including her four grandchildren and aging father.

“I expect to continue to be really engaged in my community, and I’m not exactly sure what that’s going to look like,” she says, adding she hopes to stay involved in combatting “income inequality and massive concentrations of wealth and power.”

Her dad has urged her to consider some leisure time, too: “He said that all sounds really good, Tina, but what are you going to do for fun?”

“[My husband] Archie and I bought a very nice tent for car camping, and we’ve got some cots that we can set up in the tent,” Smith says. “We did a lot of camping on our honeymoon many, many years ago, and then we kind of took a big 40-year break.”

Within Azerbaijan, rare unsanctioned demonstrations took place following the order’s implementation on Oct. 12. While arrests and internet restrictions curtailed them, small protests, bringing together both religious people and secular left-liberals, have continued.

At the same time, what began as veiled barbs and grumbling from Turkey’s ruling AKP has now burst into a stream of condemnation. On Oct. 12, Oktay Saral, chief advisor to Erdogan, and Leyla Sahin Usta, a deputy parliamentary leader for the AKP, expressed their outrage over the Azerbaijani policy in posts on X. Azerbaijani President Ilham Aliyev and Erdogan themselves have made passive-aggressive comments while stopping short of any explicit criticism.

But the rift goes beyond the hijab. Notably, the recent tensions coincided with Israeli President Isaac Herzog’s state visit to Baku. Azerbaijan’s close relationship with Israel, long a subject of discomfort for Turkey, has become a more salient issue amid Ankara’s own rapidly deteriorating relationship with the country. Israel has given Azerbaijan military equipment and surveillance technology. Azerbaijan has allegedly provided Israel with a crucial forward base for operations into Iran. This summer, the Israeli Knesset dropped a resolution recognizing the Armenian genocide—intended as a jab at Turkey—over Azerbaijani objections.

At a deeper level, there is also a fundamental disagreement about sovereignty. Erdogan and his allies imagine Turkey as a leading power in a world increasingly divided into quasi-imperial spheres of influence. In such a worldview, there’s no way for two states to maintain relations without hierarchy. Azerbaijan, though ruled by a glitzy petro-oligarch nomenklatura, is poorer than Turkey, and its population is roughly the same size as that of the European side of Istanbul.

The Azerbaijani government and its supporters present their country as a rising, independent middle power capable of balancing between difficult, larger neighbors. Growing self-confidence and a hunger for strategic autonomy among leaders in both Baku and Ankara lie squarely behind the clash over the hijab ban. Ironically, in this regard, it is Azerbaijan’s similarity to Turkey that has created strain between the two countries.

Despite the intense rhetoric of brotherhood and unity that could be heard from both countries in recent years, Turkish-Azerbaijani ties were not always so close. In March 1995, an Azerbaijani colonel launched a coup attempt against Azerbaijani President Heydar Aliyev with the active involvement of Turkish citizens, allegedly including pan-Turkist elements within the Turkish security establishment. These pan-Turkists, as well as their ideological allies in Azerbaijan, were apparently unhappy that the former Communist Party leader Aliyev had himself risen to power in the wake of the coup that deposed President Abulfaz Elchibey, a charismatic radical nationalist intellectual. Turkey’s then-President Suleyman Demirel tipped off Aliyev to the plot, but the incident nonetheless did damage to the two countries’ bilateral relationship.

Changes in Turkey and the region would help bring the two countries closer. In 2003, Heydar Aliyev died and his son, Ilham, assumed the presidency. Azerbaijan was at peace and increasingly prosperous. A pipeline, finished in 2006, brought Azerbaijani oil from the Caspian Sea fields to the Turkish port of Ceyhan on the eastern Mediterranean. Meanwhile, Erdogan, who took power in Turkey in 2003, was fighting his own battle against coup plotters and marginalizing members of the pan-Turkist movement.

By the time that the Azerbaijani army made its decisive move on Nagorno-Karabakh, Azerbaijani and Turkish leaders were eager to forget their complicated past. Azerbaijan’s 2020 victory over Armenians in the Second Nagorno-Karabakh War brought Azerbaijan and Turkey together as never before. The euphoria of that event was embodied in the victory parade of Dec. 10, 2020, which featured both Ilham Aliyev and Erdogan at the rostrum surrounded by Azerbaijani and Turkish flags.

Behind the public fanfare, though, unease remained. In private, Aliyev was wary of the growing power of Turkish Islamists. According to cables published by WikiLeaks, Aliyev stated to U.S. officials in 2009 that he had banished Turkish soap operas from Azerbaijani television broadcasts to avoid promoting women wearing headscarves. In 2015, Erdogan also began his own reconciliation with his country’s pan-Turkists, who eventually grew into some of his closest allies.

In 2021, the Azerbaijani Ministry of Education blocked an effort by pan-Turkists to build a school in Shusha, a city formerly controlled by Armenians. They were presumably wary of inviting the same pan-Turkist Idealist Hearths organization that participated in the 1995 coup attempt into the highly securitized and symbolically significant city.

Credit for the 2020 victory also became a touchy subject. In the summer of 2024, Erdogan stated, “as we entered Karabakh and Libya, we’ll do similarly to them,” referring to Israel. This prompted a scolding in Azerbaijan’s official state-owned gazette, which rejected the notion that Turkish or any other foreign soldiers had participated in Azerbaijani campaigns.

In early October, Aliyev suggested obliquely that foreign arms suppliers had engaged in profiteering in their dealings in Azerbaijan. Turkish firms, which prominently supplied drones and weapons to Azerbaijan in the buildup to the 2020 war, had just struck the latest of many deals to sell arms to Baku.

Azerbaijan occupies an ambiguous and increasingly uncomfortable space within expanding Turkish regional ambitions. Erdogan has typically made his first post-election international trips to Azerbaijan and northern Cyprus. Turks often refer to northern Cyprus as yavru vatan, or baby homeland—an outpost of the motherland that must be nurtured and defended. An AKP official tasked with relations with the Turkic world addressed conflict with Azerbaijan by stating, “we know how to discuss our problems within our own family setting—in a manner that befits us.”

As the Azerbaijani government is likely realizing, these family analogies are loaded. The internationally unrecognized Turkish Republic of North Cyprus is largely dependent on and subservient to Turkey. Last year, the Turkish Cypriot government tried unsuccessfully to lift a hijab ban to appease Ankara. Saral, the same Erdogan advisor who criticized Azerbaijan’s ban, responded to the effort’s failure by insulting the Turkish Cypriot prime minister.

In contrast to their effective domination of northern Cyprus, Turks have long shown meaningful deference to Baku on key issues of mutual concern. Turkish normalization with Armenia has been slow-walked for almost two decades out of Ankara’s respect for Azerbaijani sensitivities. Responding to the public break, higher-level figures in the Turkish government have also tried to assuage Baku. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said in a press conference that any differences would be resolved respectfully. Devlet Bahceli, Erdogan’s coalition partner, was even more deferential, calling for dialogue between “two strong states” that “span the same geography of the heart.”

Still, Turkey’s AKP elite have long been a better gauge for the direction of Turkish policy than government officials such as Fidan or political allies such as Bahceli. Baku clearly understands this and is increasingly worried by those elites’ unwavering ambition to make Turkey the predominant power in their shared region. The ruling New Azerbaijan Party’s response to Saral and Usta is instructive. Its statement addressed the hijab ban issue almost as an afterthought, instead focusing on defending Azerbaijan’s position on Israel-Palestine and attacking Turkish “hegemonic tendencies” in the Islamic world.

Azerbaijan’s state-controlled media was far less measured. One article, deleted shortly after it was posted, accused Fidan, who is also the former head of Turkish intelligence, of giving secret instructions to a Turkish nongovernmental organization during his visit to Baku. It accused Fidan and other Turks of “plans to interfere in internal affairs,” “intelligence operations,” “coup and chaos plans,” and “recruiting agents of influence,” and it invoked Turkish involvement in the 1995 coup attempt. The Turkish Embassy in Baku sternly condemned these allegations.

An outlet linked to SOCAR, the Azerbaijani state oil company, chimed in to protest Turkish “attempts to turn brotherhood into a tool of economic pressure” and objected that “Azerbaijan is not a vassal state” and would not accept Turkish “‘big brother’ dictates.” Other recent posts in state-aligned media have charged that Turks historically sought to establish an Armenian state at Azerbaijan’s expense and that AKP’s foreign policy is an existential threat to the national identity of the states comprising the “Turkic world.”

The hijab ban protests are hardly the work of an invisible Turkish hand, but Aliyev is right to worry about Turkish ambitions to shape his country’s future. One of Heydar Aliyev’s achievements was fortifying his authoritarian state against the designs of Iran, Russia, and Turkey. Ilham Aliyev appears to have things under control for the moment, but he is undoubtedly worried about any potential complications, particularly as he begins promoting his only son, the 29-year-old Heydar Ilhamoglu Aliyev, as a would-be successor. The sensational allegations of Turkish plotting against Azerbaijan, which follow an alleged pro-Russian coup attempt last year, reflect the profound insecurity of Azerbaijan’s rulers, who perceive manifold threats to their power from home and abroad.

The Azerbaijan-Turkey alliance will almost certainly survive this crisis—their economies and societies are now so deeply intertwined that a deeper break is difficult to imagine. The Azerbaijani government has recently insisted that more of the weapons that it buys be built in Azerbaijan, and that they will continue to fortify their country against alleged foreign interference. It remains to be seen how Turkey will respond to Azerbaijan’s decision to issue international arrest warrants for seven Turkish nationals, among them several Islamist commentators. The Turkish Constitution forbids the extradition of citizens outside of its obligations to the International Criminal Court.

This this may well lead to Baku unwinding Turkish cultural and religious influence and promoting secular “Azerbaijanism” as an alternative to Islamic conservatism and pan-Turkism. However events unfold, expect more bickering and frustration between these brotherly Turkic states.

But even if, as expected, Democrats win substantial victories in the House and possibly the Senate—and if Trump doesn’t follow through on his threats to call it fraud and somehow nullify the vote—the nation may still be heading into a political morass.

Rather than rallying around a common direction—other than opposition to Trump—the Democratic Party appears to lack anything like a coherent governing philosophy or compelling leadership. On the contrary, even on the verge of victory on Nov. 3, Democrats seem to be coming apart at the seams ideologically, making a party that is fractious in the best of times much more so today. It is racked by deep fissures between moderates and candidates allied with left-wing populist forces such as the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), including major divisions over taxes, health care, and Israel.

In a bruising Senate primary race in Michigan, for example, most establishment Democrats backed Rep. Haley Stevens, a moderate. The DSA-aligned candidate, Abdul El-Sayed, a public health official who ran on a progressive platform of Medicare for All and major new taxes on the rich, barely squeaked out a win. That costly battle, along with El-Sayed’s controversial criticism of Israel, has dampened his support among centrist voters in a tightening race against former Republican Rep. Mike Rogers. The state’s former Democratic chair, Lon Johnson, even urged Michiganders not to vote for El-Sayed, saying that doing so would surrender “our party to ideological extremism.”

“The Democrats have this incredible capacity to snatch defeat from the jaws of victory,” said Sidney Milkis, a political scientist at the University of Virginia. “I’m worried about voters’ reaction to the Democratic socialist insurgency. Michigan’s a really important race for control of the Senate. El-Sayed is a good candidate, but he has a lot of baggage.”

Many younger voters are openly dismissive of those who, in the absence of any other national figure, remain placeholders at the top of the party: Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer and House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries. (Schumer’s unfavorability ratings have soared since 2025, and Jeffries’s ratings are only slightly better.)

Much of the party’s direction depends on the size of the Democratic victory, if one occurs, especially in the House. “If it’s narrow, then Jeffries is going to be under pressure from those very far left folks, the DSA, but if it’s large, he can tell them to pound sand,” said Matt Bennett of the Third Way, a centrist Democratic think tank. “But either way, the identity of a party is forged by presidential politics not congressional. What will determine what Democrats are is the presidential primary process.”

That quadrennial beauty contest will get underway early next year. But it is difficult to identify a party savior with a centrist, electable national platform on the order of U.S. Presidents Barack Obama or Bill Clinton—or even Joe Biden.

Much of the charisma and star power grabbing headlines today comes from the far left—with new faces like New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani and others affiliated with the DSA—despite the broad mass of Democrats remaining closer to the center.

That could leave the party vulnerable to a 2028 GOP strategy of tarring Democrats as “communists” or too leftist to govern, possibly costing the election for whoever becomes the party standard-bearer. In the last week, Trump and other Republicans lost no time in painting the violent protests in France as a warning of what could happen in the United States at the hands of left-wing ideologues supporting open immigration and infected by Islamist radicalism.

“When you see burning steeples and raging fires in France, just remember that is what the radical Dumocrats want to do to America,” Trump said in Kansas on Oct. 19. (The French government said the demonstrations have more to do with high prices, especially soaring energy costs, driven up by Trump’s war in Iran.)

Still, most political experts would say there’s a long way to go to 2028. “If you’d asked me after the midterms in 1990, I wouldn’t have known Bill Clinton was coming along to save our party,” Bennett said. “Someone is going to emerge.”

Leading moderate Democratic candidates are clearly vying for a national platform. Among them is Georgia Sen. The fierce divisions within the Democratic Party suggest that even a victorious slate on Nov. 3 would likely end up, once again, defaulting to a least common denominator policy of attacking and investigating Trump.

“The problem is that the party overall enjoys such low levels of approval and trust, even among partisan Democrats themselves, and is characterized by a such a collective quality of leaderless and directionless rule … that the party has little capacity to handle internal disagreements,” said Sam Rosenfeld, a professor at Colgate University and co-author of The Hollow Parties: The Many Pasts and Disordered Present of American Party Politics.

“Democrats have spent a decade reacting to Republican provocations instead of building something worth believing in,” Sarah Longwell, a prominent anti-Trump Republican, wrote in her new book, How to Eat an Elephant: One Voter at a Time. “Ask swing voters what they want from the Democratic Party, and you’ll hear a version of this over and over: Tell us what you’re for.”

Hence next month’s midterm election is largely a battle of negative perceptions that will almost certainly be decided by which party’s reputation is less bad.

“It’s definitely true that the Democrats’ commanding position in the midterms does not reflect much in the way of underlying political strength and popularity,” Rosenfeld said.

There have been attempts to bridge the ideological gaps within the party, but none have taken hold. Few any longer tout an “abundance” agenda popularized last year by the pundit Ezra Klein or so-called supply-side progressivism. The movement to eliminate red tape preventing new infrastructure and construction has lost momentum in the face of public resistance to building data centers.

Another big question is whether Democrats will control the Senate, which remains a close-run affair. “That, to me, is really crucial,” Milkis said. “The Senate has a lot more control over the judiciary and appointments. That would make it much more difficult for Trump to appoint a new [U.S. Defense Secretary Pete] Hegseth.”

Many experts see the midterm elections as yet another “fulcrum point” for U.S. democracy. That resurrects an overused phrase popularized by Biden during the Trump era.

“This election offers a choice to voters about whether they want constitutional government or not,” said Justin Levitt, an election expert at Loyola Marymount University and former senior policy advisor for democracy and voting rights in the Biden administration.

In recent weeks, Trump’s poll numbers have been trending steadily down. A poll published on Oct. 21 by the New York Times and Siena University showed that 36 percent of likely voters approved of his performance, a new bottom for that survey, while the latest Reuters/Ipsos poll published on Oct. 19 found that Trump’s approval rating has plunged to a record low of 31 percent.

Those numbers have led some Republicans to fear a blowout like the 2006 midterms that gave Democrats both the House and Senate on the back of U.S. President George W. Bush’s deep unpopularity. As Trump has done in Iran, Bush was mired in a highly criticized and costly war of choice in Iraq, and he was dogged by various Republican political scandals and legislative defeats.

This time, a net gain of just four seats in both the House and Senate would do the trick of shifting power, erasing Republicans’ ​218-214 and 53-47 majorities.

Another key question is whether Trump’s plunging numbers—if they precipitate a GOP defeat like what Bush called his “thumping” in 2006—will begin to loosen his decade-long stranglehold on the Republican Party. The president himself has set the stage for his own comeuppance by telling voters to “pretend I’m on the ballot” and relentlessly campaigning across the country. But several establishment Republicans have split from him on Iran and his trade war with Canada. They have also criticized his recent moves to accommodate Russian President Vladimir Putin by waiving sanctions on diesel imports and withdrawing support for Ukraine.

Then there’s the question of whether Trump will even accept the results. “If there’s free and fair election and kind of a transfer of power, it can send a signal to Americans and people around the world that the United States is not destined for a Jan. 6 at every contested election,” said Matthew Dallek, a political scientist at George Washington University, referring to Trump’s attempt to overturn the 2020 election results by calling on his supporters to prevent the certification of Biden’s win (which he failed to do).

“At the same time, there is no single election that can repair the damage to America’s image in the world as a defender of democracy and reliable ally or repair the lost faith of Americans in the ability of government to improve their lives,” Dallek said.

Milkis added: “Right now, I think the country is in the most danger it’s been in since the Civil War, really, given the current polarization.”

Experts are projecting several possible outcomes along a spectrum marked by cautious hopefulness at one end and utter disaster at the other.

The hopeful scenario—call it “back from the brink”—would usher in huge Democratic victories, render Trump a badly weakened and lame-duck president, and perhaps offer a glimmer of a way out of Trumpism and back to constitutionalism.

The other end of the spectrum—call it “into the abyss”—could mean that Trump manages to follow through with plans to conduct massive vote manipulation and fraud, denial of election results, possibly even an attempt to declare a national emergency and send in troops under the Insurrection Act, which permits the use of regular military domestically.

“It seems evident that there are no guardrails erected around him at all,” Bennett said. “The craziest of the crazies are talking to him. Anything is possible, and Trump has proven that, in this term, he’s willing to do basically anything. I think the odds of mischief are high.”

But most experts think Trump will not be able to follow through on his worst threats. The courts have already denied his demands to hand over voter rolls, among other setbacks, and his SAVE America Act, which aimed to put new limits on voting, has gone nowhere in Congress. Even the conservative-dominated Supreme Court rejected his efforts to implement new mail-in voting restrictions.

“None of these methods of interference will work,” said Levitt, who has conducted a study on the issue. “The people who run elections are state and local officials, and when he screams about stuff, they’re not going to listen.”

Even if Trump tries to seize ballots, he added, it will be too late: They already will have been counted. Nor is there anything in federal or constitutional law that would allow Trump to declare martial law to prevent the vote.

“The only thing I’m actually afraid of is if we buy the narrative that we the people are not in charge,” Levitt said. “If the environment is so bad that people are scared of the threats and don’t show up at the polls. That’s possible. I don’t think it’s likely.”
