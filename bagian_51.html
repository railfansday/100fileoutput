#1015. Early-onset cancer rising worldwide: Causes, risk, prevention

Across Latin America, early-onset cancer rates have risen, especially of bowel, breast and cervical cancers. But it is a worldwide trend.

The numbers are striking. Some cancer rates have doubled in the past 20 years. It's just not yet clear whether we're seeing a rise in actual cases or whether we're seeing more early-stage cases because of better awareness, diagnosis and treatment in hospitals.

Take, for example, colorectal (or bowel) cancer: In 2001, about one in every 26,000 people under 50 in England were diagnosed with it each year. By 2023, the rate of diagnoses was about one in 12,000. Twice what it was at the turn of the century.

That's according to the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF), an international network of charities, which analyzed registry data from England, the United States and the Netherlands. It found the number of new cancer cases had risen for seven cancers in England alone.

So, rates have doubled, but the numbers overall are still small, said Amy Berrington de Gonzalez, a professor of clinical epidemiology at the Institute of Cancer Research in London.

"Cancer is still very uncommon among young people," Berrington de Gonzalez told DW. "Three times a small number is still a small number."

But she and other scientists still want to know why this is happening, because it is a worldwide trend. Latin America is a case in point.

A 2025 study analyzed registry data from 50 countries and territories up to 2017. It found early-onset colorectal cancer rising in 27 of those regions.

In Latin America, rates rose in Chile, Argentina, Ecuador, Costa Rica and Puerto Rico. Chile's rise — at almost 4% per year — was among the highest.

In Brazil, the evidence comes directly from hospitals. At the A.C. Camargo Cancer Center in Sao Paulo, the number of colorectal cancer patients under 50 grew by 7.6% per year between 2000 and 2023. That's a five-fold increase.

The number of patients over 50 grew even faster — at 8.1% — or about six-fold.

But since these figures came from a single hospital, they do not show whether cases are rising across Brazil. They may only reflect that this one hospital is treating more patients.

"We need better cancer registration systems," said Berrington de Gonzalez, "especially in countries like Brazil and Mexico, where obesity rates have also been increasing."

Marc Gunter, professor of cancer epidemiology at Imperial College London, said he expected Latin America to follow richer countries as diets there become more Westernized and lifestyles more sedentary. "I think it's already happening," he said.

In another study in 2025, involving 42 countries, Berrington de Gonzalez compared data from two age groups — 20 to 49 years and 50 years and older — between 2003 and 2017.

Six cancers — thyroid, breast, colorectal, kidney, uterine and leukemia — had risen among the younger groups in most countries. All but colorectal cancer were seen to rise in older adults, too.

In fact, colorectal cancer stood out. In about a third of the countries, it rose faster in younger adults than in older ones. "It is the one cancer that seems to be increasing in younger people whilst decreasing in older people," said Berrington de Gonzalez.

This has consequences for research. If cancers are rising in all age groups, the causes are likely to be widespread, so studies looking only at young people risk chasing complicated explanations with too little data to test them, she said.

A US study of pancreatic cancer in adults aged 15 to 39 years found the rise was almost entirely made up of early-stage tumors.

The authors said this suggested that instead of the number of cancer cases going up, it was the number of early diagnoses that had gone up. And that can be explained by better awareness in the community, with people getting checked more often.

But the data for colorectal cancer looks different: Some US studies show it rising in advanced stages of the disease as well, which suggests an increase in the number of cases, not just the number of diagnoses.

Obesity is an established cause of at least 13 cancers, according to the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).

According to a 2021 review of existing studies, young adults with obesity were almost twice as likely to develop colorectal cancer before the age of 50.

Obesity has also spread fastest among the young. The WHO reports that obesity rates have doubled among adults since the 1990s and quadrupled among adolescents.

Some also say ultra-processed foods and microplastics cause cancer. But Gunter said the evidence was inconclusive.

"There's a lot of hype," Gunter said, adding that more research was needed. Berrington de Gonzalez agreed: "Diets have changed, but we don't yet know the role they might play."

Gunter said individuals should not be blamed for living in societies built around convenience foods and desk jobs, and that lasting change needed public health policy.

But he also said that young people could lower their cancer risk by maintaining a healthy weight, moving more, eating more fiber and cutting back on processed meat.

"Going for a walk, being more physically active, eating more fruits and vegetables," he said. "Those things are maybe not so trendy, but they are really important."

For a 30-year-old reading this, Berrington de Gonzalez's message was one of reassurance: Cancer at a young age remains relatively rare.

#1005. 2026 Nobel Prize winner Anne Carson: Where to start reading

The works of this year's Nobel Prize-winning poet and essayist Anne Carson can feel slightly daunting. Here are some keys to understanding her universe.

This quote is from the long, lyrical poem "The Glass Essay" published in Anne Carson's 1995 collection "Glass, Irony and God" — an entry point into the works of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature laureate.

In the poem, the narrator explores the burden of memory and obsessive desire after a failed love affair, all while regularly reflecting on Emily Brontë — an author renowned for her intense literary depiction of destructive and passionate love in "Wuthering Heights."

The poem brings several of Carson's recurring obsessions together into one work. She has repeatedly written about desire, abandonment and jealousy, and has often used other literary figures as emotional doubles, as historical anchor points to her ideas.

In a way, "The Glass Essay" encapsulates Carson's method; the Canadian poet, essayist, translator and classicist has often intellectualized emotional themes by intertwining her personal reflections with analysis, etymology, translation, literary history and philosophical thought.

Another trademark of her work is the genre fluidity found in "The Glass Essay," which — as the title indicates — is partly a literary criticism essay, even though it is also a poem due to its form.

"Almost all her literary production has centered upon the intersection between the philologist's obsession with words and the poet's desire for new meaning," noted Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, in his description of the Nobel laureate on October 8.

Born in the Canadian city of Toronto, Carson first developed her fascination for ancient Greek myths as a teenager. She told literary journal The Brick that she spotted a bilingual edition of "Sappho" by Willis Barnstone in the display of a bookstore of a shopping mall in Ontario, and that had led her to ask a high school Latin teacher to teach her ancient Greek.

She went on to publish her doctoral thesis in Classics, entitled "Odi et Amo Ergo Sum" (I hate and I love, therefore I am).

Carson's first book of criticism, "Eros the Bittersweet," was published in 1986. It lays the foundation of her approach, as she draws on ancient Greek writers such as Sappho and Plato to examine how desire — and the obstacles between the lover and the beloved — creates emotional tension.

The book suggests that the ache of wanting is not separate from love; it is one of the things that makes desire so powerful.

Because "Eros the Bittersweet" moves between Greek poetry, philosophy and literary criticism and personal interpretations, it is not considered a conventionally easy read. A more immediately engaging work and good starting point to discover Carson is the verse novel "Autobiography of Red," published in 1998.

"Autobiography of Red" was inspired by the ancient Greek myth of Herakles (or Hercules) and the red-winged monster Geryon, which the hero kills as his 10th task in his series of 12 monumental labors.

An otherwise minor figure from mythology, Geryon is turned into Herakles' lover in Carson's book. She reimagines the myth as a modern, queer coming-of-age story, with the monster experiencing love, jealousy, sexuality and heartbreak.

"We all feel we're monsters most of the time," explained Carson.

The work is accessible, funny and moving, and no deep knowledge of Greek mythology is required to enjoy it.

The key to reading Carson is not to expect every connection to be immediately clear. Her books reward curiosity rather than certainty, and her sprawling, non-linear web of knowledge can be compared to going down a hyperlinked rabbit hole of narrative ideas.

"Men in the Off Hours" (2000) is another intellectually playful collection of poems. Here Carson collides the modern with the ancient, with references to figures as diverse as Beethoven, Virginia Woolf, Emily Dickinson, Jean-Luc Godard, Sigmund Freud, Homer and Hokusai.

The poems bring together fragments of history, mythology and ordinary life, allowing unexpected emotional and intellectual connections to emerge.

In the spoken and sung performance piece "Norma Jeane Baker of Troy" (2019), Carson similarly brings together classical and pop culture, reimagining the story of Helen of Troy alongside the life and cultural image of Marilyn Monroe.

How do people become the victims of desires they cannot control? This is a core theme Carson explores in "The Beauty of the Husband: A Fictional Essay in 29 Tangos," published in 2001.

It features the Canadian author's wry humor in a powerful and moving way, with erotic, painful and heartbreaking scenes from a doomed marriage: a 30-year obsession with a pathological liar whose singular redeeming quality is his sheer physical radiance.

The collection of poetry, which won her the T.S. Eliot Prize, is dedicated to the English Romantic poet John Keats, challenging his famous aphorism, "Beauty is truth."

#996. Finland, the world's happiest nation, faces a glum winter

Finns have many reasons to feel content, but the country's pile of debt is not one of them. Russia's war in Ukraine has added to the fiscal burden. Can the Nordic nation absorb another shock?

There's an uneasy mood in Finland, the country that's ranked the world's happiest for nine consecutive years. A colder-than-usual winter is on the way and so is the toughest austerity budget in years.

With an election due in April, the argument is no longer how much to cut, but which services, benefits and pensions will face the biggest cuts.

Finland's debt and deficit levels are in their worst state since the 1990s, when a banking crash and the collapse of the Soviet Union — a key export partner — pushed the Nordic nation into its own Great Depression.

Last month, Finland's national statistical institution confirmed that the national debt had reached 90.3% of gross domestic product (GDP) in the second quarter. Before the pandemic, that figure was around 65%.

Trouble to the east is, again, part of the reason. Finland ramped up defense spending after joining NATO a year after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Helsinki, which used to rely on Moscow for about a third of its energy, also faced much higher costs as it transitioned to other suppliers.

The Finnish government has spent more than it earns since the 2008/9 global financial crisis, partly due to an aging population and sluggish economic growth. Last month, the State Treasury projected the fiscal deficit would reach 4.2% of GDP in 2026.

Brussels has piled pressure on Helsinki to bring down the borrowing gap, as European Union rules require member states to stay below 3% of GDP. In January, the European Council opened an excessive deficit procedure and gave Finland until the end of 2028 to reach the desired target.

Prime Minister Petteri Orpo's government, which took office in June 2023, said it would aim to save about €9 billion ($10.1 billion) during this parliamentary term. But whoever wins the next election will have to cut much deeper, economists warn.

"The amounts currently estimated range from €8 to €11 billion," Jarkko Kivisto, an advisor to the Bank of Finland's monetary policy and research department, told DW.

Earlier this year, all but one political party agreed to support a debt brake, where the next government commits to tightening the country's deficit to about 2% of GDP by 2031.

As a mathematician who uses AI tools for my own research, I can see that the field has fundamentally transformed. The challenges associated with this new way of doing maths are already evident. Many AI-generated results are not described clearly enough to meet quality standards for publication. Confined to pure mathematics, this ‘proof indigestion’ might be a minor concern. For example, complex AI-generated models are already used to some extent to determine who qualifies for a bank loan and to measure financial risk in markets. The defining image of Paris Saint-Germain’s 5-0 win over Inter in the 2025 Champions League final was not the trophy lift, or any one of the goal celebrations, but Dembélé, poised on the edge of the opposition penalty area, with hunched demeanour and furrowed brow, his face a picture of focus, ready to press. Just how did he get to this point?

One lesson to learn from the Dembélé story: the path to greatness is not always linear. He started off like a train, winning France’s young player of the year award in his first season as a professional. The following season, he helped Borussia Dortmund win the German Cup.

Around that time, in the course of research for my book, Edge, I spoke to Thomas Tuchel, his coach at Dortmund. The A category stood for “aggressive-motivated”, and referred to players motivated by individual glory and awards (in Tuchel’s eyes, this was not necessarily a negative: think Neymar at Paris).

B stood for “binding-motivated”, those players who love being part of the group and bringing people together (often captains, such as César Azpilicueta at Chelsea).

C stood for “curious-motivated”. These were the players capable of greatness, who needed to be trained in a slightly different way. Their talent could take them far; and on a good day, they were capable of anything. Tuchel loved coaching these players. Dembélé, he felt, was a C.

But the journey was not always smooth. In 2017, he moved to Barcelona, who were flush with money from selling Neymar for €222m (£194m). It was a sliding doors moment. That same summer, Barcelona changed coach: in came Ernesto Valverde; out went Luis Enrique. In six injury-laden years at Barcelona, Dembélé started only a third of the league games. He scored a total of 24 league goals. His most memorable moment might have been missing a last-minute one-on-one in the 2019 Champions League semi-final against Liverpool, when Barcelona were already 3-0 up (remember what happened next? #964. Author or artificial intelligence? Literature in crisis

AI is a hot topic at this year's Frankfurt Book Fair. An international literary scandal has raised questions about machine-generated texts.

It's the hot-button topic at the Frankfurt Book Fair: How profoundly will artificial intelligence (AI) transform the book industry, and how can this transformation be regulated?

Given the warnings about the technology's long-term risks to humanity as a whole, such debates might appear rather narrow in scope. In reality, however, they concern very concrete issues — copyright protection, the power of tech giants and the future of creativity. Many agree that the political measures taken don't go far enough.

This year, however, another aspect has entered the debate: Can we actually recognize when a text has been written with the help of artificial intelligence, and if so, can this be proven?

These are some of the questions raised by the current scandal surrounding Haitian-Canadian author Thelyson Orelien and his debut novel, "C'etait ca ou mourir," (This title means "It Was Either That or Die" in English but an English translation has not yet been published), which was published in Canada in the spring.

The book about global migration was universally acclaimed, with translation rights sold in more than 20 languages. Soon after the book was published in France, it was long-listed for the prestigious Prix Goncourt.

But then, all of a sudden, allegations emerged that the book had been written almost entirely using AI. Orelien, a rising literary star, found himself branded a fraud despite denying all the allegations. His nomination for the Goncourt was withdrawn.

So, who should we believe? An author or an AI that claims to have identified AI-generated writing?

"We will have to learn to live with doubt," said Hannes Bajohr, a professor of German literature at the University of California, Berkeley.

Bajohr has spent years examining how AI is changing both writing and reading. His prediction: If the origins of texts can no longer be reliably established, we will have to evaluate them differently. What matters will be their quality alone, rather than whether AI was used in the writing process.

Nina George, a bestselling author and the honorary president of the European Writers' Council, is unwilling to accept this.

At the Frankfurt Book Fair, she was awarded the Friedrich Perthes Prize in recognition of her commitment to issues such as AI and copyright reform.

George has addressed the European Parliament and the lower house of the German parliament, speaking and writing tirelessly about the dangers of AI models. It is perverse, she said, that an author should suddenly have to prove that their writing is the creation of a human. The focus of her campaigns are the tech corporations' self-serving mentality and copyright protection.

But what if an author sees AI not as an enemy but as a tool that makes writing easier?

US author and artist Luke Stoffel deliberately used AI in his memoir, "How To Win One Million Dollars and Shit Glitter," and was open about doing so.

"I'm dyslexic," he said in a video interview. "I've always written and made up stories, but I never dared to take them any further."

It was only with the help of AI, he said, that he was able to fulfill his dream. The technology drew his attention to weaknesses in his writing and suggested a marketing strategy that actually worked.

In May 2026, he received an award from the Independent Book Publishers Association for the book.

For South African author Coral Hart, AI is actually the foundation of her business model. Publishing under various pseudonyms, each year she releases more than 200 different novels, spanning the genres of romance, thriller and mystery.

Hart sees herself as an ideas person, as well as an editor and publisher. Although none of her books has yet become a bestseller, the business is profitable, she said, adding that each book reaches more than 5,000 people.

Stoffel considers the entire debate hypocritical and points out that many publishers have been working with AI tools for some time. He's certain that "right now, a million-copy bestseller is being printed that was created with the help of AI. The author won't admit it, and then there'll be a scandal."

It is entirely possible that the scandal surrounding Thelyson Orelien is precisely that story. But we may never know for sure.

For now, the battle against the tech giants continues, some of which are themselves increasingly concerned about losing control.

At the very start of the Frankfurt fair, the German Publishers and Booksellers Association issued an appeal to policymakers in Berlin and Brussels, calling for stricter regulation of artificial intelligence, which they said is more urgent than ever. More than 140 publishers have signed it.

But for now, it remains just an appeal. And the question of what authors themselves should be permitted to do with AI will undoubtedly continue to fuel debates in the future.

#951. 'Ragebait' is the 2026 German Youth Word of the Year

Already named the 2025 Oxford Word of the Year, "rage bait" has also been widely adopted in German slang.

The 2026 Youth Word of the Year, "Ragebait," was announced on Saturday at the Frankfurt Book Fair by publisher PONS Langenscheidt.

The "Jugendwort des Jahres," or German Youth Word of the Year, is an annual campaign that documents the popularity of new slang expressions.

"Ragebait" (in English: rage bait) refers to social media content that is deliberately designed to make people angry, frustrated or morally outraged.

It aims to drive engagement, since the algorithms of platforms like Instagram, TikTok and X reward high-energy interaction. A post that generates a heated debate through angry comments, or that is widely shared due to the emotional reaction it provokes, counts as high engagement, which leads the platforms to push the content to more feeds.

"Ragebait" was on a shortlist of three words for the 2026 Youth Word of the Year, alongside "Peak" and "Crashout."

All three terms are borrowed from English, demonstrating once again the language's significant influence on German youth culture.

Around 1 million young people, aged 11 to 20, have voted since August 28 for their favorite term.

"Ragebait" obtained 36.5% of the votes, followed by 33.8% for "Peak" and 29.7% for "Crashout."

"The close result shows that all three terms have a firm place in the vocabulary of generations Z and Alpha," said Artur Raiser, marketing manager at PONS Langenscheidt, in a press statement. "'Ragebait' held a slight lead from the start," but only by a narrow margin throughout the voting period, he added.

"It remained an exciting race for us at the publishing house, too, until the very last moment," said Raiser

"Crashout" (used by young Germans as "crashout gehen," or "going crash out") originally comes from the English "to crash out," which traditionally means to fall asleep very quickly due to exhaustion.

But in newer internet slang, popularized on gaming and social media platforms, "crashing out" means having an intense emotional breakdown, losing control of one's temper or reacting with sudden, uncontrolled anger or stress. It is often used with a touch of humor, to emphasize that the reaction might be somewhat exaggerated.

As for the other shortlisted word, "Peak," it refers to something that is the absolute best. In German youth slang, it functions like an adjective: "That was peak football" or "that is peak comedy."

PONS Langenscheidt, a publishing company that specializes in language reference works, has been selecting the German Youth Word of the Year since 2008 — originally via a jury, and since 2020 through an online vote. Offensive, racist or discriminatory terms are not permitted. The multi-stage public nomination and voting process for next year's word will start in the summer of 2027.

"Das crazy" was selected last year, while "Aura" was the Youth Word of the Year 2024.

A powerful gene-editing technique has been used to edit DNA directly in a person’s body — the first known instance of such a treatment.

The milestone treatment is part of a clinical trial of prime editing, a technique that can alter DNA with more precision than standard CRISPR–Cas9 methods can. In this case, prime editing was used to treat a condition called Wilson’s disease by rewriting DNA in liver cells, to erase a mutation that prevents them from eliminating excess copper.

Prime Medicine, a biotechnology company in Cambridge, Massachusetts, announced that it had treated the first person in its trial earlier this month. It expects to announce the first results from the study next year.

“I never dreamed that in my lifetime I would see this applied to my rare disease,” says Rhonda Rowland, president of the Wilson Disease Association in Atlanta, Georgia. “It’s going to be a really exciting year.”

In launching the trial, Prime Medicine joins a host of companies aiming to engineer cells directly in the body, rather than using the standard method of extracting a person’s cells, editing their DNA in a laboratory and then reinjecting the altered cells. Rewriting DNA in the body, or in vivo, is appealing because manufacturing and administering the treatment might be much cheaper and faster than making a bespoke therapy for each person from their own cells.

But in vivo approaches have their own challenges, says Krishanu Saha, a biomedical engineer at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. “It depends on what types of risks you’re willing to accept,” he says.

Wilson’s disease is caused by mutations in a gene called ATP7B, which encodes a protein that transports excess copper out of the liver. If left untreated, the resulting build-up of copper can cause liver and nerve damage, as well as neurological disorders.

People with Wilson’s disease are eager for a one-and-done treatment for their condition, says Rowland, rather than the current, complicated regimen of copper-clearing molecules and other medicines. And gene editing could, in theory, provide a lasting solution by correcting the faulty ATP7B gene.

So far, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved only one gene-editing therapy, called Casgevy, which is used to treat two inherited blood disorders. Casgevy relies on CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing, which is often used to disable genes rather than to rewrite their sequences.

Another form of gene editing, called base editing, can rewrite individual DNA letters, but cannot make the particular corrections needed to fix the most common ATP7B mutations. That left prime editing, a more complex and versatile system that can insert, delete and rewrite segments of DNA. In another clinical trial, Prime Medicine used the technique to treat two people with a rare immune disorder, by rewriting DNA in each participant’s blood stem cells outside of the body and then injecting them back in.

So far, no gene-editing therapies have been approved for use in vivo. But the FDA is expected to issue a decision early next year about an in vivo CRISPR–Cas9 therapy for a condition called hereditary angioedema, which causes severe swelling.

#942. German 'lawfare': When lawsuits become a political weapon

The Left Party is suing two government ministers for accusing them of antisemitism, while Chancellor Friedrich Merz has been sued several times by various parties. Is German politics descending into "lawfare?"

A verbal slip, an attempted quip, a lawsuit: Things can move fast in the rancorous atmosphere that currently rules German politics.

The brief exchange happened on a political TV talk show shortly after the Berlin election on September 20, won by the socialist Left Party. The show's host, Maybrit Illner, quickly corrected herself after accidentally saying of the Left Party's Berlin mayoral candidate, "Elif Eralp says she is an antisemite…. She is not an antisemite."

One of her guests, Federal Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt, of the conservative Christian Social Union (CSU), responded immediately: "Na doch" (or, "well, yes she is"). In the angry fallout, the Left Party's lawyers jumped on these two words, and have filed a lawsuit, arguing that this amounted to defamation of Eralp, as it falsely suggested that the Left Party politician did indeed consider herself antisemitic.

Not only that, the suit stated, Dobrindt's remark would "undermine her integrity as a political officeholder" and hinder her attempts to form a government in Berlin.

It was not the first time the Left Party has got its lawyers involved following its election victory, as it gets bombarded by accusations of antisemitism from the center right, especially over an incident at an election campaign event in Berlin when a rapper chanted "Death to the IDF" — the Israel Defense Forces, or the Israeli army.

Economy Minister Katherina Reiche also angered the socialist party by giving a speech in which she said that the party was now "openly embracing antisemitism." A video of the speech was posted on the Economy Ministry's Instagram account.

The lawsuit has become an increasingly popular weapon in German politics in recent years, usually by deploying sections 185, 186 and 187 of the German Criminal Code, which cover offenses including insult, defamation and "intentional defamation," which means knowingly asserting or disseminating untrue facts about others. Theoretically, the punishment can be a fine or up to a year in prison.

In September 2025, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) pressed defamation charges against Chancellor Friedrich Merz after he said that the far-right party's policy of remigration amounted to "ethnic cleansing."

Similarly, the Green Party in Berlin brought hate speech charges against Merz last year, when he said "we still have this problem in the cityscape" when describing the presence of immigrants.

In another case, the Green Party's own Robert Habeck was sued in 2024 by the Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) for claiming that the new upstart party was "buying votes" on the internet by building up "troll armies." Prosecutors dropped the case after Habeck agreed to pay €12,000 ($13,500) to three charities without admitting any wrongdoing, on the grounds that there was only limited public interest in a criminal procedure.

But charges like these, especially when politicians claim defamation, have little chance of success. "Prosecutors usually dismiss such cases very quickly due to a lack of sufficient grounds for suspicion," media lawyer Christian Solmecke, of the firm WBS Legal, told DW in an email.

"The Federal Constitutional Court has consistently held that, in the political arena, the boundaries of what is permissible are significantly wider than in the private sphere," Solmecke added. "Anyone who voluntarily enters the political arena must also be prepared to endure sharp, polemical, exaggerated and hurtful criticism."

Felix Zimmermann, editor-in-chief of the legal affairs outlet Legal Tribune Online (LTO), agrees, because German law makes a careful distinction between an opinion and a false statement. "'Antisemitic' or 'not antisemitic' is an opinion, especially at the moment, when people have very different definitions of it," Zimmermann told DW.

That hasn't deterred politicians from trying it more and more often. "We are undoubtedly observing a new level of intensity and scale of this practice," said Solmecke. "Political culture has become harsher and more polarized. The threshold for no longer merely engaging political opponents through debate, but instead outright criminalizing them, has dropped dramatically."

The danger, Solmecke argues, is that this often amounts to the "misuse" of law enforcement agencies as political instruments.

But lawfare appears to be effective as a political tactic, not least because it's a guaranteed headline. "From the point of view of the parties filing the complaints, they are highly effective as a PR strategy," said Solmecke. "The political benefit arises entirely independently of the legal outcome."

In the Left Party's case, Zimmermann thinks it certainly pays to show you won't stand to be called antisemitic — not least because Eralp has repeatedly underlined her commitment to the Jewish community in Berlin.

"I think it's mainly about the fact that the Left Party wants to form a coalition in Berlin and Ms. Eralp wants to become mayor," Zimmermann said. "So of course it's good if you're insulted to challenge it, as a signal."

The motion against Katherina Reiche, on the other hand, may have much better chances — because she chose to post her inflammatory speech on the Economy Ministry's official social media channels. This could, Zimmermann said, well be seen as a violation of the "principle of neutrality," a constitutional rule that forbids the state from vilifying any particular political party.

"That is of course difficult for a politician, because if they say it on their own account, they're allowed, but if they say it on the ministry's account, they're not allowed," said Zimmermann. "It's a little absurd, because you're effectively splitting one person into two."

But even though the Left Party filed the complaint against Reiche as an urgent motion, the Constitutional Court may take several weeks, if not months, to actually decide the case — by which time the coalition negotiations in Berlin may have long since finished and Reiche's speech has had its effect.

The consequences for Reiche, in other words, will be limited — even if her ministry is forced to take down the post and the Left Party can eventually claim a victory.

#931. Scientists find a way to fight Alzheimer’s brain damage without removing toxic tau

Protecting brain cells from destruction remains one of the biggest challenges in treating Alzheimer's disease and related neurodegenerative conditions. These disorders, known as tauopathies, involve the buildup of abnormal tangles of a protein called tau inside the brain. As the tangles accumulate, nearby neurons begin to die. However, growing evidence suggests that tau itself may not be responsible for most of the damage. Instead, the immune system's reaction to the protein appears to play a major role in the loss of brain cells, memory, and independence.

Scientists at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis have now identified a promising strategy for interrupting this damaging immune response. In mice with tau accumulation similar to that seen in Alzheimer's, researchers used an antibody to block a protein called CXCR3, which helps immune cells known as T cells enter the brain. After several months of treatment, the number of T cells inside the brain had fallen by approximately half.

The results were striking. Compared with untreated mice, animals receiving the antibody retained roughly 40% more tissue in brain regions involved in memory and performed better on a memory test. These improvements occurred even though the amount of accumulated tau remained unchanged.

The findings were published September 28 in the journal Neuron.

"In tauopathies, including Alzheimer's disease, there's no treatment right now that actually decreases neurodegeneration," said David M. Holtzman, MD, senior author of the study and the Barbara Burton and Reuben M. Morriss III Distinguished Professor in the Department of Neurology at WashU Medicine. "If we can show that we're really decreasing brain cell death, it's certainly worth trying to pursue that pathway to prevent the most devastating consequences of these diseases."

Why Immune Cells May Be a Key to Alzheimer's Brain Damage

Current Alzheimer's treatments primarily focus on removing amyloid, another protein associated with the disease. Two approved medications, lecanemab and donanemab, target amyloid plaques that develop between brain cells during the early stages of Alzheimer's. These deposits interfere with communication between neurons, contribute to the formation of tau tangles inside cells, and ultimately lead to neuronal death.

Although these medications can slow cognitive decline, they have not been shown to prevent brain cells from dying. They also offer no treatment for primary tauopathies, conditions in which tau accumulates without the presence of amyloid. Alzheimer's is classified as a secondary tauopathy because both abnormal amyloid and tau are present.

Holtzman's laboratory has been investigating whether the immune system offers another way to address these diseases. In a 2023 study published in Nature, the researchers discovered that T cells enter the brains of mice with abnormal tau accumulation, concentrating in regions containing the most tau and contributing to neurodegeneration.

A related study published earlier this month in Nature Neuroscience provided another important clue. It showed that the signals directing these T cells originate in lymph nodes located outside the brain.

"For this therapeutic approach, if it is safe, you wouldn't have to design the drug to get into the brain -- which is a big deal since most molecules don't cross the blood-brain barrier well -- and you don't have to get rid of the tau to get this therapeutic effect."

Scientists Identify the Pathway That Guides Immune Cells Into the Brain

Despite these earlier discoveries, researchers still needed to understand how activated T cells found their way into the brain.

T cells navigate through the body by following chemical signals known as chemokines. Earlier experiments in Holtzman's laboratory had identified unusually high levels of one such signal, CXCL10, in mice with tau accumulation. Other researchers had also reported elevated CXCL10 levels in people with Alzheimer's disease.

Activated T cells carry a surface receptor called CXCR3 that recognizes CXCL10, allowing the cells to follow its chemical signals. This relationship suggested that the CXCL10 and CXCR3 pathway might be responsible for guiding harmful T cells into the brain.

To investigate, the researchers studied mice that developed abnormal tau deposits resembling those associated with Alzheimer's. The team included co-senior author Jason Ulrich, PhD, a professor of neurology at WashU Medicine, and first author Joshua T. Emmerson, PhD, a postdoctoral researcher in Holtzman's laboratory.

The experiments revealed that T cells could not infiltrate the brain when mice lacked either CXCL10 or its matching receptor, CXCR3, on T cells. This protection persisted even when the scientists deliberately triggered inflammation to stimulate an immune response.

Experimental Treatment Preserves Brain Tissue and Memory

With the pathway identified, the team tested whether blocking CXCR3 with an antibody could reduce brain damage.

The researchers selected young mice that already had tau deposits in their brains but had not yet experienced substantial loss of brain cells. They administered the antibody once every five days for three and a half months.

By the end of the experiment, treated mice had approximately half as many T cells in their brains as untreated animals. They also showed less evidence of neuronal injury and retained substantially more brain tissue, including roughly 40% more tissue in memory-related regions. Their performance on a memory test was also better.

Importantly, these protective effects occurred without any detectable reduction in tau tangles. The treated and untreated mice had similar amounts of abnormal tau, suggesting that limiting the immune response could reduce neurodegeneration even when the underlying protein deposits remain.

Another finding could have major implications for future drug development. The antibody reached the boundary of the brain but did not enter the brain tissue itself.

This suggests that it may be possible to reduce neurodegeneration by targeting immune activity from outside the brain. Such an approach could avoid one of the major difficulties in developing neurological treatments: getting drugs across the blood-brain barrier, which prevents many substances from entering brain tissue.

A Potential New Direction for Alzheimer's Treatment

Although the results are encouraging, the treatment has so far been tested only in mice. Additional research will be needed to determine whether the approach is safe and effective enough to evaluate in people.

Holtzman suggested that medications already used to control T cells in other diseases could provide a starting point. Certain treatments for multiple sclerosis and other autoimmune conditions, in which the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy tissue, might be worth investigating for their potential to reduce Alzheimer's-related brain damage.

This would represent a significant shift in treatment strategy. Rather than concentrating exclusively on the abnormal proteins associated with neurodegeneration, researchers could also target the immune responses that contribute to the destruction of neurons.

"Tauopathies aren't thought of as autoimmune disorders, so they haven't been treated the same way, but this study shows for the first time in an animal model that these diseases respond to a specific T-cell therapy," said Holtzman, who also directs WashU's Hope Center for Neurological Disorders and the Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center at WashU Medicine.

"For this therapeutic approach, if it is safe, you wouldn't have to design the drug to get into the brain -- which is a big deal since most molecules don't cross the blood-brain barrier well -- and you don't have to get rid of the tau to get this therapeutic effect."

The findings raise the possibility that protecting the brain from harmful immune activity, rather than removing tau itself, could become an important strategy for treating Alzheimer's and other tauopathies.

The analysis focuses on motorized mass transit, leaving out bikes and pedestrians as well as taxis, shared vehicles and e-scooters.

The EU wants to become the first climate-neutral continent by 2050. A key part of that plan is cutting transport-related GHG emissions by 90% from 1990 levels.

As DW's analysis shows, Copenhagen is the only EU capital that has almost electrified its entire bus fleet — its vehicles do not have combustion engines and therefore do not produce tailpipe emissions. In cities like Rome, Lisbon and Nicosia, more than 90% of buses still run on diesel, and metros and trams account for less than 10% of their public transport fleet.

Although cars still dominate European streets, "electric buses are one way to reduce local air pollution," said Martin Baierl, data analyst with Transport & Environment, an advocacy non-profit for clean transport.

EU funds enabled many cities to increase their electric and alternative fuel bus fleets from 2021 on.

Electric buses comprise 20% of the total fleet in EU capitals and selected cities. Their number increased by 55% between 2021 and 2025 — equivalent to 1,234 more such vehicles on the road. The number of diesel-only buses has been declining since then, although they still outnumber those running on alternative fuel.

In addition to decarbonizing the bus fleet and helping to reduce air pollution, embracing electric models can help to lower noise and public transport operating costs. All the more so if the city produces its own electricity, explains Baierl. However, the outlay for e-buses is higher than for diesel models. In 20 out of the 31 cities with data to analyze, over 50% of the bus fleet is made up of traditional vehicles.

Brussels, for example, was an early adopter of moving away from traditional diesel-powered buses, switching to hybrids instead. Now the Belgian capital is in the process of electrifying its fleet, but at a slower pace. "It's not easy to know what the right technology will be in the end," Baierl said.

Another example is hydrogen-powered buses, which also produce zero emissions but are more costly to operate than their electric counterparts.

Transportation is the only major sector where greenhouse gas emissions remain above 1990 levels, despite the EU's ambition of a 90% reduction by 2050.

On average, public transport — including buses, trams and metros — is used for more than a quarter of trips in the 10 largest EU capitals, according to recent data from the non-profit group CDP (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project). In Stockholm, the share is 49%, while in the smaller Slovenian capital of Ljubljana, it's 11%. DW analysis of transport sustainability pledges reveals that only four cities have explicitly committed to achieving zero emissions in buses or all public transport.

Yannis Papadimitriou lives 85 kilometers (about 53 miles) away from Athens, the EU capital with the highest air pollution mortality risk. He travels to work by commuter rail, a means of transport he'd like to see promoted more.

"For me it is a very conscious choice to use electric public transport, especially trains, which are the safest, most environmentally friendly and quickest way of transport."

Though trains, trams and metros run on electricity, there are more buses within city boundaries, and in many urban areas the majority still run on diesel.

In Northern Europe, 15 kilometers away from Copenhagen, Poul Kattler also used to commute to work. He is now retired and takes the bus more frequently with his grandchildren. Despite Copenhagen having an almost completely fossil-fuel-free public transport system (only one bus runs on biomethane), the metro expansion, which is responding to the city's growth, comes with its own set of concerns.

"The whole construction phase [of the new metro line] is extremely environmentally demanding," Kattler said. If the construction emissions are considered, "the CO2 balance would be very poor for the metro now."

But in the long run, the benefits of a new metro line in reducing CO2 emissions outweigh its construction-related emissions, Le Pira said.

Combustion or exhaust emissions are not the only way transport pollutes the air and the environment. Indirect emissions, like those coming from electricity generation or fuel production, add to transport's CO2 footprint. Brakes and wheels release non-exhaust particle matter when wagons or vehicles slow down.

Since 1992, buses and any vehicle running on fossil fuels in the EU have been subject to stricter emissions standards. Fine particle emissions from buses and heavy-duty vehicles had dropped by 92% in 2022 according to the European Environment Agency. Yet overall road transport non-exhaust emissions from tire wear have increased 58% in two decades, as cars have become heavier.

With a few exceptions, EU member states are not close to cutting greenhouse gas transport emissions by 90%, as envisioned by the EU's 2050 target.

Zero-emission public transport is one step toward decarbonizing transport in cities and meeting GHG reduction goals. The next challenge is to get people out of their cars.

"If we talk about sustainable mobility in general and not only clean public transport, the main problem is the modal shift from private cars to public transport," Le Pira said.

On average, private automobiles account for more than a third of transport in the largest EU capitals, according to CDP data.

Cheaper ticket prices for groups, like students, and disincentives for car use, such as low-emission zones, are two complementary ways of attracting more passengers while reducing private transport.

In her research work, Le Pira investigates how public transport can be more efficient and appealing for city dwellers, for example, by combining transport modes where people step off their bikes and onto a train. This kind of solution could be particularly relevant for small cities "where cars are still the main transport mode."

According to Baierl, places with good public transport draw people in. "Cities where not everybody has to use a car are just more livable," he said.
