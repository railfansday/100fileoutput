#3317. Santoli: We are nearing a now-or-never moment for the AI trade to rebound

The difference between a restorative rest and unhealthy stagnation is time. Distinguishing between resilience and fatigue isn't always easy.

The S&P 500 has been sideways for months, last week dipping to levels first reached on June 1. It has spent nearly all of the last three months within the range the index traveled during a peppy two-day rally Aug. 3-4.

Along the way, the former leaders of the AI trade, captured in the iShares AI Innovation ETF, have collapsed by 35 percentage points relative to the equal-weight S&P 500, then regained half the loss, then gave up most of that bounce.

Aside from giving all of us plenty of time and room for overthinking it, this process of constant churn and testing and positioning shocks has done some good, in a tactical sense.

It has created short-term washout conditions in semiconductors and other former momentum AI-hardware plays. Meantime, the cyclical pockets of the market – from consumer discretionary to industrials to banks - have undergone double-digit setbacks as higher bond yields and elevated oil prices restrain growth expectations.

Equity investors have shown a clear preference for quality and defense, as the debt markets have begun rationing growth capital more stringently, raising hurdle rates for the more marginal AI-data-center borrowers.

Along the way, some familiar intermarket relationships have been scrambled: Treasury yields have stopped moving closely with oil, yields retreating well off their highs as crude stays above $100 a barrel. And the S&P 500's former inverse correlation with Treasury yields has loosened, as the bond market bid seems more tied to hiccups in the AI buildout, which has stymied the Big Tech players.

The Goldman Sachs equity desk over the weekend noted "trader exhaustion," in part from the momentum-factor stock basket having moved at least 5% in a day 30 times in 2026 – more often than in any other full year, including ones when there were crashes such as 2000, 2009 and 2020: "Not sure we can handle much more flipping of the thematic script(s)."

Of course, confusion and stress ultimately can reset positioning and attitudes for the good. The sentiment and positioning composite kept by Macro Risk Advisors' John Kolovos that runs here weekly is approaching a "fat pitch" for a tactical rebound, he says.  By most measures, hedge funds have radically cut their leverage and exposure to tech. Broad institutional positioning in tech is not far above the Iran-war lows, according to Deutsche Bank, which adds that "the last two reversals higher in tech coincided with strong earnings releases."

This setup takes shape as the mega-cap cloud platforms Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft report results this week. Further, AI-funding anxiety should arguably ease a bit with the Anthropic IPO filing showing good operating metrics and $100 billion in cash before the IPO proceeds are collected, alongside Blackstone's financing for SpaceX capex and Broadcom lining up $63 billion to fund chips for Anthropic.

It's not quite a now-or-never moment for the AI trade to find some relief, but it's not far from one.

If there's a nagging outlier to the evidence of investor retrenchment and waning risk appetites, it's the ceaseless buying by retail traders of even the worst-looking tech charts.

Scott Rubner, head of equity and equity derivatives strategy at Citadel Securities, wrote on Friday after Thursday's nasty 3% tech selloff, "Despite commentary from several sell-side banks suggesting retail capitulation, our platform data showed the opposite." Namely, the retail order flow Citadel handles as a top market maker revealed extreme dip buying in the leveraged semi ETFs, Micron, Meta and other hard-hit names. This matches data from Robinhood, which according to the firm's Sherwood Media showed its traders stampeded into Bloom Energy on Friday as it fell 11% with the second-highest day ever of net buying in the stock.

While impressive on some level, and even admirable to some, this aggressive buying of severe weakness is not something that has typically coincided with important market lows. Citadel's data tell us that the late-March and late-July bottoms in the market occurred when retail traders panicked out and were net sellers for a week or two.

Not to say that retail's stubborn dip-buying precludes some relief in hard-hit parts of the market, but it likely limits how strong or durable a rally might become.

John Kolovos of Macro Risk Advisors on the latest reading: "Sentiment and breadth are finally giving us something to like, and a fat pitch may be approaching, but the macro needs to cooperate. Our studies show that improving momentum amid tight financial conditions has historically produced disappointing returns and left the market dangerously vulnerable to left-tail outcomes or market corrections."

-Sometimes assessing the weight of the evidence requires a long wait for all the evidence. I grant that the anxious churn and valuation compression of recent months has generated conditions for upside surprise and a relief trade higher.

But bigger picture, I want to stay alert to changing atmospheric conditions.

As I've noted in the weeks since this newsletter launched, I think we're feeling late-cycle tremors – narrower market leadership, investors' unwillingness to pay up for fast earnings growth, a flattening yield curve, stingier credit markets.

I've suggested the market might be telling us companies are "over-earning" through front-loaded capex recycling. It's probable that the Iran war and its macro disturbances prevented the AI theme from going full bubble mode, restricting the craziness to semis and related groups. But that doesn't mean we're sure to rebuild toward a broad bubble this cycle.

And I've argued that even if stocks do fine, bonds now offer a useful yield cushion against concentrated equity risk.

Last year around this time, cyclical-sector outperformance, approaching fiscal tailwinds and benign credit conditions were telling us not to worry about economic growth into 2026. So then what are the recent defensive-stock leadership, fading fiscal impulse and unsettled corporate credit whispering in investors' ears now?

I also believe in the laws of mean-reversion, even though they are enforced only sporadically. A post by Ben Carlson of Ritholtz Wealth Management on his "A Wealth of Common Sense" blog details how great trailing 15-year S&P 500 returns have been, and what this could mean looking ahead.

-This writeup by Dario Perkins of TS Lombard entered heavy rotation in the past couple of days, for good reason. It's a nicely articulated and sobering thought experiment on how the AI boom might now be incubating its own nasty bust.

The migration of money toward pure, traditional defensive stocks has been a bit too mechanical to infer that it's entirely a macro "tell," given the clockwork factor rotations that look almost too scripted to be trusted.

Still, the results are a convergence of valuations between consumer staples and technology. This might seem perverse for current-generation investors but there was a time when staples commanded a premium for their stability and enduring brands, while tech was viewed as cyclical and fickle.

Procter & Gamble has long been the class of the consumer-products industry, standing in for quality management in the way Pampers are synonymous with diapers. And somewhat the way Google is interchangeable with looking something up on the Internet.

Whether this crossover in P/E strikes you as the world's proper order being restored or as a screaming buy signal for Alphabet shares is the measure of what sort of investor you are.

#3316. Stop looking for someone to blame…the Suns are failing together

As you watch the Phoenix Suns play basketball against an Oklahoma City Thunder team that is severely undermanned, a situation that became even worse when Alex Caruso went down with an ankle injury in the first half, your natural inclination is to ask who's to blame. Which player isn't playing up to expectations? Is it the Miles Bridges trade and the impact it's had on both ends of the floor? Is it Devin Booker failing to elevate his game to the level Shai Gilgeous-Alexander was playing at on the opposing side before he got into foul trouble? Is it the bench? Is it the injuries?

Surely, somebody has to be to blame. Somebody has to be the reason this team is struggling against a Thunder squad that's quite literally limping through the game. But why does it always have to be someone? Why do we feel the need to point a finger at an individual and declare, "Yeah, that's the reason why!" Because it gives us the justification to follow it up with, "Yeah, that's the guy who needs to go!"?

In the case of the Phoenix Suns, it's a collective issue. I don't think there's one sole culprit responsible for the poor basketball we've witnessed through the first three games of the season. While continuity exists in theory, connectivity is clearly missing at this extremely early stage of the season, and we witnessed it again Monday night in Oklahoma City.

The offensive sets aren't crisp, and their execution leaves plenty to be desired. But defensively, the Suns collectively have a real problem. They allowed players like Ajay Mitchell to attack the lane at will, converting layup after layup. And don't get me wrong, Mitchell is a skilled player. With the injuries Oklahoma City is dealing with, he could very well be someone who takes a significant leap this season. But Phoenix's inability to contain his drives was troublesome.

Combine that with another night of ineffective rebounding (they lost that battle 48-38), and you have a team that seemingly can't get out of its own way. Whenever the offense sputters, the defense can't prevent the opposition from going on runs. Teams can knife into the lane for easy points while taking chances from beyond the arc, knowing that even if those shots don't fall, there's a good chance they'll get another opportunity courtesy of an offensive rebound.

That's why you can't point the finger at one person. The problems we're seeing aren't isolated. They're interconnected. Poor communication leads to defensive breakdowns. Defensive breakdowns expose rebounding deficiencies. Those extra possessions put additional pressure on an offense that's still trying to figure itself out. And suddenly, everybody looks worse than they actually are. Three games into the season, the Suns don't need a scapegoat. They need accountability across the roster, better execution, and a collective understanding of what winning basketball requires. Because pointing fingers won't fix what five guys have to solve together.

There are some nights when you know early on that Devin Booker is going to take over a basketball game. Against the Warriors, that was evident from the jump. Booker poured in 43 points, the most ever scored by a Phoenix Suns player in a home opener. Another record for a guy who seems determined to rewrite the franchise record books one page at a time.

Naturally, he dominated the Bright Side Baller voting, earning 85% of the total vote. A landslide victory, and deservedly so. Shoutout to the three people who still thought Mariah Carey was better.

#3310. Silicon Valley AI political groups turn on each other in the 2026 election

Silicon Valley's bid to dominate artificial intelligence politics has devolved into a fight with itself.

With one week until the midterm election, political groups favoring faster AI development and lighter, more uniform rules have spent roughly $8 million in general-election congressional races, besting groups pushing for stronger safeguards and more room for states to regulate that have spent about $6.9 million, according to a CNBC analysis of Federal Election Commission filings through Oct. 20.

In Florida's 25th Congressional District, that split has produced a direct face-off. During the primary, Think Big, an affiliate of Leading the Future, the main super PAC network pushing faster AI development and lighter regulation, spent $310,000 supporting Democrat Jared Moskowitz, whose district changed after Florida redrew its congressional map ahead of the 2026 election. On the other side, Defending Our Values, a super PAC favoring stricter safety guardrails and state-level authority, dropped almost $857,000 to boost Republican Scott Singer and an additional $740,000 to keep one of his primary opponents from being elected.

Now, Moskowitz and Singer face off in the general election.

It's a microcosm of the breakdown in Silicon Valley's bid to build a unified AI political machine. Both sides favor AI innovation and agree on the need for guardrails, but they remain locked in a costly tug-of-war over which candidates to back — and which will best align with their preferred deployment speed, regulatory reach and balance of state power.

Electing more allies could give either camp an edge in shaping AI policy in the next Congress, even as the feud risks weakening Silicon Valley's clout.

"Everyone supports some form of regulation. It's just a debate about how much," said Bruce Freed, president and co-founder of the nonpartisan Center for Political Accountability and a former chief investigator for the Senate banking committee. "Money is flowing in all directions to be in the driver's seat of what happens."

CNBC identified federal committees aligned with each camp and totaled their FEC-reported independent expenditures supporting or opposing congressional candidates. The analysis excludes state races and nonprofit advocacy spending.

This was not the plan. The model was cryptocurrency.

Fairshake and its affiliated pro-crypto super PACs, backed by Coinbase, Ripple and Andreessen Horowitz, spent more than $130 million during the 2024 election cycle, according to FEC filings. Candidates it supported won 53 of 58 races, a 91% success rate, according to crypto publication CoinDesk.

AI investors took note. In August 2025, Leading the Future launched with more than $100 million in commitments from tech heavyweights including Andreessen Horowitz, OpenAI President Greg Brockman and his wife, Anna, and Perplexity and Palantir co-founder Joe Lonsdale.

Co-led by Josh Vlasto, who worked for Fairshake last election cycle, the network deployed a Democratic PAC, Think Big, and a Republican PAC, American Mission, to support candidates aligned with faster AI development and opposition to a patchwork of state regulations.

The message was clear: Back AI's agenda or risk millions being spent against you.

"The AI industry announced last year that they were going to rerun the crypto industry playbook by coming in swinging with oodles of money in order to bully lawmakers into submission," said Sacha Haworth, founder and executive director of the Tech Oversight Project, a nonprofit watchdog that advocates for stronger regulation of Big Tech and AI.

At first, it worked. In the cycle's first major primaries in March, three candidates heavily backed by American Mission — Jessica Steinmann in Texas, Chris Gober in Texas and Laurie Buckhout in North Carolina — won their Republican contests after the group spent more than $500,000 supporting each, according to FEC filings.

But a rival infrastructure was already taking shape. Anthropic committed $20 million to Public First Action in February and another $20 million in July, giving the safeguards camp a major financial backer. Anthropic says its money is restricted to policy and public education and cannot fund candidate elections. Still, separately funded super PACs tied to Public First Action have spent millions advancing similar priorities.

The clash burst into view in New York’s 12th District Democratic primary. Think Big spent $8.3 million, according to FEC filings, trying to defeat state Assemblymember Alex Bores, who authored major state AI safety legislation. The Anthropic-backed Jobs and Democracy PAC countered with about $15 million to support him, while allied safety groups spent millions more, according CNBC's analysis.

Think Big and Jobs and Democracy spent a combined $23.3 million, about 40% of the two camps' primary spending nationwide, according to the analysis.

Bores ultimately lost to Micah Lasher, 39% to 35%, giving Think Big a tactical victory.

"They helped light a flame under the backlash that's now fueling so much of the midterm elections," Haworth said. "The race showed that spending against safety-first candidates could now trigger a counterattack from within the industry itself."

The fight has carried into the general election, but the pace of spending plummeted.

Safeguards-focused groups have spent $825,000 this fall opposing two Republicans who benefited from Leading the Future-aligned spending during the primaries, according to CNBC's analysis of FEC filings. The two races alone account for more than one-sixth of the safeguards camp's general-election opposition spending.

Guardrails Alliance reported spending $412,500 apiece, according to FEC filings, against Republican House candidates Jay Feely in Arizona and Aaron Flint in Montana, both of whom benefited from Leading the Future-aligned spending in their primaries. It also reported about $412,000 against Democratic Rep. Jimmy Gomez in California, whom Leading the Future endorsed but did not report spending to support, according to CNBC's analysis.

"The big AI groups still believe that their money can help influence elections even if it's just a couple in November," Haworth said.

Yet the overall flow of AI campaign money has slowed since the primaries.

Leading the Future's network reported roughly $26 million in primary independent expenditures, compared with about $8 million in general-election races. Safeguards-focused groups reported about $32.1 million in primary spending and $6.9 million in general-election contests. The groups reported another roughly $5.5 million in runoff and special-election spending.

Combined, about 74% of the roughly $78.5 million they have spent on federal candidates this cycle went toward primary races, according to CNBC's analysis.

Some of that decline reflects the smaller universe of competitive November races. But the slowdown has coincided with AI becoming a more difficult political sell.

A new CNBC All-America Economic Survey found 52% of registered voters said companies should pause development of advanced AI until Congress passes regulations, compared with 32% who favored moving ahead. Sixty-three percent said they would oppose an AI data center within 10 miles of their home.

“When evaluating risk versus reward right now, the backlash is just too big,” said Freed, of the Center for Political Accountability.

“AI executives and groups spent heavily in the primaries, so they likely achieved what they set out to do in most races. At this point, there's no real downside to scaling back,” Freed said.

The Brockmans gave $25 million to Leading the Future. But amid objections from OpenAI employees to the group's tactics, OpenAI's chief strategy officer acknowledged internally that the company was taking "reputational hits," according to Semafor.

The Brockmans later abandoned plans for another $25 million contribution, according to The New York Times.

Meta has retreated as well. Political groups it funded returned $25 million this summer — $22 million from the AI-promoting American Technology Excellence Project and $3 million from its California operation — according to IRS and state filings. The refunds cut its planned $65 million state-level political push by nearly 40%, to about $40 million. The Times first reported the refunds.

"Something's clearly not working," OpenAI CEO Sam Altman told Politico earlier this month. "I don't think spending money on politics is what's going to make people feel better about it."

#3303. Don't let inflation bite — how to hedge with TIPS, commodities and infrastructure funds

The Consumer Price Index hit 3.7% in September – the latest sign that inflation remains a problem that isn't going away.

It's a tough situation for investors looking for protection, but there are some good ways to hedge.

Long-term Treasury Inflation-Protected securities (TIPS) yield 2.9%, near multi-decade highs. Beyond TIPS, analysts suggest commodities, infrastructure, and other assets that can hedge against rising prices.

While inflation is down sharply from its 9.1% peak in 2022, it's still well above the Federal Reserve's 2% target. Headline CPI's 3.7% rate in September topped estimates for 3.6%. Core CPI, excluding food and energy, reached 2.6%, also higher than expected. Rising energy prices drove much of the increase. But the Personal Consumption Expenditures index, the Fed's preferred gauge, also remains elevated at a core 3% rate.

Americans' inflation concerns are also rising. The New York Fed's Survey of Consumer Expectations showed the one-year inflation outlook in September climbed to 3.9%, its highest level since May 2023.

Despite their name, TIPS haven't provided much inflation protection this year. The iShares TIPS Bond ETF, which tracks the ICE U.S. Treasury Inflation Linked Bond Index, has a total return of -1.16% year-to-date.

Like most other bonds, TIPS have been hit hard by rising rates. The 10-year Treasury note is up sharply this year and spiked Wednesday to 5.29%, near the highest levels since 2002. Bond prices and yields move inversely, and as rates climbed many TIPS funds suffered.

But higher yields have made TIPS more attractive. The 10-year TIPS' 2.9% yield is up from roughly 1.9% early in the year.

"Given where rates are, it's getting interesting," says Jason Draho, head of asset allocation Americas at UBS Global Wealth Management.

TIPS pay a fixed coupon, or interest rate, but as inflation moves higher, the principal value of the bond increases. The adjustments reflect changes in inflation, based on the non-seasonally adjusted CPI for All Urban Consumers, with a three-month lag.

The upshot is that TIPS investors earn interest on a higher principal value over time. That's the essence of the protection compared to traditional Treasuries.

TIPS' poor performance this year isn't because of a surge in inflation expectations. Almost all the increase has been due to rising real yields — the nominal yield minus expected inflation. Real yields are up because of factors like robust economic growth and a U.S. fiscal deficit around 6% of gross domestic product (requiring far more government borrowing).

If nominal rates were rising just due to inflation, then TIPS total return — a mix of yield and price — would be fine, said R.J. Gallo, chief investment officer of global fixed income at Federated Hermes. But that isn't the case.

"TIPS have produced losses in total return because their income accruals have been insufficient to offset the price decline produced by the rising underlying real yields," Gallo explained.

TIPS maturing in 10 years or more are highly sensitive to duration risk, Draho notes. "You're making a big call on rates not rising any further if you buy TIPS, especially longer maturities of 10-year plus," he said.

The bull case is that inflation comes down quicker than expected. That's not the market's expectation, based on short-term Treasury yields, notes Draho. But if inflation does ease next year, which he expects, the yield curve would adjust lower.

One way to manage duration risk is to own individual TIPS rather than a fund that has to maintain duration consistent with a benchmark index. Investors could create a TIPS bond ladder with varying maturities, pegged to their income needs over the next decade, for instance.

If you hold a TIPS to maturity you may experience price volatility, but you'll get the original principal or inflation-adjusted price, whichever is greater, when the bond matures.

Short-term TIPS provide more protection against rate increases and they look attractive, said PIMCO portfolio manager Greg Sharenow.

"While as a firm, our view is that inflation will ultimately come down, that view keeps on being pushed out as real world events unfold," he said. "We've seen over and over again the last five to seven years — where we're now in our third supply shock — that you end up actually underpricing versus realized inflation," he added.

Funds focusing on short-term TIPS include Vanguard Short-Term Inflation-Protected Securities ETF, iShares 0-5 Year TIPS Bond ETF and PIMCO 1-5 Year US TIPS Index ETF.

TIPS, of course, aren't the only way to go. Rick Rieder, BlackRock's chief investment officer for global fixed income, suggests a multi-pronged strategy, including equities, commodities, and alternative assets.

"I'd start layering in more income, keep interest-rate exposure moderate, and then what a lot of people are doing is use things like alternatives to to create more balance in a portfolio," he said. "That makes a lot of sense to me."

Broad inflation-protection funds include State Street Multi-Asset Real Return ETF, Fidelity Strategic Real Return Fund and PIMCO Inflation Response Multi-Asset Fund.

The PIMCO fund's holdings include TIPS, commodities, gold and real estate investment trusts.

"The idea is to have a number of different exposures so that we can have a more balanced return and not try to be so specific in getting the exact right asset class at any one moment," said Sharenow, a manager on the fund.

He adds that he sees "significant value" in TIPS, which have the heaviest weighting in the portfolio. Commodities are the next largest holding. They have price "pass-throughs," which is how much companies can shift costs to customers through price increases. That is beneficial to investors, he noted.

Separately, the fund holds about 10% each in gold and REITs.

Draho recommends commodities, based partly on long-term asset-allocation studies that include the 1970s when inflation was far higher. "Over a long period they can help reduce risk and improve diversification," he said. "Their benefits have been most pronounced when inflation was overshooting expectations."

Rather than picking individual commodities, he suggests owning a basket that's consistently rebalanced. Gold and oil, for instance, perform well in different economic and monetary policy scenarios, and it can be tricky to get the timing right.

Commodity ETFs to consider include the Invesco Optimum Yield Diversified Commodity Strategy ETF (PDBC) and the iShares S&P GSCI Commodity-Indexed Trust (GSG).

Another asset class Draho suggests is infrastructure — operators of roads, ports, bridges, and other hard assets. Companies in the space tend to be toll takers, collecting long-term revenue under contracts that include inflation riders or built-in price adjustments.

Investors can get exposure to infrastructure through ETFs such as the iShares Global Infrastructure ETF (IGF) and ProShares DJ Brookfield Global Infrastructure ETF (TOLZ).

Draho isn't sounding alarms on inflation but says it makes sense to add protection against another move higher.

"The risk now is there will be supply side shocks that could push up inflation again," he said.

He told prosecutors that if they wanted to file a superseding complaint, a judge first would have to find probable cause that a crime had been committed, instead of sending him the complaint in the middle of the night and expecting him to sign it.

Faruqui said he did not believe that federal prosecutors had previously sought to file a criminal complaint without a judge's signature.

"It is not our court's practice … that unsigned complaints are filed in the docket," Faruqui said.

"I'm concerned with your view as to what the law is," the judge told the prosecutors. "Why today, on this one case, have you changed the entire history of how you charge cases?"

The superseding complaint that prosecutors in the office of D.C. U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro filed in court early Tuesday contained a signature block with Faruqui's name typed out. But the judge had not personally signed the complaint.

It was filed hours before prosecutors and defense lawyers were due to appear before Faruqui for a preliminary hearing on the original complaint.

That first complaint, made public last Friday, accuses Floris and Benavides of making false statements to the House Judiciary Committee in 2023 about their involvement in investigations of Hunter Biden, the son of former President Joe Biden.

Floris and Benavides were arrested Friday and have been released.

The new complaint sought to charge them with obstruction and attempted obstruction of a congressional proceeding.

Typically, criminal cases for conduct that occurred well in the past — as this one alleges — are lodged through an indictment, not a criminal complaint. To obtain an indictment, prosecutors must present evidence to a grand jury, which then determines whether the evidence is sufficient to charge a defendant.

A criminal complaint only requires a judge's signature. It is often used to obtain an arrest warrant in situations where prosecutors need to move quickly.

At Tuesday's hearing, Faruqui homed in on the way prosecutors filed charges.

"This case was charged by criminal complaint on Oct. 20, for testimony that occurred over a period of time in 2023," he said. "As I noted, and I think it continues to be the case, that it's unusual in a case that has conduct like this to be charged by criminal complaint."

"This has to get to a grand jury," Faruqui said.

Under federal rules, prosecutors generally must obtain an indictment within 30 days of a person's arrest, or risk the case being dismissed.

Floris' lawyers, in an emailed statement to CNBC on Tuesday morning, criticized the prosecutors' conduct.

"In the middle of the night, the government filed an unsigned and unsworn complaint attempting to fix the issues with their original charge against Ms. Floris and adding a new charge just as meritless as the first one," the attorneys wrote.

"In our combined decades of DOJ service, we have never seen or even heard of something like this being filed. That's because the public lodging of criminal charges must be authorized by the grand jury, a judge, or the defendant herself," they wrote.

"Here, the government has taken that power for itself in a desperate attempt to delay our client's right to challenge the charges in court and litigate this case in the media, rather than the courtroom," they said.

"We have asked to address this outrageous government conduct against a decorated public servant at today's court hearing," Floris' legal team wrote.

With open enrollment season in swing, many employees may notice something conspicuously missing from their benefits menu — coverage of GLP-1 drugs for weight loss.

More companies are dropping coverage completely or offering employees less generous payouts. In some cases, they're shifting to a different model altogether — offering employees a consolation prize of sorts — access to a weight management program through a third party.

Coverage of these drugs for weight loss is pricey for employers — typically costing in the mid-$500s to mid-$600s per user monthly. Some companies say whatever the price, covering the drugs is worth it based on employee health. "We spend about $250 million or more on GLPs, and that's up from zero" four or five years ago, Bank of America CEO Brian Moynihan told CNBC's Andrew Ross Sorkin in August. "We see a great impact on the employees. ... Believe me, we're pounding everybody on price and trying to get as cheap [as possible]. But our view is that [because of] the long-term health benefits, plus there may be more short-term health benefits ... it's a good investment."

Nevertheless, as healthcare costs continue to climb, more employers have been looking for an exit ramp on GLP-1 coverage for weight loss, and that includes bigger companies. A WTW annual health benefits survey fielded over the summer and being released in full on Wednesday found that 53% of employers with more than 100 employees cover GLP-1s for weight loss, down from 57% in 2025. That's the first decline in three years. What's more, 16% of employers said they cover the drugs now but are considering removing them, up from 9% last year.

According to a recent Marsh survey of over 1,800 US employers, health insurance cost per employee is expected to rise 8.2% on average in 2027, the biggest increase since 2003, with GLP-1 use accounting for a full percentage point of the overall cost growth. Its experts have cited the soaring costs among reasons employers are considering dropping coverage for 2027.

"Employers are really balancing empathy with economics," said Caroline Susie, principal in Marsh's total health management practice.

Some workers who use employer-sponsored health plans are being provided with options in the event of GLP-1 coverage being dropped, though nothing may match full health insurance coverage. Here are alternatives to expect.

Some employers are directing workers to buy GLP-1s through direct-to-consumer options like Novo Nordisk's NovoCare or Eli Lilly's LillyDirect. WTW's survey showed that 8% of employers have gone this route, and 28% were planning or considering it.

Seth Friedman, pharmacy and health plans practice leader at Gallagher, estimated that 20% to 30% of the firm's clients have dropped coverage for 2027 and are sending employees the direct route. For employers, this move can mean significant savings. The percentage of U.S. adults who take GLP-1 medications for weight loss purposes climbed to 11% in 2026, up significantly from 3% in 2024, according to Gallup. What's more, 15% report having used the medicine for weight loss at some point, a nine-percentage-point boost.

"Many people will choose to pay for this on their own even if it's not covered by the plan," said Jeff Levin-Scherz, population health leader at WTW.

Employers can often design the program that works for their employees' needs, at a significant savings from paying for GLP-1 drugs through a pharmacy benefit manager, said Alysha Fluno, national pharmacy practice leader at Marsh. A PBM is a third-party administrator that manages prescription drug benefits for health insurers and employers.

Based on direct-to-consumer prices, employers could fully subsidize, pay a vendor fee, and still spend much less than they would to purchase Wegovy, for example, through a PBM, according to WTW's Levin-Scherz.

#3233. BlackRock's Rick Rieder sees 'historic' opportunity in high-quality bonds

Investors may be wary of the volatility hitting parts of the bond market right now, but they have an "historic" opportunity to grab attractive yields in high-quality credit, Rick Rieder, BlackRock's chief investment officer for global fixed income, told CNBC in an exclusive interview.

Hypothetically, it's now possible for investors to build a diversified portfolio that yields more than 7% with less than three years of duration and an average credit quality of A-, he said. Duration is a measurement of a bond's price sensitivity to fluctuations in rates, and issues with shorter maturities tend to have lower duration.

"Not only is it historic in that these yields are where they are, but the only time these yields are ever here is when there's some problem, like an economic problem or default problem," Rieder said in an interview about his fourth-quarter outlook, released Wednesday.

That isn't the case now, where the economy remains strong, he said. "So you don't really have to worry about defaults of any significance."

While Rieder didn't specifically identify the credits in the aforementioned portfolio, the iShares Aaa - A Rated Corporate Bond ETF (QLTA) has a 30-day SEC yield of 5.68% and an effective duration of 6.48 years — higher than Rieder's low duration focus.

The iShares Flexible Income Active ETF (BINC), which is managed by Rieder, has a 5.66% 30-day SEC yield and three-year effective duration, but its portfolio extends beyond investment-grade credit.

Rieder sees the best opportunity right now in investment-grade corporate bonds — which he pointed out he hasn't said "in a really long time." Investment grade bonds are rated AAA through BBB- by S&P and Fitch, while Moody's rates them Aaa through Baa3.

That includes hyperscaler bonds, which have flooded the market with issuance this year as they ramp up their AI infrastructure. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta Platforms and Oracle have issued a total of about $228.4 billion in bonds year to date, as of Oct. 20, according to LSEG.

"They're very low leverage. They have tremendous amounts of cash flow coming in," Rieder said of the hyperscalers in general. "You're now getting to buy these entities which are higher quality, paying you a very attractive yield to get it."

Rieder's iShares Flexible Income Active ETF has upped its allocation to investment-grade corporates to 14.3% of its portfolio. In July, that stood at 8.5%.

That said, investors need to be patient and sit tight since rates could still move higher from here, he advised.

"Your starting point is by far your best barometer of what your return is going to be," he noted. "So if you get seven and a quarter, maybe you get eight, maybe you get six, but ... if your holding period is a year or longer, you're going to get that yield."

Caution should also be exercised outside of investment-grade credit, which requires more selectivity and deep research, he warned. That includes AI names.

"There's parts of the data center supply that's not hyperscaler guaranteed or hyperscaler backed," he said. "That we've been more cautious around, and we'll continue to be."

The bond market, particularly high-yield credit, has been rocked as investor concern grows over the massive amount of debt some companies are taking out to fund their artificial intelligence build-out.

Junk-rated CoreWeave has become the poster child, seeing its stock drop, bond yields jump and credit-default spreads widen. Oracle, which is sitting in the lower end of investment grade and is seen as a bellwether on the broader AI market, has also taken some hits.

Rieder is also keeping an eye on the Federal Reserve, which is expected to raise its federal funds rate again in December to combat inflation. The market is currently pricing in a nearly 90% probability that the central bank will hike at that time, according to the CME Group FedWatch tool.

The money manager expects that to happen, although he does not agree with it. Higher rates are hurting parts of the economy, like housing, while not having much of an impact on other areas — such as tech companies' capital expenditures, which have become a big driver of growth, he said.

"They're not borrowing off the overnight funds rate," Rieder noted. "There's a lot of floating rate finance in housing, credit card, auto loan finance, and you're seeing those areas already under pressure."

Instead, the market is and will continue to price the AI supply coming into the market, he added.

Rieder sees a number of possible risks ahead, including rising levels of government debt across the globe. In the United States, the budget deficit has grown to $2 trillion for fiscal 2026.

"The more the central banks raise the rates, the cost of debt to these to already indebted governments is something we've got to be careful about over time," he said.

Bond supply coming from AI companies should also be watched.

"With the amount of supply that's coming — data center, hyperscaler — there's some crowding-out effect that's taking place," he said. "With government rates moving higher, that is something that we just got to keep an eye on."

In the meantime, Rieder would just continue to reap the rewards of high investment-grade yields "and smile a lot," he said.

He got the same treatment in a lopsided loss to the Lakers on opening night and finished at 21, almost played 30 minutes against the Grizzlies and sat out the second half of their back-to-back a night later in Phoenix.

It's all part of the plan to manage the load on Curry's runner's knee that cost him 27 games last season. That, and the ebbs and flows of the scoreboard in the NBA.

He still managed to log 24 points in his abridged time on the floor against the Nuggets. Curry connected on all eight of his shots from the field.

Believe it or not, the greatest shooter of all time had missed at least one in every other game he has played, except one. In 1,227 career games, including the postseason, the only other time Curry had a perfect shooting performance was Feb. 5, 2011 — on five attempts for 14 points.

It didn't look like it was going to be Curry's night early. He went into halftime with just three points and one attempt from the field. Then, came a classic Curry flurry.

Curry went off for 17 points in the third quarter as Golden State pulled away.

"I didn't go into the game thinking that's what it was going to be," Curry said. "But I did go into it with the mindset of not trying to force anything, get the ball moving."

At halftime, Kerr orchestrated a game plan to get Curry going, setting screens in the back court, extending the Nuggets' defense and forcing them to chase him around.

The Warriors went into halftime leading 60-56. By the time Curry checked out for the final time with 8:01 left in the fourth quarter, Golden State had extended its lead to 110-91.

In a sense, Curry played himself out of more minutes.

The Warriors need him to make it through another 79 games.

#3222. Biotech stocks are surging. How investors can play the new drug innovation boom

Moderna gave investors a scare as its stock plunged on Thursday. But it's still one of the hottest biotechs this year. And it's hardly alone – the sector is on a roll and looks poised for more gains.

The iShares Biotechnology ETF (IBB) is up 24% through October 23, building on a 28% gain in 2025. Two dozen of the ETF's 233 holdings have more than doubled year-to-date. Moderna leads the pack with a 587% surge — even after an 18% decline following a leak of clinical data for its cancer vaccine (which was actually positive).

Analysts say a new biotech cycle is underway, fueled by clinical trial results, M&A activity, and promising technologies such as genomics, mRNA — and AI.

"We're in the midst of a powerful innovation cycle," said John Hall, portfolio manager of the T. Rowe Price Biotechnology ETF. "What's super exciting is we're seeing newer modalities start to make it to market." Companies "understand the disease biology better," he adds, and that could ultimately lead to more novel drugs making it to market.

Investors are excited about technologies like mRNA and genomics, along with a steady stream of new drug approvals. The FDA approved about a dozen novel biologic drugs in 2025, out of 46 approvals in total. Roughly 66 novel drugs are in the pipeline for FDA approval, including potential blockbusters like Moderna and Merck's cancer drug for melanoma, and a next-generation obesity drug from Eli Lilly.

Analysts say AI could also lead to more approvals as companies deploy the software to speed up drug development.

"Biotech is a phenomenal application for artificial intelligence and machine learning," says Piper Sandler analyst Ted Tenthoff. "You can set up exciting predictive models that, frankly, can do things that we've never been able to do in the space in terms of novel drug discovery."

Granted, the industry is recovering from a fallow period. The sector's last strong performance was during the Covid era when ultra-low interest rates encouraged investment in early-stage drug developers, and mRNA vaccines fueled a revenue surge for companies like Moderna, Pfizer and Biontech.

The sector then went cold as many early-stage companies fizzled. Demand for Covid vaccines evaporated, and surging interest rates put a chill in IPOs and M&A.

The risks haven't evaporated – this is still a sector whose products take years to develop, resulting in far more failures than successes. But analysts say the outlook is healthier now. While that's reflected in strong performance and richer valuations, there's still good reason to invest.

Recent clinical trial results are stoking excitement that novel drugs in the works could tackle some of the toughest problems in medicine.

Moderna and Merck's melanoma drug, for instance, uses mRNA to trigger an immune response in patients, targeting tumors that have been surgically removed and genetically analyzed. It's not a preventive vaccine, but it uses a similar immunology mechanism and showed a 42% reduction in cancer recurrence or death (used in conjunction with Merck's cancer drug Keytruda).

While the Phase 3 results disappointed Wall Street, oncologists say the outcomes were still highly positive. And they see the technology as a potential game changer, ushering in an era of personalized treatments for cancers. Moderna and Merck have said they aim to submit the drug for FDA approval and get it launched in 2027.

Inflammation treatment is another promising area. AstraZeneca and Amgen recently released positive Phase 3 results for another use for their drug Tezspire, an injectable approved for nasal polyps and asthma. In clinical trials, it showed that it successfully relieved swallowing symptoms and healed microscopic and visual tissue damage in patients with severe throat inflammation.

Roche, meanwhile, notched a win after the FDA approved its drug Enspryng, a therapy to treat rare inflammatory and autoimmune diseases. It became the first and only at-home injection for adults with thyroid eye disease.

Obesity drugs are also advancing beyond the first generation of GLP-1s. Eli Lilly's retatrutide, a "triple agonist" targeting three different hormone receptors, cleared two more clinical trials over the summer. The drug produced 28%-30% weight loss at the highest doses in the study, Lilly said. The company plans to submit the drug for FDA approval in the first quarter of 2027.

Much of the innovation isn't just happening in drug-making – it's also in areas like gene sequencing, mRNA, and high-tech instruments.

Illumina has more than doubled this year. The DNA and gene sequencing company is seeing accelerating demand for its NovaSeq X high-end sequencing platform. Pharmaceutical companies including Bristol-Myers Squibb, Merck and Kura are working with Illumina to develop precision oncology solutions.

Another big winner is 10x Genomics, up more than 300%. The biotech firm offers its lab instruments to help scientists research cancer, immunology and neuroscience. The company's Atera platform is helping produce data sets to train AI models, a process that helps accelerate personalized therapies.

On the AI front, companies are using the technology for more efficient drug discovery, says Shreefāl Mehta, president of Guidant BioTherapeutics. Using AI, "you can now bring a drug to clinical trials with less capital. More investment is also going into companies that are demonstrating that," he said.

Global AI in drug discovery will grow from a $20 billion market in 2025 to more than $160 billion by 2035, according to forecasts by Towards Health, a research and consulting firm.

One other catalyst for the sector could be M&A. Dealmaking for biopharma hit $134 billion in the first half of 2026, up from $112 billion for all of 2025, notes Jeet Mukherjee, biotech analyst at BTIG. That's a positive signal, indicating more drugs in development are showing clinical promise.

It also helps that Big Pharma has a "patent cliff" ahead. Products representing $180 billion of revenue will lose exclusivity by 2030, according to research firm RTW Institute. Companies with looming expiries "are looking for novel products and innovative products to put into their pipeline," Mehta says.

GLP-1 blockbusters have also left some companies flush with cash for acquisitions. Eli Lilly alone has completed 10 acquisition deals in 2026 worth $25 billion.

ETFs can capture the investing theme, though they aren't all alike. The iShares Biotech ETF is one of the lower cost funds. It tracks a market-cap weighted index of biotech stocks, led by companies like Gilead Sciences, Amgen, Vertex Pharmaceuticals, and Moderna.

The State Street SPDR S&P Biotech ETF holds stocks in equal weight, offering more exposure to small- and mid-cap names. Its top holdings are Moderna, Natera, Twist Bioscience and Halozyme Therapeutics.

Piper Sandler calls Illumina and 10x Genomics the "primary infrastructure plays" in the life science arena, saying their technologies are "driving the next generation of precision medicine."

Piper analyst David Westenberg has a buy rating and $305 target on Illumina, implying 14% gains from recent prices around $267. He has a hold rating on 10X with an $80 target, above recent prices around $70.

Twist Bioscience is another name to consider. The company makes customized synthetic DNA and sells DNA sequencing tools. Twist has partnered with Eli Lilly's AI and machine-learning platform and linked up with Anthropic in August to start testing its AI model, Claude, for protein development.

Twist isn't expected to turn an EPS profit until 2029, according to consensus forecasts. But revenue is expected to go from $572 million in 2027 to $833 million in 2029. Analysts have an average target of $168 on the stock, implying 21% returns from recent prices around $138.

In the small-cap space, Piper Sandler's Tenthoff likes Monte Rosa Therapeutics, a pioneer in protein degradation that selectively destroys disease-causing proteins.

The firm has partnered with Novartis to develop a drug for inflammatory conditions and autoimmune diseases. The companies announced a Phase 2 clinical study in September for an autoimmune disease treatment, which has been "triggering milestones to Monte Rosa," Tenthoff said.

Tenthoff has a buy rating and $37 target on the stock, which recently traded around $10.70.

Also in the small-cap arena, BTIG's Mukherjee recommends Faeth Therapeutics.

The biotech is developing a novel two-drug regimen to treat breast cancer and other tumors. The regimen "has the potential to address safety and dosing limitations of another recently approved drug for breast cancer," he says. And it could become "a mainstay of the future breast cancer treatment paradigm and other cancers."

Faeth is a clinical-stage company that has yet to bring a drug to market. Yet the stock is up 93% year-to-date on positive clinical trial results, trading around $18. Mukherjee has a $55 target on it.

Kymera Therapeutics is worth considering too. The company is developing an oral version of the blockbuster drug Dupixent (made and co-developed by Regeneron Pharmaceuticals and Sanofi). Dupixent is an injectable, however, and oral agents "are frequently preferred by patients," Mukherjee says. "The drug has the potential to not only take share from patients taking Dupixent for numerous immunological diseases but also expanding the market to those not taking systemic biologic drugs," he tells CNBC.

Kymera has said it doesn't expect a patent fight over Dupixent because its drug is distinct, not a biosimilar or biologic copy.

T. Rowe's Hall also likes Kymera for its potential "to unlock disease targets that have been classically considered un-druggable." The stock is the second largest holding in T. Rowe's Biotech ETF, accounting for 4.2% of the portfolio.
