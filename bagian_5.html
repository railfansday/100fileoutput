#5620. The New Energy Stalemate in Ukraine

TATIANA MITROVA is a Global Fellow at the Center on Global Energy Policy at Columbia University.

SERGEY VAKULENKO is a Senior Fellow at the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center in Berlin.

Why Russia’s Escalating Attacks Are Unlikely to End the War

In the days since U.S. President Donald Trump’s highly public falling out with Ukraine and growing rapprochement with Russia, Kyiv’s prospects going into the fifth winter of war look grim. The Trump administration has demanded that Ukraine suspend its attacks on Russian refineries, which have been one of Ukraine’s most effective means of putting pressure on Moscow. Meanwhile, Kyiv faces dangerous gaps in its air defenses against Russia’s jet-powered drones and stepped-up use of ballistic missiles. And Trump has entered into a deal with Russian President Vladimir Putin that temporarily waives U.S. sanctions on Russian diesel exports, offering Moscow a diplomatic win. Trump’s envoys have also proposed that Europe increase purchases of Russian gas as part of an effort to gain the Kremlin’s support for peace—further complicating the efforts of Ukraine and its remaining allies to squeeze Russia.

Yet missing from this bleak picture are the ways that Ukraine has already adapted to escalating attacks. Kyiv has developed innovative strategies to mitigate the impact of Russian energy strikes and keep its grid running, even amid intense bombardment. And despite the new U.S. pressure, Ukraine has continued to launch powerful drone strikes against Russia’s energy infrastructure, including successful mid-October attacks on an oil terminal in St. Petersburg and industrial gas processing plants as far away as Surgut and the Novy Urengoy, 1,800 miles from the Ukrainian border. Overall, since mid-2025, Ukrainian drone strikes have reduced Russian refinery throughput by roughly 30 percent; they have also eroded a significant share of Moscow’s excess refining capacity, resulting in a gasoline shortfall of about ten percent.

Still, this is only one part of a rapidly evolving energy war. Moscow has developed its own ways to limit the effects of Ukrainian strikes. And it has intensified its assault on Ukrainian infrastructure, including an October 19 barrage that damaged more than ten energy facilities. DiXi Group, a Ukrainian think tank, has estimated that Russia launched 12,800 missiles and drones at Ukrainian energy facilities in 2025 and early 2026, compared with just 2,800 during the first three years of the war combined. Although these figures count launches rather than successful strikes, they show how massive and relentless the Russian campaign has become.

Moreover, both sides are now able to target the substitutes and reserves that in the past could be used to cushion losses. Following Russian strikes on local power plants in early October, Ukrainians experienced widespread blackouts, even though Ukraine’s national grid had electricity to spare, because Russia continued hitting the lines and substations carrying the reserve power. During the summer, Ukraine hit so many of the refineries and pipelines around Moscow that Russia was forced to bring fuel by rail from as far as Lake Baikal, thousands of miles away.

If U.S. pressure and negotiations cannot stop the energy war, the central question for Ukraine is whether its remarkable capacity to adapt can keep pace with Russian attacks. At the same time, its fortunes this winter will depend on whether it can continue to hit Russian infrastructure in ways that challenge Moscow’s ability to repair and rebuild. Both Russia and Ukraine must now protect and repair their entire national networks around the clock; attackers can concentrate on the most vulnerable sites and strike again as soon as repairs are done. In such a war, defensive ingenuity matters as much as offensive capability. In the end, energy attacks alone are unlikely to force either side to accept the other’s terms, neither side may be able to gain a decisive advantage, even as both incur mounting costs. Recognizing the limits, as well as the power, of these strikes may be crucial not only to ending the present conflict but also to understanding the role that energy infrastructure will likely play in future wars.

For Ukraine, international support has been especially crucial in containing Russia’s energy barrage. As of November 2025, before the worst of last winter’s attacks, Ukraine had suffered $17.1 billion in direct damage to its electricity infrastructure, according to a report by the UN, the World Bank, and the European Commission. Yet Kyiv was able to keep the electricity flowing by drawing on the country’s remaining nuclear plants; repairing thermal generation facilities; increasing electricity, gas, and diesel imports; and compensating for lost power plants with distributed generation—smaller units running on solar, wind, gas, and diesel energy that are connected to the main grid.

Europe has also provided crucial replacement technology. In 2025, the European Commission delivered equipment from a retired Lithuanian thermal power plant to Ukraine in 149 shipments, enabling Ukraine to provide power to one million people. Such transfers have turned foreign utilities and suppliers into part of Ukraine’s rapid repair system. In September, Ukraine and the UN Development Program launched a new coordination center to speed up the installation of donated energy equipment.

Expanded use of batteries and distributed power also play a growing role. In September 2025, the Ukrainian energy company DTEK and the U.S.-based battery maker Fluence installed battery storage at six sites around Kyiv and Dnipro, with enough capacity to supply 600,000 households, roughly half of Kyiv, for two hours. Meanwhile, Kyiv has put in place 150 megawatts of distributed power generation—not enough to cover winter peak demand, but sufficient to provide more than half of the city’s essential services, including water supplies, hospitals, and heating systems. Nonetheless, Ukrainian reports suggest that 2,500 to 3,000 of Kyiv’s 12,000 apartment buildings could enter winter without an alternative heat supply.

That vulnerability is especially dangerous because restoring electricity does not automatically restore heat. Replacement boilers can help, but they must be installed locally, supplied with fuel, and connected to functioning heat networks, often while the same city remains under repeated attack. Last winter, UN monitors recorded at least 74 Russian attacks on heating infrastructure across Ukraine—attacks that left hundreds of thousands of people without heat or hot water for weeks on end in frigid temperatures. Keeping the national electric grid running did not make those homes habitable in freezing weather. Residents can try to solve the problem with electric space heaters, but their widespread use could overload the already fragile grid.

To prepare for even worse conditions this winter, Kyiv has been building up gas reserves. As of August, Ukraine’s gas stocks stood at 13.1 billion cubic meters, compared with 9.6 billion a year earlier. Still, stored gas must reach functioning heat and power plants, and electricity must pass through functioning transmission networks, both of which Russia has been continually attacking. Regardless of how resilient the national system is, Russia’s incessant targeting of particular Ukrainian cities has left them exposed, because local sources of substitute supply are often attacked before damaged facilities can be restored.

Meanwhile, Ukrainians are paying for the survival of their energy system in ways that capacity measures miss. Across the country, businesses have to run expensive generators, cut shifts, or close. Households spend more for fewer hours of reliable service. Declining industrial demand can make the electricity balance sheet look healthier, when the reality is that the economy is deteriorating under the pressure of relentless attacks. Those losses weaken the tax base and increase the need for foreign support. Russia does not have to switch off Ukraine’s power to impose an extraordinary burden.

Russia has also felt the growing effects of the energy war. In theory, Moscow has a much larger cushion: when the war began, its refineries produced more fuel than its domestic market consumed, and many had excess capacity to distill crude than to convert the resulting streams into finished motor fuel. Losing one distillation unit did not necessarily mean losing an equivalent share of gasoline or diesel supply. But Ukraine’s attacks this year have tested the limits of that flexibility.

In June, Ukrainian drones hit one of two processing units at Moscow’s Kapotnya refinery and hit the other six days later. The refinery was shut down after the June attacks and although it subsequently resumed operations, another strike again halted processing in September. Elsewhere, Ukrainian attacks have damaged complex units that are designed to raise fuel yields and improve quality; they can take six to eight months to fix, so they might remain unavailable through the coming winter heating season. Spare primary distillation capacity cannot replace damaged secondary equipment needed to turn intermediate products into finished gasoline and diesel.

To make up for these losses, the Russian government has loosened fuel standards, allowing naphtha blended with octane boosters to enter the gasoline supply, and extended state subsidies to imported gasoline. Russian companies can also send crude to its ally Belarus for processing or import finished products. These measures have helped fill consumers’ gas tanks without restoring the equipment that previously supplied them. But they come at a cost, which consumers, companies, and the state will all ultimately have to absorb.

Not all Ukrainian drone strikes have had such long-term impact. In March 2026, for example, Ukraine attacked the Ust-Luga oil terminal, which is on the Baltic Sea near St. Petersburg, continually for almost two weeks, halting oil shipments from that terminal. But shipments from Ust-Luga resumed a few weeks later, and by late April Russia’s total seaborne crude exports averaged 3.8 million barrels a day—up seven percent from March and 11 percent from a year earlier. This increase helped offset falling exports of refined products, as damage to refineries left more crude available for export.

For Moscow, the most pressing question is how to manage fuel shortages. Through higher prices and rationing, it can ensure that the military remains well supplied even if civilians have to cut back. It can also use subsidies and price controls to provide some relief to consumers. None of these options are costless, but as long as the government is willing to impose lower living standards and absorb the resulting political costs, it retains considerable room to maneuver. Ukrainian strikes can force Russia to spread its air defense thinner, absorb money and labor, and reduce its income from refining. They can somewhat reduce the oil and gas revenue that finances Russia’s war, but none of these costs are likely to make the war prohibitively expensive for the Kremlin.

Paradoxically, the growing success of Ukraine’s strikes against Russian refineries has brought new pressure on Kyiv itself. To buttress its increasingly constrained domestic market, Russia has reduced the amount of diesel it is selling abroad. Normally, higher prices should encourage refiners elsewhere to take up the slack, but the U.S. war against Iran has made that adjustment harder. Combined Russian and Middle Eastern net diesel and gasoil exports in September were 1.3 million barrels a day below their level a year earlier, according to the International Energy Agency. That global supply crunch has also affected the United States, which has now blamed Ukraine for stubbornly high U.S. diesel prices.

Ordinarily, the United States enjoys a substantial diesel surplus and is the world’s largest diesel exporter. Yet in the absence of export controls, quotas, or export tariffs, the U.S. market is exposed to galloping international prices, even when there is no U.S. fuel shortage. It is also far easier for the Trump administration to press Ukraine to stop its attacks on Russian refineries—and to enter a diesel agreement with Russia—than to restore lost Middle Eastern diesel exports.

In reality, Washington’s moves are unlikely to add much to global supply in the near term: redirecting existing exports does not create additional fuel. A larger, lasting increase would require Russian refineries to recover and stay in operation. This is where Washington has an incentive to restrain Ukraine’s attacks—even to the point of withholding, or threatening to withhold, Kyiv’s access to Starlink satellite service or U.S. intelligence, or to block European purchases of U.S. weapons for Ukraine. If Ukraine complies with U.S. demands, one risk is that Moscow could obtain protection for its refineries while continuing its assault on Ukrainian cities.

The United States is not the only external player in this widening energy war. For Europe, Ukraine’s collapse would create a security threat far greater than the cost of replacing transformers or financing fuel imports. Thus, in mid-October, the EU’s ambassador to Ukraine described a $1.1 billion energy support package Europe is preparing for the coming winter, which will enhance Ukraine’s ability to recover. But if some European countries, despite the European Commission’s opposition, accede to the Trump administration’s proposal to expand European purchases of Russian gas in the hope of lowering energy costs for households and industry—that could bolster Moscow’s earnings and its ability to finance the war. Europe’s energy choices matter on both sides of the contest.

China and India may also affect the future direction of the energy war. Thus far, both countries’ purchases of Russian crude have helped sustain Russia’s war machine. Moreover, by refining discounted barrels of Russian crude, both countries can benefit their own industries, and by exporting more refined fuel, they can ease the global crunch that is now driving U.S. pressure on Ukraine. But the ability of these countries to raise their exports depends on available crude and government approval—particularly in China. Over time, Russia’s leverage from the current diesel crunch will shrink as other suppliers recover, trade adjusts, and consumption falls. But that shift is unlikely to happen in time for this winter: the International Energy Agency expects diesel markets to remain tight well into 2027.

For both Russia and Ukraine, the aim of the energy war is clear: to disrupt or break the adversary’s economy to the point of undermining support for the war or making it unsustainable. But there are few indications that either side can achieve such an outcome. U.S. pressure could nevertheless produce a cease-fire or a broader settlement, even on terms deeply unfavorable to Ukraine. A mutual halt to energy strikes would give both sides relief and time to recover.

For now, however, an enormous gap remains between the conditions that Moscow and Kyiv deem minimally acceptable for even a temporary cease-fire. High levels of mutual distrust have increased that gap further. Moreover, a deal to protect only refineries, as the Trump administration has called for, would leave Ukraine’s cities and other infrastructure still exposed to Russian attacks. And Moscow has less incentive to accept any such deal if it can secure Ukrainian restraint through pressure by Washington, without making concessions of its own.

If diplomacy fails, the most likely scenario is another winter of sustained strikes on energy and other infrastructure by both sides. For Russia, the loss of refining capacity has been manageable thus far, but that does not preclude larger problems ahead. With each additional loss of capacity, Russia’s protection of its military energy supply becomes more expensive. Certain regions that have already been squeezed, such as occupied Crimea, could become especially vulnerable. If Moscow is unable to make repairs fast enough or otherwise compensate for further losses of refining capacity, regional fuel shortages could develop into wider disruptions to civilian transport and industry.

For Ukraine, the central risk is a cascading series of Russian attacks on the same city. During prolonged cold, repeated attacks—whether on Kyiv or Kharkiv or Odessa—could exhaust backup supplies faster than crews can restore service. Prolonged pump stoppages could lead to a disastrous local scenario of ruptured water supply, district heating, and sewage pipes, even if electricity remains available elsewhere in the country. A milder winter and timely equipment deliveries could keep Ukraine ahead of such a crisis, but delays in air defense deliveries or the loss of a critical heating facility could overwhelm a city whose electricity balance looked adequate. An indication of danger will be local power outages that continue to grow despite repairs, showing that recovery is unable to keep pace with attacks.

As it prepares for what will likely be its harshest winter yet, Kyiv faces a dilemma: if it reduces or suspends its attacks on Russian refineries to preserve U.S. military support, it will give Moscow a repair window while the Ukrainian grid remains exposed. If Kyiv refuses, it could continue its strikes but face intelligence or Starlink cutoffs that could make its infrastructure harder to defend. An additional influx of European funds and technology could soften the consequences—and perhaps provide some alternatives to U.S. communication networks—but will not fully replace U.S. intelligence and air-defense capabilities. That dilemma will not go away as long as Ukraine’s own infrastructure is exposed to attack.

As it approaches its fifth anniversary, the conflict in Ukraine has transformed energy warfare in ways that have few precedents. The advent of cheap long-range weapons has allowed each side almost unlimited access to the energy infrastructure on which the other depends. Yet at the same time, innovations in distributed power generation, batteries, and mobile equipment have given defenders novel ways to preserve essential services and continually recover, even in the face of large-scale attacks. Russia’s deep industrial reserves and Ukraine’s impressive adaptation have shown that complex energy systems can withstand enormous damage. That endurance has been far greater than a tally of destroyed assets on either side would suggest.

But this same resilience can make wars longer and more devastating. As Ukraine and Russia have shown, each side can gain confidence from its ability to sustain repeated attacks while also persuading itself that an even more intense campaign against its adversary might finally succeed. In this dynamic, each adjustment and workaround by one side invites a fresh attack by the other, and each new strike accelerates the search for new ways to endure. This adaptation spiral can continue even as economic and human losses mount. Only a true cease-fire can break the cycle. And that will depend on political choices that energy attacks alone cannot determine.

The results are preliminary—it will take days to count all the ballots and weeks or more before a governing coalition comes together. Eisenkot’s path to power is hardly assured.

If the trend does hold and Netanyahu is ousted, this will please many people in Washington who want to see substantial changes in Israeli policy. But here’s something they should keep in mind. On the issues that journalists, analysts, and some members of Congress care most about—the Gaza Strip, the West Bank, and a two-state solution—Eisenkot’s change bloc does not differ all that much from the current coalition. On most of these issues, Netanyahu has been firmly within Israel’s mainstream, just like his opponents.

Much of Washington is afflicted with Bibi Derangement Syndrome. They have a hard time discerning between what is actually the fault of the prime minister and facts about Israeli politics that observers happen to not like. This syndrome underlines the inability of both its passionate supporters and its equally passionate critics to treat Israel as a normal country whose leaders confront internal political constraints and incentives. I don’t mean to let Netanyahu off the hook. He has rubbed a fair number of people the wrong way and played partisan politics in Washington. Former U.S. Secretary of State James Baker banned him from the State Department when Netanyahu said U.S. policy in the region was based on “lies and distortions.” President Bill Clinton, after meeting Netanyahu for the first time in 1996, asked: “Who the fuck does he think he is? Who’s the fucking superpower here?”

President Barack Obama and Netanyahu clearly could not stand to be in the same room. Jared Kushner relays in his book about the first Trump administration that his father-in-law always expected Netanyahu to undercut him. And President Joe Biden, who thought he knew Israel better than everyone, clearly found dealing with the Israeli leader to be trying. And what Americans who dislike him say about Netanyahu is mild in comparison to his Israeli opponents. And yet, personal tics aside, the prime minister clearly reflects a broader band of the Israeli electorate than many in the policy world want to believe.

On Sept. 26, Eisenkot and other leaders of the change bloc—Naftali Bennett, Yair Lapid, Avigdor Lieberman, and Yair Golan—released a document detailing their priorities should they form a government. In it, they announced a government of repair or reconstruction that would prioritize the establishment of a state commission of inquiry into the failures surrounding the Hamas attacks on Oct. 7, 2023, promised to pass term limits for the prime minister, draft a constitution, and oppose Haredi exemption from military service.

There was nothing in the document about the Gaza Strip; nothing about the E-1 settlement, which the current government has begun the process of building decades after its approval and which it is feared would make it even harder for Palestinians to travel between the northern and southern West Bank. And, crucially, there was nothing about a two-state solution. One might assume these topics were left out so that the platform could appeal to the widest possible group of Israelis—and that there will be some flexibility once a new government is in place. But after spending almost two weeks in Israel, talking to political figures, analysts, and just everyday Israelis, I am not so sure that’s the case.

On a warm Sunday evening, about 30 Israelis and I gathered in the living room of a modest home in central Israel to listen to two members of Beyachad, the joint list of Bennett’s “Bennett 2026” party and Yesh Atid under Lapid’s leadership. Both candidates emphasized the need for political and social reform in an Israel they see as battered and abused by almost two decades of governments suborned to Netanyahu’s parochial and personal interests at the expense of the rest of society. They were just as critical on foreign policy, blasting Netanyahu for Israel’s low international standing and not making the country’s case more forcefully and effectively in important national capitals and international fora like the United Nations. It was at this moment when the conversation took a turn many in Washington would not expect: Both candidates assailed the government for bungling Gaza, Lebanon, and Iran—making the case that for all of the effort Israel had expended across these battle zones, all three remained a threat. This was not a call for diplomatic solutions to the problems of Iran and its proxies, but a more effective military strategy. This position was hardly controversial among people who are wary of American and European demands for agreements that do little more than allow Israel’s enemies to live and fight another day.

They also stressed their opposition to a Palestinian state. They offered that after Oct. 7, the Palestinians could expect nothing more from a new Israeli government than autonomy in Area A (of the West Bank)—an artifact of the Oslo era in which the Palestinian Authority manages the affairs of the population. When one member of the small audience asked them about the situation in the West Bank, using the term “West Bank,” Bennett’s representative made sure he emphasized “Judea and Samaria,” the preferred terminology among the settlers for the West Bank that has become normalized among many Israelis. And when asked about their alliance with Yair Golan, whose Democrats party is considered left of center, both candidates reiterated their opposition to Palestinian statehood. Most Israelis agree with this position, according to polls.

The concern about Golan among at least one person attending the Beyachad event demonstrates just how opposed Israelis are to a Palestinian state. Golan’s position is hardly dovish. He opposes settlement expansion because he fears it would lead inevitably to annexation and a binational state. Instead, Golan believes that over the long term, a two-state solution is best for Israeli security, but because of Oct. 7, Golan supports “civil separation” and an indefinite security presence in the West Bank until a responsible Palestinian leadership can take over. I dare someone to define “long term” and “responsible” in this context. And even if you could get to this semantic promised land, Golan, like almost all Israeli politicians, believes Israel must hold on to the Jordan Valley, further truncating a Palestinian state.

It’s not that nothing would change under a new Israeli government led by the change bloc. Israel would work quickly to establish law and order in the West Bank, where settler violence against Palestinians has surged lately. Of the multiple pressures facing Israel, it is the easiest issue to deal with, but it is not easy. There will be a lot of politics around a crackdown, especially since this issue of settler violence is more complicated than the press, analysts, and members of Congress portray. Ben-Gvir could be out of power, but he is not going away, especially since he commands a loyal following among Israel’s middle and lower classes. He is among a strain on the Israeli right who do not care about sanctions or Israel’s standing in the West.

It is possible that Israel will have a new prime minister soon. And while Israel’s tone would likely be less pugnacious under a Prime Minister Eisenkot or Bennett, all the issues that separate the United States and Israel will remain. It is hard to see how a new government papers over the differences. It would be better to respectfully disagree and let the U.S.-Israel relationship change. A little bit of space would be good for everyone.

#5617. The Israel Netanyahu Made

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

DALIA DASSA KAYE is a Distinguished Visiting Fellow at Perry World House at the University of Pennsylvania and a Senior Fellow at the UCLA Burkle Center for International Relations.

Why Bibi’s National Security Vision Will Endure

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has dominated his country’s politics for the last four decades, and his influence will not end even if Israelis vote him out of office. Over the course of his tenure as Israel’s longest-serving prime minister, Netanyahu has dramatically reset the terms of the country’s national security politics. His persistent rejection of Palestinian statehood has helped push the idea firmly out of mainstream debates, and his longtime agitation for war against Iran is now widely accepted policy across Israel’s political and security establishment.

Before his first term as prime minister, Netanyahu was already a divisive figure. In 1993, as the leader of the opposition, he likened Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin’s Oslo agreement with Palestine Liberation Organization leader Yasir Arafat to British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain’s appeasement of Hitler ahead of World War II. His inflammatory rhetoric emboldened Israelis who considered any territorial compromise with the Palestinians to be treasonous, including the assassin who killed Rabin in 1995. Today, extreme policies and positions have become normalized under Netanyahu’s watch. He has presided over a three-year campaign of mass destruction and displacement in Gaza and the killing of more than 70,000 Palestinians. His government includes one politician, National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, who was barred from serving in the army in his youth because of his involvement in terrorist groups, and another, Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, who has said Israel needs “to wipe out Gaza” and that it should repeat what it “did in Gaza” in the West Bank. Defense Minister Israel Katz has made similar calls for applying the “Gaza model” to the West Bank and even southern Lebanon.

Netanyahu might soon lose power. His attacks on Israel’s judiciary, corruption trials, and refusal to take responsibility for security lapses ahead of Hamas’s terror attack on Israel on October 7, 2023, have generated significant opposition among Israelis. A change of leadership could have profound consequences for domestic policy. But whether Netanyahu leaves politics this week or years down the road, his national security vision will outlast him. He has had catastrophic success in crushing domestic support for Palestinian national aspirations and gaining mainstream backing for a permanent war footing to confront Iran. Netanyahu has changed the baseline of how Israelis think about their country’s security and turned his own uncompromising positions into an institutionalized reality. Future leaders may bring their own styles to Israeli security policy, but for the foreseeable future, at least, they will be coloring within lines that Netanyahu has drawn for them.

Netanyahu is not the first Israeli prime minister to reject Palestinian national aspirations. In a 1969 interview, Golda Meir famously asserted that there was “no such thing” as a distinct Palestinian nation. Menachem Begin embraced the 1978 Camp David accords with Egypt only because the agreement did not require him to come to terms with the Palestinians. Yitzhak Shamir refused to allow independent Palestinian representation at the 1991 Madrid Peace Conference.

The difference today is that the idea of Palestinian statehood is not even up for debate. This change did not happen overnight. Rabin’s assassination led to the dismantling of the Oslo model, which intended to produce a Palestinian state but in practice produced limited Palestinian self-government and continued Israeli control over much of the West Bank. Attacks by Palestinian terrorist groups throughout the 1990s deepened Israelis’ skepticism about the prospects of peace, which helped to bring Netanyahu to power in 1996.

Still, when Netanyahu’s first term ended in 1999, some politicians sought to keep the possibility of a political settlement alive. Israelis elected leaders such as Ehud Barak and Ehud Olmert who were open to territorial concessions to the Palestinians and who engaged in serious, albeit unsuccessful, U.S.-backed peace negotiations. Barak also spearheaded a unilateral Israeli withdrawal from southern Lebanon in May 2000. Even the hawkish Ariel Sharon, whose controversial visit to the Temple Mount in Jerusalem later that year helped spark the second Palestinian intifada, evacuated around 9,000 Israeli settlers and initiated the withdrawal of ground forces from Gaza in 2005.

But in the absence of a negotiated solution, the conflict worsened on all fronts. Hamas won the 2006 elections in Gaza and took full control of the territory by 2007. Military conflict between Israel and Hamas became routine, with major wars erupting in 2008, 2012, 2014, and 2021. Netanyahu became prime minister again after the first of those wars, in 2009. As Israel contended with frequent rocket fire from Gaza and missile attacks by the Lebanon-based Hezbollah in the north, Netanyahu linked the deteriorating security conditions to Israel’s earlier withdrawals from these areas, bolstering his case against territorial compromise.

Netanyahu’s policies also changed realities on the ground in the West Bank. Even before his return to power, after the second intifada broke out, Israel built a concrete wall to separate Israel from the West Bank that encroached substantially on internationally acknowledged Palestinian territory. Israeli checkpoints throughout the West Bank also severely limited Palestinians’ freedom of movement and access to East Jerusalem. In office, Netanyahu accelerated policies, including continued settlement expansion, that threatened the viability of a future Palestinian state. And he encouraged a growing divide between Hamas in Gaza and the Palestinian Authority in the West Bank by facilitating Qatari payments to Hamas-run Gaza for more than a decade, further undermining the Palestinian national project.

Even the coalition led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid that briefly removed Netanyahu from power from 2021 to 2022, and which promised change, did not substantially depart from Netanyahu’s positions. It is telling that this coalition—the only one able to push Netanyahu aside in the last decade and a half—boasted strong right-wing security credentials; Bennett was a longtime opponent of Palestinian statehood. Rather than taking steps to advance peace with the Palestinians, Bennett and Lapid focused on the Abraham Accords, the agreements negotiated by the first Trump administration in which several Arab states committed to normalizing relations with Israel without requiring concrete progress toward Palestinian statehood in return.

Few vocal supporters of Palestinian statehood remained within the political establishment on the eve of October 7, 2023. The attacks that day all but ensured that opposition to a Palestinian state would become the norm. No Israeli political leader, even in the opposition, speaks anymore about ending Israeli occupation of Gaza and the West Bank. After destroying much of Gaza and killing tens of thousands of Palestinians, Israel has regained military control of nearly 70 percent of the strip. Israeli settlers in the West Bank, whose numbers have more than doubled since the Oslo accords were signed, have been attacking Palestinians with alarming frequency. The settlers have ravaged Palestinian villages as the Israeli government and military look the other way, if not outright condone the violence. Palestinians, as a result, are now more focused on survival than statehood. Whether Netanyahu wins or loses this election, there is no clear path to the two-state future he has been so determined to avoid.

Netanyahu’s hardline position on Iran has been just as consistent as his hardline attitude toward the Palestinians. As early as 1992, as a member of the Knesset, he warned that Iran was “racing to develop nuclear weapons” and that the “threat must be uprooted.” More than three decades later, Netanyahu finally found in Donald Trump a U.S. president willing to launch the military campaign against Iran he had long sought. The first attack went forward in June 2025, when the United States and Israel struck key nuclear sites in Iran. Then, on February 28, they began a larger war targeting the Iranian leadership itself. As Netanyahu announced in a video message at the start of hostilities, “Together with the U.S., we will strike the terror regime hard and create the conditions that will enable the brave Iranian people to cast off the yoke of this murderous regime.”

The Israeli public and Israeli politicians expressed near-uniform support for the war in its early months; Lapid, the leader of the opposition, declared it a “just war.” By June, polls showed more public criticism of Netanyahu’s handling of the war. But few Israeli politicians have questioned the need to maintain military pressure on Iran, and active planning for renewed attacks continues. When Yair Golan, the leader of the left-wing Democrats party, called Israel’s killing of Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khameini a “mistake” that led to an even more extremist Iranian leadership, politicians across the political spectrum condemned him.

There used to be more debate among Israel’s military, intelligence, and political elite about how to deal with Iran. When Netanyahu’s government began seriously considering a conventional military strike on Iran’s nuclear facilities in 2012, for instance—a development that drew concern in Washington—Israeli leaders did not all immediately coalesce around the idea of attacking Iran. Notable voices such as Meir Dagan, the former head of Israel’s intelligence agency, the Mossad, were arguing against a military strike and questioning what such a move could accomplish. The debate over military options became moot, however, once the Obama administration announced an interim nuclear deal with Tehran, negotiated in 2013 via back-channel diplomacy through Oman. The deal surprised and infuriated Netanyahu, who tried without success to scuttle the final nuclear agreement, which was signed in 2015.

Netanyahu’s strident opposition to U.S. President Barack Obama’s diplomacy was not supported by all Israeli politicians, either. Many worried that campaigning against the nuclear deal would damage Israel’s relations with Washington and that Netanyahu’s growing alignment with the U.S. Republican Party threatened bipartisan support for Israel. Although the Israeli government officially opposed the agreement, several prominent political leaders and members of the security establishment argued that the deal could have upsides. Benny Gantz, the former chief of staff of the Israel Defense Forces, called the deal an “achievement” that could postpone Iranian nuclear capabilities for ten to 15 years. Some Israeli officials even argued against a U.S. withdrawal from the deal when Trump, with Netanyahu’s enthusiastic support, threatened to do so without first establishing an alternative arrangement. Even Barak, who had supported military strikes on Iran in the past and criticized the nuclear deal when it was signed, questioned whether Trump’s pulling out of the agreement was the “optimal” way to deal with Iran.

But these debates largely dissipated after Trump withdrew from the deal in 2018. When Iran resumed its nuclear enrichment program and with diplomatic efforts at a dead end, Netanyahu seized the opportunity to renew Israel’s shadow war with Tehran. Explosions at Iran’s Natanz nuclear facility in July 2020 and April 2021 were widely attributed to Israeli intelligence, as was the killing of Iran’s top nuclear scientist in November 2020, after the election of U.S. President Joe Biden, who had promised to restore the nuclear deal during his campaign. Even when the Bennett-Lapid coalition took over, Israeli positions on Iran remained consistent. The new government was less confrontational than Netanyahu’s, but it opposed a U.S. return to the nuclear deal. Bennett argued, as Netanyahu had done before, that an agreement would channel funds into “the Iranian terror machine.”

After October 7, with Netanyahu back in power, Israel escalated its attacks on Iran and its proxies. These actions included strikes in Tehran and Beirut that killed the heads of Hamas and Hezbollah, as well as tit-for-tat missile exchanges with Iran in April and October 2024. Aside from criticism that Netanyahu was timing the attacks to serve his own political interests, opposition figures such as Lapid and Gantz supported Netanyahu’s actions and the public backed what had become open military confrontation between Israel and Iran.

Israeli leaders have rallied behind Netanyahu’s preference for military confrontation over diplomacy, and not only with Iran. The idea that preventive military action should be used to manage any potential threat has guided Israel’s campaigns not only in Palestinian territories but also in southern Lebanon and in Syria after the fall of the Assad regime. Israel has been crossing new lines: it launched its first military attack on a Gulf Arab state in September 2025 with an airstrike in Doha targeting Hamas leaders. War has become Israel’s normal state of affairs.

Even if Netanyahu loses power, Israel’s approach to national security is unlikely to change. The opposition has articulated clear differences from the current government on matters of domestic policy, but major fissures on the Palestinian conflict, Iran, and Israel’s wider regional military campaigns are less apparent. As many Israeli analysts have put it, the political spectrum of the opposition is in essence “50 shades of right.”

Gadi Eisenkot, who has emerged as Netanyahu’s main rival, might slow settlement expansion in the West Bank and crack down on settler violence if he becomes prime minister. But those steps would amount to tactical adjustments, not strategic shifts. Eisenkot is not suggesting that Israel end its occupation of the West Bank or make a serious effort to advance a two-state solution. He has called the idea of a Palestinian state “delusional” in the wake of October 7. Other leading opposition figures, such as Bennett, not only oppose a Palestinian state but also avidly support settlement expansion.

A new government would not overhaul Israel’s policy in Gaza, either. There is widespread resentment among opposition politicians and the Israeli public over how Netanyahu waged the war, especially his resistance to cease-fire agreements that could have saved the lives of more hostages. Many believe Netanyahu prolonged the war to stay in power. But there is far less concern about the war’s effect on Palestinians; only a small number of Israeli human rights groups say Israel has committed genocide in Gaza. An Eisenkot government might be more inclined to advance the stalled Trump peace plan for Gaza, which has yet to make meaningful progress on reconstruction, Palestinian governance, or other key objectives. But like Netanyahu, Eisenkot does not support giving up Israeli control of Gaza without the full disarmament of Hamas.

More fundamentally, Israeli leaders across the board now consider control of territory to be essential to national security—whether in Gaza, the West Bank, southern Lebanon, or Syria. As the Middle East scholar Nathan Brown has observed, to the extent that debate about Israel’s security doctrine remains, it “is increasingly about how Israeli predominance should be exercised, not whether it should be maintained.” A new government might be willing, with Washington’s backing, to strike a nonaggression agreement with Syria that would require the removal of Israeli forces from border areas. Many leading Israeli politicians oppose such a withdrawal, however, lowering the odds of a deal going through. And it is even less likely that Israel would cede ground anywhere else.

No major Israeli politician is arguing for a change of course on Iran. The only significant domestic criticism of Netanyahu’s Iran policy has been about his failure to prevent diplomatic agreements, such as the short-lived memorandum of understanding the Trump administration reached with Tehran in June, which most Israelis see as favoring Iran and as failing to remove Tehran’s nuclear and missile threat. Israeli military leaders are already planning for the next round of war, including for a possible scenario in which Israel has to fight Iran on its own. Maintaining this war footing seems to have broad political support, too. Lapid has argued that Israel should go even further in its attacks on Iran, including by bombing “all energy facilities” to make sure the “Iranian economy is demolished.”

Netanyahu would have found it far more difficult to bring his hardline vision into the mainstream had he faced a political opponent who could powerfully advocate for a fundamentally different vision for Israel’s place in the region and path to peace. There have been no Rabins in the Israeli opposition, no leaders willing to pivot from their own previous positions, tell the public the truth about what a real peace would require, and invest in the painstaking work of diplomacy. Instead, Netanyahu’s governing style has stifled robust debate on national security questions and lined up Israel’s defense leadership behind the prime minister’s own uncompromising worldview that favors continuous reliance on military force.

Neither did Netanyahu face serious pushback from Washington as he pursued policies that thwarted any hope for peace with the Palestinians, including the relentless destruction of Gaza. Even though U.S. public attitudes toward Israel have grown increasingly unfavorable, on both sides of the political divide, American presidents have become less willing to use diplomatic and economic leverage to cajole Israeli leaders to end dangerous activities such as settlement expansion. The lack of pressure from the United States—and some Arab states’ willingness to normalize relations with Israel without demanding serious progress toward reconciliation with the Palestinians—made it easier for Netanyahu to pursue hardline policies without making concessions. It weakened dissenting voices within Israel, too, who could not convincingly criticize those policies on the grounds that they would damage Israel’s security or relationships with its most important partners.

Of course, regional realities complicate Netanyahu’s worldview. The plight of the Palestinians continues to resonate with foreign publics, limiting how far pivotal countries such as Saudi Arabia will go in normalizing relations with Israel. Yet future Israeli leaders may not be overly concerned about that problem, convinced that Israel, with its military might and technological edge, has too much to offer potential partners to be ignored or isolated. Many Israeli politicians today are dismissive of the increasingly common view in the region that Israel is as great a security threat as Iran.

A serious reversal of Netanyahu’s security policies will not be possible until a new Israeli leader emerges with a bold alternative plan for how Israel achieves lasting peace—and with the ability to bring the Israeli public on board. And as long as the United States remains Israel’s closest political and security partner, helping this shift along will require U.S. policies that stop enabling and start pushing back on Israeli settlement expansion, territorial occupation, and reckless military escalation.

None of this is happening during the current Israeli election season. In fact, there is little chance of it happening any time soon. Netanyahu has triumphed in squashing Israeli support for Palestinian national aspirations and normalizing military confrontation with Iran. It’s a powerful legacy. But it is not one that will make Israel, the region, or the world better off.

U.S. President Donald Trump is facing yet another court challenge to his tariff-driven war on trade, after an unbroken string of prior defeats that leave the future of his mercurial trade policy increasingly uncertain.

Last week, the same company that successfully challenged Trump’s global tariffs under the 1977 International Emergency Economic Powers Act filed suit against his latest attempt to reach back in time. At issue is Trump’s use of an obscure and never-used statute, Section 338 of the 1930 Trade Act, to punish Canada for its allegedly discriminatory behavior toward U.S. commerce.

This new trade fight may not be as clear-cut as some of the prior Trump defeats in court, though.

The central thrust of the lawsuit challenging Sec. 338—part of the notorious Smoot-Hawley law that ushered in the Great Depression—is that the law was explicitly designed for a different world.

The statute, which had never been used and in fact was unknown even to many trade experts until Trump resurrected it, was designed to give the United States a way to push back against countries such as France and Great Britain that were discriminating against U.S. exports as part of “imperial preference”—a circling of the wagons that gave preferential treatment to colonies at the expense of global trade in the dawning days of the depression.

The statute was “all about imperial preferences,” said Sara Albrecht, the chairman and chief executive of the Liberty Justice Center, which filed the lawsuit on behalf of a U.S. business harmed by Trump’s latest trade spat.

And even more clearly, the statute was meant to give the White House a tool to deter other countries from discriminating against U.S. exports—not a cudgel to wield against countries (such as Canada) whose tariffs on U.S. goods were dollar-for-dollar retaliation for Trump’s first round of trade restrictions.

“Retaliation is not discrimination,” Albrecht told Foreign Policy.

But even if the narrowly tailored 1930 statute were valid for modern-day trade fights, that doesn’t matter, the suit (and many scholars) argue, because Sec. 338 is—legally speaking—dead and buried.

That 1930 law has been updated and superseded not once but twice in later legislation, first through the 1962 Trade Act and later in Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act. Everything that was in the old statute was rehashed in more modern law—and, according to established legal doctrine, that Hoover-era law has arguably been implicitly repealed. What’s more, the updated version of the statute includes a lot more limits and safeguards, including formal investigation of alleged wrongdoing by other countries. The tool that Trump has turned to for his fourth bite at the apple would dodge all those limits.

“Congress did not intend to leave in place a parallel authority that would allow the President to impose the same remedies, for the same conduct, free of every one of those safeguards,” the plaintiffs argued in their lawsuit before the U.S. Court of International Trade.

The Trump administration has leaned heavily on that updated version of the statute—Sec. 301 of the 1974 Act—for many of its tariffs, from the first-term duties on Chinese exports to the second-term attempt to slap tariffs on every U.S. trading partner. (Those tariffs are also currently facing an uphill battle in court.)

But if Sec. 338 is still valid, scholars such as Philip Zelikow argue, then Sec. 301 cannot be. That would mean that the main weapon the Trump administration is relying on to continue global tariffs in the wake of this year’s Supreme Court setback is itself invalid: Trump cannot have his cake and eat it, too.

The whole battle over Trump’s tariffs comes at a time when the United States continues to pursue elusive trade deals with major partners, including Canada. Trump administration officials have said that a deal with Canada—with whom the United States has had a free-trade agreement since the 1990s—is close, but Canadian officials say a final agreement remains far off.

This latest clash over what seems to be an arcane relic of U.S. trade legislation is important because of the way that the Trump administration has sought to weaponize trade to a much greater degree in the second term than in the first. During Trump’s first term, his administration relied on relatively targeted and legally defensible authorities to slap tariffs on big trading partners.

But starting early in his second term, Trump sought to hike tariffs on every country in the world, starting with a novel use of a Carter administration-era law that had never been used to impose tariffs—and with good reason, as the Supreme Court later ruled. That 1977 law doesn’t give the president the power to levy tariffs, even for a self-proclaimed national-security “emergency,” such as a half-century of persistent trade deficits.

Undeterred, the Trump administration replaced the outlawed first batch of tariffs with a new tranche under a provision of the 1974 Trade Act. That, too, was struck down by courts, but it had expired by then anyway.

So the administration reached for a third option, also under the 1974 law, which hit 99 percent of U.S. trade with tariffs on the grounds that every U.S. trading partner was lax on combating forced labor in supply chains. That attempt, too, is under legal challenge, and it’s likely to be struck down.

And then Trump reached even further back in the history books to find a way to punish Canada for retaliating against his previous rounds of tariffs by invoking, for the first time, a statute that even protectionist President Herbert Hoover was loath to sign into law.

The irony is that all of Trump’s efforts to hike import duties paid by U.S. businesses and consumers by every means possible is that they have conspicuously failed to achieve any of their stated goals.

Exports are falling and imports are rising, widening the very trade deficit that Trump said was a national emergency. Tariffs have failed to drive a manufacturing renaissance, either in employment or the construction of new factories. Nor have the tariffs been a reliable source of additional revenue for a government saddled with a huge budget deficit and $40 trillion in debt: Net tariff revenues as a share of overall revenues are actually lower than at any time in the past decade, in part because courts have made the U.S. government refund tariffs collected illegally.

And increasing the price of nearly everything from nearly every country in the world has exacerbated inflation, which is a problem for the Trump administration as it tries to grapple with an affordability crisis ahead of the midterm elections. U.S. inflation is now running at 3.7 percent year-on-year, higher than when Trump took office, fueled by higher energy prices (fruit of the Iran war), tariffs, and the artificial intelligence build-out. That self-inflicted higher inflation makes a serious case for the Federal Reserve to hike interest rates this week—the very opposite course of what Trump has advocated for years.

The through line of all of Trump’s second-term tariff fights, from the Rose Garden “Liberation Day” tariff announcement that slapped duties on penguins to his efforts to resuscitate moribund trade relics to punish Canada, is that Congress is missing in action. Despite a handful of tepid attempts early in Trump’s second term to wrest back the constitutional powers of taxation from the White House, there have been no serious legislative efforts to reverse nearly a century of congressional delegation of trade authority to the executive branch.

“If Congress were doing its job, we wouldn’t be in this place,” said Albrecht of the Liberty Justice Center.

That may soon change. U.S. midterm elections are only a week away, and Democrats are widely predicted to win back the House of Representatives and perhaps even control of the Senate. Trump’s one-man war on trade (and trade law) may soon have an expiration date.

Trump Could Salvage the Kremlin’s Objectives in Ukraine

Vladimir Putin plays the long game. For decades, the Russian leader has worked to subjugate Ukraine. For decades, he has also cultivated ties with Donald Trump. Now, success at the latter endeavor is allowing Putin to recover from failures at the former: after nearly five years of thwarted Russian objectives in the war on Ukraine, the U.S. president is helping him win. By easing sanctions on Russia, cutting military and economic assistance to Ukraine, disrupting intelligence cooperation, and pressuring Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky to accept Putin’s war aims, Trump is making Russia’s successful annexation of substantial Ukrainian territory and de facto control over Kyiv’s future decisions more likely than at almost any point since February 2022.

Since Putin launched his full-scale invasion of Ukraine, battlefield momentum has swung back and forth between the two sides. Russian forces had the advantage at the start. But by the fall of 2022, Ukraine had seized the offensive, liberating so much territory once held by Russian troops that Washington worried Putin might use a nuclear weapon to stop Ukrainian advances. The war then settled into a tragic grind on the frontline—until last spring. Momentum returned to Ukraine after Kyiv deployed new long-range missiles capable of striking targets deep inside Russia, new midrange drones able to attack Russian supply routes in the occupied parts of eastern Ukraine, and improved interceptor drones that could better defend against Russian attacks on Ukrainian cities. Putin’s army proved unable to advance on the frontline; Ukrainian troops even made incremental progress in liberating territory.

But Trump has helped return the momentum to Russia. Stymied at the frontline, Putin has shifted his focus to terrorizing Ukrainian society through rocket and drone strikes on both civilians and the infrastructure their livelihood depends on—energy and water in particular—driven by the hope that a suffering population would pressure Zelensky to capitulate. New weaponry has made this terrorist campaign especially brutal, in particular the mass production of ballistic missiles and a new drone, the Geran 5, equipped with a powerful Chinese rocket engine that Ukrainian air defenses cannot intercept. Because Russia has more troops, greater military production capacity, and some new and better weapons, Putin believes that he can afford to persist despite the seeming stalemate.

Trump’s support has bolstered Putin’s belief that time is on his side. In just the last few weeks, Trump and his allies have expanded his help to Moscow: suspending sanctions, buying Russian diesel, disrupting Ukraine’s access to Starlink and Starshield communications systems, and even insisting that Zelensky must go. “It is equally clear that when some partners ease the pressure on Russia,” Zelensky has noted, “it only reinforces Putin’s belief that his goals are within reach.” The combination of less support from the United States, especially when it comes to air defenses, and faster Russian drones and more missiles will make the coming winter the most brutal yet for Ukrainians.

Putin’s long game with Trump began years ago, driven not so much by some specific objective, but by the hope that Kremlin influence on him might someday prove useful—a common KGB tactic. Now that day has come.

Years before Trump first ran for president, Russian actors close to Putin pursued business ties with the then real estate mogul, including most famously when Trump collaborated with Emin and Aras Agalarov to host the Miss Universe pageant in Moscow in 2013. Putin then orchestrated unprecedented foreign interference in the 2016 presidential election to help Trump and hurt his opponent Hillary Clinton. Whether the Russian theft and publication of Clinton campaign emails, combined with a comprehensive social media effort to bolster Trump, affected the 2016 election outcome is impossible to prove. That Putin was trying to influence the result is without question.

#5608. The push to DC: Why data centers are rethinking power architecture

As AI pushes rack densities higher, 800 VDC is emerging as more than a new way to power compute and is changing the way data centers are designed from the grid to the rack.

As rack densities climb toward 1 MW and beyond, traditional low-voltage alternating current (AC) architecture is being pushed into territory it wasn't originally designed to handle. A primary reason the industry is increasingly looking toward 800 VDC to support the next generation of high-density compute.

But the bigger question isn't whether direct current (DC) can power these new racks. It's where the conversion to DC should happen and how much of the traditional power architecture is still necessary.

Read SMA's white paper, published by Tim Stocker, data center system architect, The Road to 800 VDC, for a deeper look at the solution architectures, efficiency calculations and design considerations behind the transition to 800 VDC.

One near-term answer is the sidecar, a power-conversion unit positioned next to the compute rack.

Sidecars give operators a practical way to introduce 800 VDC racks into facilities built around existing low-voltage AC distribution. Rather than redesigning the facility, operators can convert AC power to DC beside the rack.

That makes sidecars an important bridge between today's infrastructure and emerging DC compute. But much of the existing electrical architecture remains in place. Power still moves through transformers and low-voltage UPS infrastructure before another conversion takes place near the rack.

Each stage introduces losses and nearly all of that lost energy ultimately turns into heat, putting more pressure on the cooling system to remove it.

SMA's white paper models the difference. In the architecture studied, delivering 1 MW through a traditional AC system with rack-level conversion results in approximately 80 kW of power loss per megawatt delivered to the rack. Converting medium-voltage AC to 800 VDC outside the building and distributing DC directly reduces that loss to approximately 20 kW.

At data center scale, the difference becomes more than significant.

Efficiency is only part of the equation. Data center operators are also under pressure to maximize one of their most valuable resources: white space.

A native DC architecture takes a different approach. Instead of generating 800 VDC beside the rack, conversion from medium-voltage AC to DC happens once, outside the data center building. Native 800 VDC is then distributed throughout the facility.

Much of the supporting electrical infrastructure can move outside as well, including power-conversion equipment, switchgear and energy storage.

Inside, the architecture becomes more simplified: compute, cooling and DC busway. There are fewer conversion stages, fewer pieces of electrical equipment and more room for compute.

Any new data center architecture still must meet demanding resilience requirements.

DC creates new options. A rack can be supplied by two independent DC paths, each capable of carrying the full load. During normal operation, both can remain active and share the load.

Unlike AC sources, DC sources don't have phases that must be synchronized. With the appropriate protection architecture, both sources can continuously supply the rack, potentially eliminating the need for a traditional transfer event. Sharing the load between two paths also reduces current on each path, helping lower electrical losses without sacrificing redundancy.

Moving energy storage outside the building can expand its role.

Instead of primarily waiting to provide backup power, storage integrated into an external DC power system can help smooth large power swings from AI workloads, provide ride-through during grid disturbances and control how quickly a facility returns to full power following an event. Depending on the system and market, storage could also support other grid services.

Sidecars have an important role as data centers begin adopting 800 VDC. They provide a practical way to deploy new compute infrastructure without rebuilding existing electrical systems.

But the longer-term opportunity is bigger than changing the voltage delivered to the rack.

Moving conversion outside the building and distributing native DC can reduce conversion stages and electrical losses, free up valuable space for compute, simplify redundancy and give energy storage a more active role.

For an industry racing to add enormous amounts of computing capacity, those aren't incremental changes. They represent an opportunity to rethink data center power architecture from the grid to the rack.

#5599. Inside the resilience gap

The 2026 State of Grid Resilience puts data behind the pressures utility leaders face as they strengthen resilience while balancing affordability, performance and investment.

Utilities do not need research to tell them the operating environment has become more demanding. They see it in the systems they manage, the investment decisions they make and the expectations customers and regulators place on them.

New research helps quantify that experience.

Three in four utility and energy industry professionals surveyed for the 2026 State of Grid Resilience say operational risk is higher than it was five years ago. More than half expect load growth on their distribution systems to be very or extremely significant over the next decade. Nearly four in five expect resilience-related investment to increase over the next five years.

At the same time, 61% identify affordability and customer rate pressure as the biggest barrier to accelerating that investment.

Taken together, the findings show a challenge utilities have navigated for years. They are committed to resilience, but how resilience is measured, funded and deployed can affect how quickly they can act.

Utilities already recognize a distinction between reliability and resilience. Nearly half of respondents define reliability as day-to-day operational performance and resilience as performance under disruption.

The measures guiding investment do not always reflect that distinction.

Traditional reliability metrics such as SAIDI, SAIFI and CAIDI provide an important and consistent view of everyday grid performance. But only 48% of respondents agree that current reliability metrics adequately capture performance during major disruptive events.

The difference becomes clearer when utilities are asked what matters during those events. More than half say restoring service as quickly as possible is the most important outcome during a major storm or disruption. When asked what best demonstrates a resilient grid, respondents first point to limiting the number of customers affected and quickly restoring normal operations.

Yet investment decisions remain weighted toward traditional reliability. SAIDI and customer outage frequency are the most-cited metrics influencing investment decisions. Only 17% cite resilience-specific measures such as extreme-event recovery and adaptive capacity.

Reliability measures remain essential. Adding measures of what customers experience during major disruptions, including how many are affected and how quickly service is restored, can provide a more complete picture of performance under stress.

Utilities also confirmed a familiar tension between investing and keeping electricity affordable.

Nearly four in five respondents expect resilience investment to increase over the next five years. Yet 61% identify affordability and customer rate pressure as the biggest barrier to moving faster, more than three times the percentage citing competing capital priorities.

Utilities are already making the case for resilience in terms of customer value. Nearly nine in ten respondents justify resilience investments through service quality and reliability, either alone or combined with cost avoidance. Forty-seven percent use both cost avoidance and reliability improvement.

Better measures of performance during major events could help strengthen that case. If an investment can limit the number of customers affected or restore service faster, utilities have another way to show its value to customers and regulators.

Affordability makes prioritization especially important. Utilities must determine not only how much to invest, but where that investment can make the greatest difference.

The research reinforces the attention utilities are placing on the distribution grid.

Fifty-eight percent of respondents say most customer-impacting outages originate on distribution. This aligns with U.S. Department of Energy research finding that more than 90% of electric power interruptions originate on the distribution system. Fifty-three percent of respondents also identify distribution as part of the grid offering the greatest opportunity to improve customer outage outcomes over the next decade.

Their investment priorities tell a similar story. Fault location, isolation and service restoration (FLISR), protective devices and sectionalizing and distribution automation rank highest for resilience return.

Full deployment remains limited. Automated switches and reclosers are fully deployed at 33% of respondents’ utilities, compared with 18% for FLISR and 12% for self-healing distribution automation.

Respondents identify faster restoration and fewer customers affected as automation’s greatest operational benefits. Those are also among the outcomes they value most during major disruptions.

Investment is beginning to follow. Over the next five years, respondents expect the greatest increases in distribution investment in distribution automation, FLISR, undergrounding and pole and wire hardening.

“The research shows that utilities are being asked to strengthen grid resilience in an increasingly challenging operating environment,” said Anders Sjoelin, president and CEO of S&C Electric Company. “Operational risk is rising, with electricity dependence and demand growing and affordability remains a significant constraint. These findings reinforce the importance of prioritizing investments that deliver the greatest impact for customers and communities, particularly on the distribution grid.”

The research does not suggest utilities are waiting to act. Most expect resilience investment to grow and 79% are confident their current investment trajectory will meaningfully improve resilience within five years.

Instead, the findings support what many utilities are already experiencing. Better measures of performance during major events can help demonstrate the customer value of resilience. A clearer value case can support investment in the distribution technologies that limit customer impact and speed restoration.

The 2026 State of Grid Resilience provides a benchmark for that work and for an industry conversation already underway. As measurement evolves and investment continues, future research can show whether the gaps between resilience goals, affordability and deployment begin to narrow.

#5591. When does a BI value stop being the right number?

For energy companies, the assumptions behind a BI value can change materially during the policy period. Margins and throughput can shift, while repair timelines can extend. External events can also change the economics of an operation quickly. Yet the declared BI value and the policy provisions governing recovery may remain unchanged.

This raises a practical question: what should trigger a reassessment, and how will the policy respond if the exposure has changed materially?

A BI calculation draws on assumptions about revenue, margins, production, throughput, fixed costs and recovery time. Those inputs can change at different rates and for different reasons.

For a downstream operator, a significant movement in crack spreads can change the earnings exposure associated with an interruption. For a midstream operation, a change in throughput or a prolonged constraint on a critical pipeline can alter the financial impact. Across the energy value chain, an extended repair or recovery period can materially increase the duration of a loss.

Organizations should establish materiality thresholds for reassessment, supported by a consistent methodology for revisiting the valuation when conditions change. Where a material change increases exposure, organizations may also need to consider whether to redeclare their values.

Aon’s approach to business interruption valuation provides a methodology for reassessing the valuation when material changes occur.

A stated volatility allowance can overstate the protection available if the exposure has increased materially or operations resume during the recovery period.

The Lloyd’s Market Association introduced an updated Onshore Energy BI Volatility Clause in 2024 after carriers identified the previous version as unsuitable for partial losses. The revised clause was intended to address the policy’s indemnity exposure in those circumstances.

Organizations should model the provision against realistic loss scenarios rather than rely on the stated percentage.

Production may resume at 60% of capacity while repairs continue. The loss has not ended.

Revenue may recover partially while margins remain affected, equipment is still under repair or other constraints limit operations. The financial loss can therefore follow a different trajectory from physical production and the policy response may change with it.

Some onshore property provisions can reduce the applicable volatility cap when partial operations resume, potentially taking the cap below 100% of the reported BI value. The specific outcome depends on the policy wording and the circumstances of the loss. Organizations should discuss whether such a provision is appropriate for their operations and understand how it could restrict recovery.

As Greg Reynolds, Onshore Energy Property Lead at Aon, puts it:

“The critical issue is how the loss develops through disruption and recovery. Partial operations, shifting margins and repair delays can change both the earnings impact and the policy response.”

Effective modeling tests how critical assets, dependencies, mitigation measures and financial impacts evolve across different loss scenarios.

Downstream businesses may be highly sensitive to refining margins, while many midstream businesses rely predominantly on fee-based revenue. LNG exposures may also be less sensitive to margin volatility depending on their commercial structure. A volatility mechanism designed around commodity margins may therefore have limited relevance for some operations, making alternative wording or a carve-out more appropriate.

The starting point should be the economics of the business rather than a standardized treatment across the energy value chain.

Test each scenario against the declared BI value, volatility cap, deductible, indemnity period and treatment of partial operations to identify where the program has headroom and where assumptions or terms warrant attention.

#5589. Tesla now says it needs its own maps for FSD after years of dismissing maps

Tesla says it’s building its own maps to fix some of the most common problems with its “Full Self-Driving” system, including reading speed limits, parking, and choosing routes. The company’s head of AI described the effort on Tesla’s Q3 2026 earnings call.

It’s a notable shift for a company whose CEO spent years arguing that self-driving systems relying on maps are brittle. And it shows Tesla’s fleet is now doing the same job Waymo’s cars do: mapping the roads so the self-driving system knows what it can’t reliably figure out on its own.

Tesla fans argue that Waymo’s approach differs because it creates HD maps with lidar, but ultimately both are creating their own maps; if your argument is that Tesla’s maps are less well-defined and needed for driving for different reasons than Waymo’s, you don’t really have an argument.

The question came from Tesla’s shareholder Q&A. A retail investor wrote that mapping “continues to be a problem” for FSD, listing speed limits, parking and typical routes as the three biggest issues, and asked why Tesla relies on third-party maps instead of its own.

Tesla’s VP of AI, Ashok Elluswamy, agreed that “we shouldn’t be depending on external people’s maps.” He said Tesla is building its own maps using a new AI-based mapping and navigation architecture, fed by what the fleet collectively learns, that will also learn individual drivers’ preferences and respond to voice commands.

He said the technology will start handling the beginning and end of trips with FSD v15, and over the following months take over navigation, parking and destination handling.

Tesla has gone back and forth on this before.

In 2015, when it launched Autopilot, Tesla told Fortune it had started building high-precision maps with “100 times the level of granularity” of standard navigation, using data from its customer cars.

At its Autonomy Day in 2019, Elon Musk said Tesla had tried high-precision lane maps and realized it was “a huge mistake.” He called relying on them a “really bad idea” that makes a self-driving system “extremely brittle,” in the same presentation where he called lidar a “fool’s errand.” The message was clear: Waymo needed lidar and detailed maps, and Tesla would solve driving with cameras and AI alone, anywhere.

Seven years later, Tesla is building a map layer from its own fleet to fix the things its vision system gets wrong.

To be fair to Tesla, this isn’t an HD map in the sense Musk criticized. Tesla has always used navigation maps, and building its own instead of licensing them isn’t a contradiction on its own.

But look at the problems Tesla is trying to fix. Speed limits, parking and routes are all about understanding the environment, and FSD still often fails at it, especially with speed limit signs.

In the US, NHTSA is weighing an investigation into claims that Tesla Robotaxis speed through active school zones, covering about 1,000 Model Y and Cybercab vehicles.

A speed limit sign is about as simple as road information gets. If an end-to-end vision system can’t reliably read and apply it, and the fix is a better map, then vision alone isn’t doing the job Tesla said it would.

That brings Tesla closer to Waymo than it would like to admit.

Waymo maps a city by driving its sensor-equipped vehicles through it before launching service, and keeps those maps updated as its fleet drives. Tesla is now describing the same idea: its cars collect what they see, and that data feeds a Tesla-built map that helps every car drive better. The main difference is that Waymo’s cars have lidar on top.

It also fits how Tesla’s Robotaxi service actually operates: city by city, inside small geofenced service areas, with Tesla handling the fleet operations. Tesla isn’t talking about centimeter-level HD maps today, but a fleet-built map that keeps getting more detailed could easily head that way.

Hyperscalers’ interest in on-site and nearby energy storage has converted into firm orders this year, the global energy consultancy said last week in its latest industry outlook.

Energy storage deployments have far outpaced solar capacity additions in recent years, with the global cumulative installed solar-to-storage ratio falling from 94:1 in 2016 to 7:1 in 2026, BNEF said Thursday.

BNEF sees the capacity ratio stabilizing at 4:1 in 2030, and annual deployments remaining steady at 3:1 thereafter, as the energy storage industry matures.

Much of the new solar and storage capacity is colocated. The United States had installed 20.7 GW/66.4 GWh of energy storage projects colocated with 41.3 GW of solar by the end of 2025, about 30% of total colocated solar-plus-storage capacity, according to BNEF. Some 4 GW of storage was colocated with solar in the U.S. in the first nine months of 2026, the group said.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which extended federal tax credit eligibility for energy storage through 2035 while sunsetting solar eligibility later this decade, will support a pipeline of 83.7 GW/166.1 GWh of energy storage to be paired with 149.2 GW of solar, BNEF said.

The robust U.S. pipeline comes despite the Trump administration imposing trade barriers BNEF characterized as world-leading, including import restrictions on foreign-made power conversion systems and recent broad limits on imports of bulk power system equipment. The combined duty on stationary storage batteries imported from mainland China is 40.9%, according to BNEF.

“The US has been making the most aggressive [protectionist] efforts by favoring the domestic battery storage industry while restricting the use of supply chains involving mainland China through the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and other policy measures,” the report said.

Though still a relatively small share of overall U.S. battery deployment, on-site data center energy storage is growing quickly, with 4.9 GW/7.4 GWh expected this year — up from 500 MW/1.4 GWh in 2025, BNEF said.

The group sees on-site data center energy storage deployments reaching 6.3 GW/8.4GWh in 2027 and continuing through the decade thanks in part to large-scale deployments like the 2.6-GW battery array NextEra Energy plans to build alongside a 2-GW gas-fired power plant at a $100 billion proposed Brookfield Asset Management computing campus in Kentucky.

Multiple battery suppliers have announced firm commitments from data center customers or utilities serving them this year, BNEF said. Fluence Energy inked a $300 million deal with a data center operator for behind-the-meter batteries earlier this year, while Canadian Solar subsidiary e-Storage said in March it would deliver 500 MW/2.5 GWh of energy storage to “a major U.S. utility” in 2027 to support data center grid infrastructure.

Cumulative data center energy storage could reach 36 GW/66 GWh in 2036, much of it on grids overseen by the Electric Reliability Council of Texas, PJM and the Midcontinent Independent System Operator. Data centers in PJM are more likely to use energy storage for load shifting than behind-the-meter gas due to tighter restrictions on power plant emissions in the region, BNEF said.

Though lithium-iron-phosphate will remain the dominant chemistry for new energy storage deployments over the next 10 years, sodium-ion alternatives will begin to eat into its market share, reaching 9% penetration by 2036, BNEF said. Sodium-ion batteries can be passively cooled, reducing parasitic energy load, and may be more durable than lithium batteries over a 20-year service life, proponents say.

Led by Google, data center operators are experimenting with alternative chemistries that can discharge power for longer than lithium- or sodium-based batteries, including a proposed 10 MW/100 MWh zinc battery in West Virginia and a 300 MW/30 GWh iron-air battery in Minnesota. But those efforts are too early to include in deployment forecasts, BNEF said.

#5545. Smarter Cooling, Stronger Reliability: Advancing Wind Turbine Gearbox Performance

As the wind energy industry matures, operators and service providers are increasingly focused on maximizing turbine availability while controlling long-term maintenance costs. Although major drivetrain components often receive the spotlight, one overlooked system can have an outsized impact on turbine reliability and performance: gearbox oil cooling.

Heat exchangers play a critical role in maintaining proper oil temperature and viscosity within wind turbine gearboxes. When cooling systems become clogged with airborne debris, dust, or organic material, oil temperatures rise, lubrication performance declines, and component stress increases. Over time, these conditions can contribute to reduced gearbox efficiency, elevated maintenance demands, and avoidable downtime.

“Wind operators today are balancing increasing performance expectations with the realities of aging fleets and rising maintenance costs. Technologies that improve cooling efficiency and reduce service exposure can have a meaningful impact on long-term turbine reliability and operational profitability,” according to Mike Erickson, HYDAC’s Market Manager for the Wind Industry.

Traditional “panel cut” heat exchanger designs have long been susceptible to clogging due to their tightly packed fin structures. In many wind environments — particularly agricultural, dusty, or high-pollen regions — debris accumulation can severely restrict airflow. Oil leaks can further compound the issue by trapping contaminants within the exchanger core, making cleaning more difficult and labor intensive.

This operational challenge has driven the need for more resilient cooling technologies designed specifically for real-world wind farm conditions.

HYDAC’s new “Square Wave” heat exchanger design represents a significant advancement in addressing these reliability concerns. Engineered to allow debris to pass more freely through the exchanger fins, the design minimizes clogging while improving cooling efficiency under demanding operating conditions.

The importance of effective cooling cannot be overstated. Wind turbine gearbox oil typically operates within an optimal temperature range of approximately 45°C to 65°C. Even modest temperature increases can significantly impact oil viscosity. According to field data, a 12°C rise in oil temperature can reduce viscosity by nearly 28 percent — affecting lubrication quality and accelerating wear on critical drivetrain components.

Field trials have demonstrated measurable operational benefits from the Square Wave design. During a one-year trial on GE 1.x turbines, the upgraded exchanger consistently reduced clogging and lowered maintenance requirements compared to traditional panel cut cores. Operators reported reduced up-tower cleaning time and fewer temperature-related cooling faults.

Field validation across multiple turbine platforms has further reinforced the operational advantages of the Square Wave design. In one case study involving legacy 1 MW-class turbines operating in the Pacific Northwest, a major renewable energy operator installed pilot sets of Square Wave heat exchangers as part of a cooling performance evaluation. Installation teams reported the upgraded exchangers integrated seamlessly into the existing platform with no retrofit complications.

After initial operating data demonstrated improved cooling consistency, the operator expanded the evaluation program with additional units. Long-term SCADA analysis later showed turbines equipped with Square Wave technology operate approximately 20 to 25 percent cooler than comparable turbines using conventional heat exchanger designs under similar operating conditions. The data also highlighted another important advantage: the Square Wave cores continued to maintain airflow and cooling efficiency in debris-heavy environments where traditional exchanger designs became increasingly restricted over time.

The successful deployment also generated interest from additional wind service organizations seeking retrofit-ready cooling upgrades that could improve turbine reliability while reducing maintenance burdens across aging fleets.

“What operators are increasingly recognizing is that small improvements in thermal management can create meaningful gains in turbine reliability, maintenance efficiency, and long-term asset performance,” said Erickson. “The field data we’re seeing continues to validate the value of designing cooling systems specifically for real-world wind operating environments.”

More recently, long-term SCADA data from Mitsubishi platforms further validated the technology’s performance. After two years of operation, turbines equipped with Square Wave heat exchangers operated approximately 19 to 25 percent cooler than reference turbines using standard exchanger designs under comparable operating conditions.

For wind asset owners and service providers, the implications are substantial: Improved thermal management supports healthier oil viscosity, more stable gearbox operation, and potentially longer component life. At the same time, reduced cleaning frequency and fewer cooling-related service events translate directly into lower operational expenditures and increased turbine availability.

As the industry continues to prioritize lifecycle optimization and energy production efficiency, innovative subsystem upgrades such as advanced heat exchanger technology are becoming increasingly valuable. Rather than waiting for costly failures or performance degradation, operators are seeking practical retrofit solutions that improve reliability without requiring major system redesigns.

“Effective thermal management is critical to protecting gearbox health and maintaining oil performance over time. By addressing one of the most common causes of cooling degradation — debris accumulation — operators can improve uptime while reducing unnecessary maintenance interventions,” said HYDAC’s Erickson.

For wind stakeholders evaluating ways to improve gearbox reliability, reduce maintenance exposure, and enhance turbine performance, cooling system optimization deserves greater attention. Proven retrofit technologies like advanced heat exchanger designs can offer a practical path toward improved uptime and operational efficiency without requiring major system overhauls.

As fleets age and performance expectations continue to rise, collaboration between operators, service providers, and technology partners will be essential to building more resilient wind assets for the future. #5543. Capacity, clarity, cash and compliance: the 4 C’s of electric co-op funding success

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Compliance is where cooperative utilities tend to struggle the most, and the latest federal funding programs have introduced even tougher terms, writes Fedsprout CEO Aalap Shah.

Aalap Shah is president and CEO of Fedsprout, which helps electric cooperatives and utilities manage federal and state awards.

If you ask a lineman what it takes for a storm restoration to go well, the answer is usually not about having the right tools or truck. Quick restoration relies on good training, a plan before getting to the first pole and adequate back-office support.

Federal funding works the same way and the latest federal programs are demanding even more.

I’ve worked with electric cooperatives across 14 states and I’ve come to realize there is a pattern. Cooperative utilities do not have an eligibility problem, and most qualify for federal and state funding programs. Yet some co-ops never apply for funding. Others win an award and lose part of it because of disallowed costs.

In its October outlook, the U.S. Energy Information Administration expects record electricity demand this year and next, and projects the average U.S. residential electricity price at 18.2 cents/kWh this year, up from 16.5 in cents/kWh in 2024. Co-ops need funding to support load growth and reliability improvements while keeping rates affordable for members.

In my experience, co-ops that win federal and state funding stand apart in four areas, before and after the award: capacity, clarity, cash and compliance.

The capacity gap is clearly visible across most rural and small co-ops. At a typical co-op, the person who writes the funding application is the same person who runs finance and human resources. They often manage the award, too.

Clarity is not as apparent. Hundreds of federal and state programs are published each year and knowing which one fits the co-op’s needs and what sways the funder needs a dedicated effort. Identifying which federal and state regulations to follow is an arduous task.

I worked with one co-op that planned to reconductor aging lines for reliability. But the approach was not competitive. Once we framed it around economic development and new local hydropower that the added line capacity would allow, the project was one of 19 selected out of 659.

Cash is a constraint for most government-funded projects, which require cost share and pay on reimbursement. Cost share can mean taking on debt, which needs board approval. For reimbursements, the co-op must first spend, sometimes using its line of credit, and then wait weeks or months to be paid back.

Compliance is where co-ops struggle the most after the award. All federal and state money comes with regular reporting, requests for information, procurement rules and written procedures, and audits. Some co-ops learn only after the award what it really requires.

The latest federal funding programs make each of these four problems harder because applicants now have to plan for more cost and higher risk.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture recently announced the Affordable Rural Cooperative program, which offers $175 million in loans and grants for nuclear power supply and transmission. On a hypothetical $100 million transmission project, the grant is capped at $18.75 million, and the co-op would need to bring in at least $25 million of its own. The remainder can be borrowed from USDA.

The final rule for the Rural Energy for America Program, published Oct. 1, compounds the cash flow problem. REAP grants for renewable energy and efficiency projects will be awarded only to completed projects that have operated for at least 12 months.

A co-op now has to finance the work and ensure compliance with federal procurement standards even before knowing whether it will receive a grant. According to the new rules, an applicant is allowed one submission during an annual window and all applications will be ranked nationally, which raises the bar. USDA staff expect the first window to open in January 2027. Co-ops that miss this window will need to wait an entire year to apply.

Federal dollars are flowing again to co-ops, but on much tougher terms.

Even so, I expect that federal programs will be limited over the next few years because of many other competing budget priorities. With less funding available, more applicants will compete for each dollar.

On energy, the current administration has made its priorities clear. These are grid reliability and resilience, more generation, affordability and reducing reliance on foreign imports. I expect programs outside those priorities to be cut back. The new REAP rule, for example, does not allow for ground-mounted solar on certified cropland and restricts systems built with Chinese-made materials.

ARC is a new USDA program aligned with the administration’s focus on nuclear power. Geothermal should also draw more support as the technology matures. In February, the U.S. Department of Energy announced $171.5 million for next-generation geothermal field tests and drilling. In October, Fervo Energy’s Cape Station in Utah became the first enhanced geothermal project of its kind to reach commercial operation.

Hydropower is worth considering for co-ops looking for increased capacity. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission approved two orders cutting environmental review for low-impact hydropower actions and DOE is releasing nearly $430 million to upgrade aging facilities.

Even with midterm elections, I don’t expect the direction to change much. Both Democrats and Republicans support affordability and reliability. A proposal built around these two pillars will have a better chance of success.

This administration has also made data centers a priority and federal support is likely to follow. But before pursuing federal or state funding, co-ops need to answer two questions: Does the load growth from a data center actually serve members? Could water scarcity or community opposition derail the project?

I have been working with a group of Texas co-ops that are first-time recipients of state grants administered by the Public Utility Commission of Texas. Three lessons stood out.

First, the co-ops struggled to understand how much detail the funding agency needed for reporting and responding to requests for information. Different agency coordinators also asked for different details. The result was a lot of back-and-forth with the agency and delays in starting the project and receiving approvals. Having plans, reports, templates and RFI responses from other projects can reduce delays because the co-op knows what to expect.

Second, not every project can start on day one of the award. Explaining schedule delays to the awarding agency can be difficult. Waiver approvals can take weeks or months and significantly delay the project. Co-ops need to start early with waiver requests and prepare detailed budgets and task schedules before the award.

Third, purchases above state or federal thresholds must be competitively bid. A trusted contractor used over the years isn’t automatically approved for a project. Justifying a sole-source or noncompetitive award takes someone well-versed in procurement policy.

The co-ops that win get ahead of the four C’s. Co-ops that prepare a short list of projects and talk to board members about cost share in advance of the funding notice are in a better position to respond with stronger proposals.

Co-ops already work with outside engineers, auditors and attorneys for specialized work. When a big storm hits, they bring in outside crews because they don’t have year-round staff to manage these worst-case events.

Similarly, grant-writing and compliance experts can better prepare co-ops for writing proposals and managing compliance. Bringing in help before the funding notice is released, and again before the project starts, is a smart move.

Storm restoration relies on good planning, a ready crew and back-office support in advance of the storm’s arrival. Federal funding rewards the same preparation because today’s programs can be a bigger storm than co-ops have faced before.

#5541. Tesla’s $10B solar factory could get 35 years of tax breaks in Texas

Tesla’s proposed $10 billion “Project Crystal Sun” solar factory in Texas could get up to 35 years of local tax breaks on its equipment, according to the four agreements Fort Bend County commissioners approved on Thursday.

The vote was 4-0. And Tesla still hasn’t committed to building the plant in Texas.

As we first reported in August, Tesla applied for a 10-year school property tax limitation under the state’s JETI program, through Lamar CISD and the Texas Comptroller. The plan is a vertically integrated solar plant near Richmond, southwest of Houston, that would make everything from silicon ingots to finished modules, with commercial production targeted for Q1 2029.

Lamar CISD approved the school tax piece in September. The county was next.

On October 22, the commissioners court approved items 25A to 25D on its agenda: a Chapter 312 tax abatement, a Chapter 381 incentive agreement, a foreign trade zone agreement with a payment in lieu of taxes (PILOT), and a “true up” PILOT agreement. Interim County Judge Daniel Wong abstained because his engineering firm has worked with Tesla.

Each agreement covers a different slice of Tesla’s tax bill, and together they run for decades.

The Chapter 312 agreement abates 100% of county property taxes on Tesla’s equipment and other tangible personal property at the plant for 10 tax years. The clock starts in tax year 2029 by default, but Tesla can move it to the first year after production starts, as late as 2031 without needing the county’s approval.

Tesla’s JETI application splits the $10.1 billion into $1.52 billion of real property and $8.6 billion of personal property, mostly production equipment.

Land and buildings aren’t part of the deal. Anything that isn’t eligible for the abatement, including the land and buildings, stays “fully taxable at all times,” according to the agreement.

Inventory goes through the foreign trade zone deal instead. Tesla’s site would be part of Port Freeport’s Foreign-Trade Zone No. 149, which exempts eligible inventory from local property tax. In exchange for the county not objecting, Tesla agreed to make PILOT payments, set at “zero dollars or 0%” of the forgone taxes for as long as the abatement is in effect.

When the abatement ends, the Chapter 381 agreement takes over. Tesla starts paying county taxes on its equipment, and the county pays 90% of them back as an annual grant for 20 years. The county keeps 10% for its general fund.

Inventory follows the same pattern. After the abatement, Tesla’s FTZ payment rises to 10% of the taxes that would otherwise apply.

There’s one more lever. If Tesla averages at least 12,000 employees for any two consecutive years during the first decade, the first five years of the rebate period become a 100% grant, and the rebate period stretches from 20 to 25 years.

That pushes the agreement out to Project Year 35. It’s also 2,288 jobs above the 9,712 Tesla says it will create.

To keep the tax breaks, Tesla has to start construction by June 30, 2027, begin “regular commercial production of solar cells” by December 31, 2030, and invest $10 billion by the later of December 31, 2031 or two years after production starts. Force majeure and “ERCOT Delay” provisions can extend those dates.

The jobs ramp is 2,500, 5,000, 7,500, then 9,712 employees by the fourth year of operations, with an average compensation of $55 per hour. Tesla gets the full abatement as long as it hits 90% of the headcount target, which is about 8,741 people.

The penalties are mild. Missing a jobs or wage target for one year only costs Tesla that year’s break, and two misses in a row lets the county terminate the agreement. Full recapture of abated taxes, plus a possible 15% penalty if they aren’t repaid within 60 days, is reserved for “Material Defaults” like missing the $10 billion investment, blowing the construction deadlines or abandoning the plant for 12 months.

Under the Chapter 381 deal, grants from earlier years can’t be clawed back for a headcount shortfall.

The county’s floor is small. The true-up agreement only guarantees that Fort Bend collects at least $2 million a year, and 25% of the taxes Tesla pays on its real property, the county’s 10% share and the FTZ payments all count toward that total.

For scale, Tesla’s own consultant, Kroll, estimated in the JETI filing that the project would pay $276.5 million in Fort Bend County general taxes over 38 years without incentives, peaking at $16.2 million in 2030. That estimate lumps land, buildings and equipment together. The equipment portion is the one the county just agreed to give back, for most of those years.

In its application, it says the economics of the Fort Bend site are “not competitive” with an unnamed site in another state without the JETI limitation and local abatements. The competing state is redacted in a confidential supplement.

None of the documents state a production capacity. The only big number is a quote from Elon Musk at Davos in January, cited in the application: “The SpaceX and Tesla team, both separately, are working to build to 100 GW a year of solar power in the U.S., of manufactured solar power.”

For comparison, Qcells is spending $2.5 billion on its ingot-to-module campus in Cartersville, Georgia, where a 3.5 GW cell line started production this year. Crystal Sun’s budget is four times that.

Tesla has been here before. #5522. Rivian buys startup to turn 400,000 daily clips into 3D sims

Rivian wants to rebuild the roads its customers drive as 3D test worlds for its self-driving system, and it just bought a 15-person UK startup called Third Dimension to do it, as first reported by TechCrunch.

Third Dimension pitched itself as a generative AI tool that builds 3D environments ready for game engines. When it raised a $7 million seed round led by Felicis in October 2024, its target customers were game studios, film, the US military, and autonomous vehicle companies.

The founders’ résumés tell you which of those Rivian cared about. CEO Tolga Kart spent more than two years at Tesla working on Autopilot simulation, after five years as a senior director on Call of Duty at Activision, and later ran engineering at synthetic data company Parallel Domain. Cofounder Piotr Sokólski spent four years at Wayve building photorealistic neural simulators for self-driving cars.

The whole team joins Rivian’s autonomy and AI group under James Philbin. Most of them are in the UK, so Rivian also gets a bigger footprint in London.

“Simulation is central to how we train and validate our autonomous driving systems,” Philbin told TechCrunch in an exclusive interview. He added that Third Dimension “has built impressive technology that turns real-world data into detailed 3D environments.”

Rivian pulls driving footage from R1S, R1T, R2, and commercial van owners who opt into camera data sharing. Philbin told TechCrunch the fleet sends back more than 400,000 sequences every day.

But the vast majority of that footage is boring. The events that actually break self-driving systems, like a mattress in the fast lane, a cop waving traffic through a red light, or a kid running out between parked cars, are rare. You can wait months for your fleet to catch enough of them, or you can rebuild real scenes in 3D and change the variables yourself. That’s the pitch here.

Rivian isn’t first. Waymo unveiled its Waymo World Model, built on Google DeepMind’s Genie 3, back in February to generate scenarios its cars have never seen. Tesla has shown off its own neural “world simulator” for FSD as well.

Rivian needs this to work fast. Uber agreed in March to invest up to $1.25 billion in Rivian to deploy R2 robotaxis, but only $300 million comes up front. The rest is tied to autonomy milestones, starting with 10,000 driverless R2s on Uber’s network in San Francisco and Miami from 2028.

On the consumer side, the timeline has already slipped once. Rivian’s point-to-point driving feature goes to a small group of invited Autonomy+ owners later this year, and those testers have to sign an NDA. “Eyes-off” driving, which RJ Scaringe promised for 2026 back in January 2025, is now a 2027 target, with Level 4 planned for 2028 on the Gen 3 hardware Rivian showed at its AI & Autonomy Day.

It is not just about video clips, it is about what the vehicle sees in the clip and what it simultaneously sees on its Lidar and Radars. The fusion of that data is what needs to get fed into the simulation model.

We don’t have all the details, but this looks like a good deal. It’s small and targeted, and it buys a team that has already done this exact work at Tesla and Wayve. For a company with a fraction of Tesla’s fleet, getting more out of every clip it collects is the right bet, and Waymo going down the same road tells you the leaders agree.

But simulation is a training and testing tool. It isn’t proof. A system can ace millions of generated edge cases and still screw up on a real street, and the only way the public finds out how it’s actually doing is when the company shares real-world numbers.

However, this is the best way to do a “simulation” because it doesn’t create a fake world with simulated physics; it reconstructs a real-world scene based on real-world data. It’s still a simulation and not as good as the real thing, but it’s as close as it gets.
