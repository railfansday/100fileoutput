#620. France: Young voters blase about Glucksmann's Socialists

Raphael Glucksmann wants to lead France's Socialists to power without working with the left-populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, who is particularly popular with young voters. How can the center-left candidate win them over?

The historically working-class Parisian neighborhood of Belleville has gentrified in recent years. It was here that Raphael Glucksmann, a member of the European Parliament for France's center-left Socialists, celebrated his victory in the party's presidential primary on October 11.

Two rounds of voting had been anticipated, but the 46-year-old son of philosopher and essayist Andre Glucksmann triumphed in the first round with a convincing 66% of the vote and will be the Socialist candidate in France's 2027 presidential election.

As he left the Cantine de Belleville restaurant, Glucksmann was flour-bombed by an unidentified assailant. The following morning, he called on his rival, the left-wing French populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, to condemn attacks carried out by his supporters, though it is not clear that the attacker was indeed a supporter of Melenchon's La France Insoumise (LFI).

The incident has highlighted the issue set to dog Glucksmann's presidential candidacy over the coming weeks: How can he win over Melenchon's supporters if he refuses to work with their candidate?

"Where do you stand on Melenchon?" That was the question that dominated much of the Socialists' primary campaign. Party leader Olivier Faure spoke in favor of leaving the door open for cooperation with the LFI, but Glucksmann called for a clear break with the far left. His decisive victory is therefore also seen as a vote in favor of a clear demarcation.

The other primary candidates questioned Glucksmann's political experience. Segolene Royal, a previous presidential candidate, compared his bid for the Elysee with that of an "intern" wanting to take charge of a company. She said Glucksmann had no experience in government or even running a local authority.

Despite Glucksmann's victory in the primary, his starting position is a difficult one. A poll by Toluna Harris Interactive, published the day after his win, projects that he would win 10–11% of the vote. The same poll put Melenchon on 18%, meaning the far-left man would be much more likely to reach the runoff. But the survey was conducted before Glucksmann won the primary, and so does not take into account any potential boost the nomination may give him. Still, 10% would be an improvement on the Socialists' performance in the last presidential election in 2022, when their candidate Anne Hidalgo won just 1.75% of the national vote.

There is also the complication that the Socialists are banking on a man who is not even a party member to lead them from irrelevance. Raphael Glucksmann actually belongs to a small center-left party called Place Publique.

Glucksmann has his work cut out for him — especially with young people. In an Ipsos survey in September, which posited Glucksmann as the social-democratic candidate and Edouard Philippe as the only candidate from the political center, Melenchon scored 41% among the under-25s. Meanwhile, 20% of this age group supported the far-right nationalist Marine Le Pen. France's young electorate is politically divided, but Melenchon still dominates on the left.

The 75-year-old is currently rallying alongside the students whose protests have drawn international attention. At a rally near Clermont-Ferrand in central France, he expressed solidarity with their movement and reiterated his calls for lowering the voting age to 16 and abolishing Parcoursup, France's national admissions procedure for higher education.

In an analysis for the Fondation Jean-Jaures, a Socialist-aligned think tank, the political scientist Antoine Bristielle distinguishes between a core group of committed LFI voters and the broader left. Melenchon's most loyal supporters, he says, are often young, educated city-dwellers, and many are under financial pressure. Four in 10 households in this group live on a net monthly income of less than €2,000 ($2,200).

Bristielle estimates that just under a third of the left-wing electorate in his study care more about a left-wing victory than their allegiance to any particular party. Almost half of the people in this group are younger than 35 and would consider voting for the Socialists, the Greens or LFI. Redistribution and an active government are important to them, but most would prefer politicians who are willing to compromise.

The pattern recently observed in Germany, where young women are more likely to vote for the left and young men for the right, does not apply in France. Political scientists Anja Durovic, Nonna Mayer and Noemie Piolat found that in the 2024 European and parliamentary elections, young French women were not significantly more likely to vote for the left, or less likely to vote for the far right, than men of the same age. It remains to be seen whether this pattern will be replicated in the 2027 presidential election.

Glucksmann says he would ease the burden on earned income by reducing France's CSG social security tax while increasing taxes on inheritances worth more than €2 million. He also presents himself as a committed supporter of European integration, while Melenchon has called the EU a neoliberal institution. Glucksmann's political profile combines social policy demands with foreign policy positions that could also appeal to voters from the camp of the current president, Emmanuel Macron.

France's Ukraine policy is one such issue. Glucksmann favors decisive support for Kyiv. Manon Aubry, a member of the European Parliament for Melenchon's LFI, says her party supports military assistance only under certain conditions, namely: The provision of aid to Ukraine must first be subject to democratic debate; it must not enable attacks deep inside Russian territory; and it must not weaken France's own defense capabilities. In 2024, LFI MPs voted against the French–Ukrainian security agreement.

The tension between the parties of the left was once again apparent in the aftermath of the flour attack in Belleville. The LFI MP Sebastien Delogu posted a declaration of support on X for the taxi driver who was going to have to clean his car.

Glucksmann must win over a lot of voters from Melenchon's camp if he is to have a chance of winning the presidency. This is likely to prove a much more difficult task than simply distancing himself from Melenchon.

#617. One of Earth’s most powerful supervolcanoes is refilling with fresh magma

One of Earth's most powerful supervolcanoes is slowly refilling its enormous underground magma reservoir, thousands of years after producing one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recent geological history. Scientists at Kobe University have discovered evidence that fresh magma is accumulating beneath Japan's Kikai caldera, which unleashed a catastrophic eruption about 7,300 years ago.

The discovery offers a rare look at how giant volcanic systems rebuild their magma supplies after massive eruptions. It could also provide important clues about the behavior of other supervolcanoes, including Yellowstone in the United States and Toba in Indonesia, potentially helping scientists improve their ability to recognize warning signs of future eruptions.

How Supervolcanoes Store Enormous Amounts of Magma

Some volcanic eruptions are so immense that they dramatically reshape the landscape. The amount of magma released can be staggering, enough to bury all of New York City's Central Park beneath a layer roughly 12 kilometers (7.5 miles) thick.

When such enormous volumes of magma escape from underground, the rock above the emptied reservoir can collapse, leaving behind a broad, relatively shallow depression known as a caldera. These features are associated with some of the most powerful eruptions in Earth's history.

Famous examples include Yellowstone, Toba, and Japan's Kikai caldera, most of which lies beneath the ocean. Kikai last experienced a giant eruption approximately 7,300 years ago, producing the largest known volcanic eruption of the Holocene, the geological epoch that began roughly 11,700 years ago and continues today.

Although scientists know that giant caldera volcanoes can erupt more than once, they still have an incomplete understanding of how the enormous quantities of magma required for these events accumulate underground. This makes it extremely difficult to anticipate when another major eruption might occur.

"We must understand how such large quantities of magma can accumulate to understand how giant caldera eruptions occur," says Kobe University geophysicist Nobukazu Seama.

Scientists Discover a Massive Magma Reservoir Beneath Japan

Kikai's underwater location might seem like an obstacle to studying the volcano, but it actually gives researchers an important advantage. The surrounding seafloor allows scientists to carry out extensive geological surveys across the volcanic structure.

"The underwater location allows us to implement systematic, large-scale surveys," Seama explains.

To investigate what lies beneath Kikai, researchers from Kobe University collaborated with the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC). They used powerful airgun arrays to generate seismic pulses that traveled through the crust beneath the ocean floor.

Instruments called ocean bottom seismometers recorded how these waves moved through the underground rock. Because seismic waves travel differently through solid rock and regions containing molten material, scientists can analyze their behavior to investigate structures that cannot be observed directly.

The findings, published in Communications Earth & Environment, revealed a substantial region containing a high proportion of magma directly beneath the site of Kikai's massive eruption 7,300 years ago.

By examining the seismic measurements, the team was able to determine the approximate size and shape of the underground reservoir. Its position and extent suggest that it occupies the same magma storage system involved in the ancient eruption.

"Due to its extent and location it is clear that this is in fact the same magma reservoir as in the previous eruption," Seama says.

Fresh Magma Is Recharging the Ancient Supervolcano

Perhaps the most intriguing finding is that the magma currently beneath Kikai does not appear to be simply leftover material from its catastrophic eruption thousands of years ago.

Previous investigations revealed that a new lava dome has been developing near the center of the caldera over approximately the past 3,900 years. A lava dome forms when relatively thick, viscous lava accumulates around a volcanic vent rather than flowing freely across the surrounding landscape.

Chemical analyses also showed that volcanic material from this dome and other more recent activity differs in composition from the material released during Kikai's giant eruption.

That difference provides an important clue. It suggests that the magma now stored beneath the volcano was supplied by new injections of molten rock from deeper within Earth's crust or mantle, rather than remaining unchanged since the previous eruption.

"This means that the magma that is now present in the magma reservoir under the lava dome is likely newly injected magma," summarizes Seama.

Together, the seismic observations and chemical evidence help researchers develop a clearer picture of how a giant volcanic system can gradually rebuild its underground magma supply following a massive eruption.

This replenishment process does not mean that another catastrophic eruption is imminent. Instead, it reveals how a magma reservoir can be recharged over thousands of years, an important process that scientists need to understand before they can reliably assess the long-term behavior of supervolcanoes.

What the Discovery Could Reveal About Yellowstone

The findings could have implications far beyond Japan.

Researchers believe the process identified beneath Kikai may help explain why other enormous caldera systems, including Yellowstone and Toba, also contain large magma reservoirs relatively close to Earth's surface.

"This magma re-injection model is consistent with the existence of large shallow magma reservoirs beneath other giant calderas like Yellowstone and Toba," says Seama.

The proposed model suggests that, following a giant eruption, new magma can gradually enter and replenish an existing underground reservoir. Understanding how this happens could reveal important patterns in the long-term cycles of magma accumulation, volcanic activity, and major eruptions.

Researchers now hope to improve the seismic techniques used at Kikai so they can investigate the movement and storage of magma in greater detail.

"We want to refine the methods that have proved to be so useful in this study to more deeply understand the re-injection processes. Our ultimate goal is to become better able to monitor the crucial indicators of future giant eruptions."

By revealing how fresh magma accumulates beneath a volcano responsible for one of Earth's most extraordinary eruptions, the research brings scientists closer to understanding the processes that eventually make such colossal events possible.

#610. Instagram is testing 90-minute videos

Instagram is introducing longform video. Called Features, the new format will allow users to upload videos up to 90 minutes long.

"So many creators are producing longer videos, and they're using clips of those videos to drive discovery on Reels and help build their audience," said Instagram VP of Product Tessa Lyons, announcing Features in a video alongside Head of Instagram Adam Mosseri on Monday.

"Reels is still going to be a core part of Instagram. But when you want to go deeper with creators you love and the content they're creating, you should be able to do that on Instagram."

Longform video creators have typically favoured YouTube, which allows verified accounts to upload videos up to 12 hours long. In contrast, Instagram Reels are restricted to a maximum length of 20 minutes. Features aims to allow these creators to share their longer videos on Instagram as well, keeping their audience on the platform.

Features appears to focus on content shot in landscape, and will be available on desktop, the mobile app, and Instagram for TV. Lyons stated that part of the new format's purpose is to help populate Instagram's television app, which allows users to watch Reels and video content on their TV.

"We've been working on a TV version of Reels, and we know that to do that well, we need more than just short-form video," said Lyons.

"People might discover a long form video that they love by seeing a Reel that's a clip of the video when they're watching Reels on mobile, and then when they go at the end of the day and open their TV and open Instagram on TV, we'll know that they watched that Reel and we'll be able to say, 'Hey, do you want to finish watching…?'"

Though Instagram is introducing longform functionality, it doesn't yet offer creators the same financial incentives as YouTube. While Instagram creators are primarily paid through third-party brand partnerships and collaborations, the YouTube Partner Program gives YouTubers a cut of revenue from ads placed on their videos (55 percent for longform and 45 percent for Shorts).

At time of writing, the most liked comment on Mosseri and Lyons' post was from mental health content creator Divija Bhasin (@awkwardgoat3), who asked, "Why would anyone post an unpaid long video on Instagram if we can just post it on YouTube and actually get paid for the effort?"

In a statement to TechCrunch, an Instagram spokesperson said that it is exploring potential monetisation models, "including performance-based incentives." However, it isn't ready to announce any such plans, the spokesperson instead focusing on users potentially being able to find more brand partnerships by expanding their audience through Features.

"Over time, we think there will be compounding benefits for creators to tell new stories, reach new audiences, and find new partners, which should lead to many monetization opportunities," the Instagram spokesperson told TechCrunch.

Judging from Lyons and Mosseri's announcement video, Instagram Features is currently missing a lot of the functionality available on YouTube. While viewers can adjust the playback speed, features such as captions and chapters appear absent during this early testing stage. Of course, such functionality may be added later, with Instagram currently conducting small tests of Features in the U.S. and Brazil.

#608. This 150-million-year-old bird had dinosaur teeth and a strange secret

A remarkably preserved 150-million-year-old bird fossil discovered in China is challenging long-standing ideas about how birds evolved. Rather than developing increasingly sophisticated flight abilities, some of the earliest birds appear to have taken a very different evolutionary path, including losing the ability to fly altogether.

The newly identified species, Yuezhuornis yangyuanensis, lived during the Late Jurassic period (150-148 million years ago). Described in a study published in Nature, the crow-sized creature provides evidence that early bird evolution was much more complex than scientists once believed. Instead of evolving primarily to become better flyers, ancient birds were adapting to a variety of environments and ways of life, including those that favored living on the ground.

Researchers from the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology (IVPP) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) and the Fujian Institute of Geological Sciences uncovered the fossil in the recently recognized Zhenghe Fauna of Fujian Province in southeastern China. According to their findings, Yuezhuornis represents the earliest known example of a bird lineage losing its ability to fly.

An Ancient Bird With Dinosaur Teeth and Claws

One of the most remarkable features of Yuezhuornis is its unusual combination of bird and dinosaur characteristics. Although it possessed skeletal features associated with birds, it also retained anatomical traits that would have been familiar among small predatory dinosaurs.

Its jaws contained closely spaced, serrated teeth resembling those of deinonychosaurs, a group of meat-eating dinosaurs that included agile predators related to birds. Such teeth are not normally associated with early birds.

Even more striking was its second toe, which could bend backward unusually far and carried a curved, sickle-shaped claw. This specialized feature is strongly associated with deinonychosaurs, including dromaeosaurids and troodontids, two groups of predatory dinosaurs.

"When we examined Yuezhuornis, we were struck by its extraordinary mix of features," said Dr. Min Wang, corresponding author of the study. "It possesses bird-like pelvic and hindlimb structures yet retains the specialized predatory foot and teeth of a small meat-eating dinosaur. It shows us that early bird evolution was far more complicated than we previously realized."

Where Does This Strange Jurassic Bird Fit in Evolution?

To understand how Yuezhuornis was related to other prehistoric birds and dinosaurs, scientists carried out detailed analyses of its evolutionary relationships. They used two approaches, maximum parsimony and Bayesian tip-dating, to compare anatomical characteristics and reconstruct the species' position on the evolutionary tree.

Maximum parsimony identifies evolutionary relationships that require the fewest changes in characteristics, while Bayesian tip-dating also incorporates statistical estimates of evolutionary history and information about when fossils lived.

Both methods produced consistent results, placing Yuezhuornis within Avialae, the evolutionary group that includes modern birds and their closest extinct relatives.

"The results consistently placed Yuezhuornis firmly within Avialae (the group containing modern birds and their closest extinct relatives), just crownward of Archaeopteryx and as a sister taxon to Zhengheornis," said Dr. Zhonghe Zhou, a co-author of the study.

This placement is particularly important because Archaeopteryx, which also lived roughly 150 million years ago, is among the most famous early bird relatives. The findings indicate that Yuezhuornis belonged to an early branch of bird evolution rather than simply being a small predatory dinosaur with bird-like characteristics.

Evidence That Some Early Birds Lost the Ability to Fly

The researchers also examined how Yuezhuornis might have lived. Using measurements of its skeletal proportions and statistical comparisons with other animals, they investigated whether its anatomy was better suited for flying or moving along the ground.

The results showed that its limb proportions closely resembled those of modern ground-dwelling birds and certain other prehistoric dinosaurs closely related to birds. This similarity suggests that Yuezhuornis spent its life on the ground and was likely unable to fly.

The findings point to an unexpected development in the history of birds. Rather than simply progressing toward stronger wings and more efficient flight, some early bird lineages appear to have moved in the opposite direction, adapting to lifestyles in which flying was no longer necessary.

This challenges the traditional assumption that the earliest stages of bird evolution were driven primarily by the demands of flight. Instead, birds were already exploring different ecological roles and developing a surprisingly wide range of body forms.

A 150-Million-Year-Old Discovery Rewrites Bird Evolution

The discovery of Yuezhuornis suggests that the evolution of birds was never a simple, one-directional process. Even during the Late Jurassic, when the earliest bird lineages were emerging, some species were developing adaptations that favored life on land rather than in the air.

Its unusual combination of bird-like anatomy, dinosaur-style predatory features, and apparent flightlessness provides a revealing glimpse into that evolutionary experimentation. The fossil shows that early birds were more anatomically and ecologically diverse than previously recognized.

It also underscores the scientific importance of the Zhenghe Fauna, a fossil assemblage that is helping researchers investigate a poorly understood period in bird evolution. As additional discoveries emerge from this region of China, scientists may gain an even clearer picture of the surprising evolutionary paths taken by the earliest birds.

#599. These tiny snakes roll four times faster than they slither

Tiny snakes found in Southeast Asia have developed an extraordinary way to escape danger. Instead of relying on the familiar side-to-side motion of slithering, dwarf reedsnakes can rapidly roll across the ground, reaching speeds about four times faster than their usual movement.

Scientists have now uncovered how these small reptiles accomplish such an unusual feat. By studying video recordings captured from multiple angles, researchers discovered that the snakes coordinate their movements with remarkable precision. While the rear portions of their bodies rotate rapidly, their heads remain relatively steady, facing forward and staying at a nearly constant height.

[See link to video after the end of the article.]

The findings reveal how an animal can achieve an unexpected form of movement through specialized behavior and body control, even without the physical structures normally needed for rolling.

Rolling might seem like an effective way to travel quickly, but it presents a major challenge for living creatures.

A wheel can spin continuously around a fixed axle, allowing it to rotate without disturbing whatever it supports. Animals, however, have no comparable mechanism separating their rotating bodies from their heads.

If an animal's entire body spins together, its head and sensory organs must also rotate. This can make it difficult to maintain balance, adjust body position, and steer in the intended direction. As rolling speed increases, these problems become even harder to manage.

Yet dwarf reedsnakes belonging to the genus Pseudorabdion have found a way around these limitations.

These small Southeast Asian snakes are among the few animals known to deliberately roll as a means of escaping potential predators. Their unusual behavior has appeared in field guides and was previously documented during a field observation, but scientists had not fully understood the movements that made it possible.

Tiny Snakes Roll Four Times Faster Than They Slither

To investigate this remarkable escape strategy, researchers filmed dwarf reedsnakes from several angles and carefully examined how different parts of their bodies moved during rolling.

The results revealed impressive speeds. While rolling, the snakes reached peak forward velocities ranging from 1.72 to 3.69 meters per second, approximately four times their maximum speed when moving through lateral undulation, the familiar side-to-side motion used by most snakes.

Despite traveling so quickly, the snakes were able to follow a narrow racetrack measuring just 20 centimeters across, demonstrating that their rolling movements are not simply uncontrolled tumbling.

Closer examination of the recordings revealed a surprisingly intricate sequence of movements.

Rather than spinning their entire bodies together, the snakes alternate between rolling on one side and swinging a curved section of their bodies downward beside their heads. The researchers called this downward sweeping movement a "stroke."

During each successive cycle, the side touching the ground switches, and the stroke changes direction from left to right or right to left. By repeating this carefully timed pattern, the snakes generate continuous forward rolling motion.

The Remarkable Secret Behind Their High-Speed Escape

One of the most surprising discoveries involved what happens to the snakes' heads during these rapid movements.

Although the rear portions of their bodies undergo substantial rotation, the snakes keep their heads pointed in the direction they are traveling. Rather than spinning along with the rest of the body, the head remains comparatively stable.

The researchers also found that the snakes maintain a relatively consistent head height above the ground. This stabilization becomes especially noticeable at higher speeds, when the snakes tend to hold their heads elevated and minimize upward and downward movements.

Keeping the head steady while the rest of the body rotates may help the animals maintain control and navigate effectively during their rapid escapes.

This ability is particularly remarkable because the snakes accomplish it without a mechanical separation between the rotating and stabilized portions of their bodies.

What Rolling Snakes Reveal About Evolution

The discovery demonstrates how sophisticated coordination can allow animals to overcome physical limitations that might otherwise make certain types of movement impractical.

Although dwarf reedsnakes lack anything resembling a wheel's axle, they effectively divide their bodies into regions with different functions. Much of the body performs the powerful rotational movements needed for propulsion, while the head remains stabilized to support controlled travel.

This unusual strategy allows the snakes to combine speed, directional control, and rapid body rotation in a way that would be difficult to predict from their anatomy alone.

More broadly, the findings highlight an important principle of animal evolution: physical structure does not necessarily determine the full range of movements an animal can perform. Innovative behaviors and precise muscular coordination can open up entirely new possibilities, allowing creatures to accomplish feats that their body shapes might initially suggest are impossible.

#598. What Earth's sixth mass extinction event means for humans

Human activity is driving plants and animals to extinction at an unprecedented rate. As countries meet in Armenia to discuss ways to reverse nature loss, DW reveals how declining biodiversity is already harming people.

From the tiniest microbes in the human gut to giant whales in the ocean, biodiversity is the sum of all life on the planet. It's another word for nature. And because nature is like a team, every player has a job.

When various species from trees to animals work together, they regulate weather, maintain soil health, purify water and support food production. These benefits are known as ecosystem services and they underpin human well-being.

"It is really an abstract concept, but we experience it absolutely every day," said Rebecca Shaw, chief scientist at conservation group WWF. "And we almost never think about it when nature is functioning."

But as biodiversity declines, Shaw and other scientists are calling on the world to really think about it. Because biodiversity is struggling as humans push ecosystems to the brink through intensive farming, burning fossil fuels and pollution from sources like plastic and chemicals.

Over the past 52 years, monitored vertebrate populations have collapsed by an average of 73%, while freshwater ecosystems are faring worse with a drop of 85%, according to WWF's recent Living Planet report.

It tallies with other studies that show a million animal and plant species face extinction unless humanity reduces its pressure on nature.

"It is a really steep decline," said Shaw. "What it's telling us is that nature is in trouble, and it's an early warning indicator that the fabric of life that we depend on for freshwater and food and for clean air is unraveling."

Some 200 countries are gathering in Yerevan, Armenia over the next two weeks for COP17, the latest UN biodiversity summit, to advance commitments to reverse nature's unraveling by 2030. But progress toward those targets remains insufficient, with funding and implementation among the biggest challenges.

Take wetlands as an example. Some 85% of these vital ecosystems disappeared between 1700 and 2000. One study from Canada's University of Waterloo found that habitats like marshes and swamps in southern Ontario provide C$4.2 billion (€2.63 billion) worth of water filtration services each year, removing nutrients like phosphorus and keeping drinking water sources clean.

The study found that as they disappear, water quality worsens, which massively increases the cost of treating it.

Another example is the decline in pollinators like bees and bats. More than 75% of global food crop types rely in part on animal pollination.

"This is why we need to care about global biodiversity."

Nature also provides the basis for many modern human medicines, with some 50% coming from natural compounds.

Penicillin, the first antibiotic, is produced by a mold and is estimated to have saved over 500 million lives. Before its discovery, a person could die from a simple cut. Around 70% of cancer drugs come from natural products or synthetic compounds "inspired by nature," according to the World Health Organization.

As biodiversity disappears, humans risk losing access to undiscovered treatments.

To return to the "nature as a team" metaphor, if a player disappears, sometimes a species with similar functions in the ecosystem can jump in as a substitute.

But not always, particularly if it's a "keystone species." They play a disproportionately large role in their environments, and their disappearance has significant knock-on effects.

The oyster is one such species. Wild oyster populations are facing massive declines, while 85% of their reefs have been lost worldwide. An adult oyster filters up to 50 gallons of water a day.

Extinction is a natural process. But the planet has experienced five mass extinctions, when a large proportion of species globally disappeared over a short geological period. The rate at which species are disappearing today suggests Earth is experiencing a sixth event driven largely by human activity, according to scientists.

Astronomers have spotted a remarkable gamma-ray burst (GRB) that's a real Incredible Hulk compared to other cosmic explosions of the same type.

What makes this GRB so special is the fact that the central engine powering it remained active for around 27 days, flaring in X-rays following the initial blast. That's 20 days longer than the longest previous GRB on record.

This record-breaking burst — which is designated GRB 220706A and was spotted on July 6, 2022 — is even more exciting because scientists don't know what the central engine powering it actually is.

"In a nutshell, GRB 220706A is an extremely late X-ray flare. X-ray flaring is common following GRBs, but previously it has always come within the first few days after we detect the burst," study team member Benjamin Gompertz, of the University of Birmingham in England, told Space.com.

"The fact that we see flaring at around 27 days in the GRB’s rest frame tells us that there’s an ongoing active process, and that’s unprecedented in the known population," Gompertz added. "The previous record for delayed flaring was about six days after the GRB."

GRBs are some of the most powerful explosions in the cosmos. They have been linked to the collapse of a massive star, which also triggers a supernova and creates a black hole. This results in a long-duration GRB, while short-duration GRBs are thought to be triggered when two stellar remnants called neutron stars collide.

GRB 220706A is an example of an ultra-long GRB, a rare cosmic blast of gamma rays in which the engine powering the GRB stays active for longer. They are incredibly rare, with their cause still unknown.

"We don’t know what the central engine driving this outburst is, and that’s what makes this such a compelling event," Gompertz said. "The fact that we see a supernova tells us that a star has collapsed, which is the standard power source for GRBs, but it doesn’t immediately account for the extremely late flaring or unusually bright supernova. We can interpret that through previous events to some degree."

Gompertz added that another ultra-long GRB detected in 2011, GRB 111209A, was associated with an unusually bright supernova (SN2011k) that had clear evidence for a central engine. The team behind that research determined that GRB 111209A is powered by a magnetar, a neutron star with a particular powerful magnetic field. In this case, the magnetic field is rotating at around 1,000 times per second.

"Our supernova looks a lot like that one, so a magnetar might be present here. That ties in nicely with super-luminous supernovas, which are also thought to be magnetar-driven," Gompertz said. "Another possibility could be that the GRB jet and the supernova are colliding with shells of dense material that were ejected towards the end of the parent star’s life, as it became unstable and began ejecting its outer layers. This sort of event is also seen in the super-luminous supernova population."

The team isn't quite sure how the central engine of this ultra-long GRB differs from that of others and why that resulted in 27 days of flaring.

"One possibility is that it’s a much bigger star than we usually see producing GRBs," Gompertz said. "A bigger star would take longer to fall in on itself and so provide a longer-lived source of energy to power the jet."

GRB sources may be members of a stellar population of massive, compact stars called Wolf-Rayet stars. These are 10 times wider that the sun, with around 10 times the mass of our star. However, even the collapse of such a star can't explain GRB 220706A.

"To power GRB 220706A, we estimated we need a star more like 1,000 times the radius of the sun, which would require a population of stars known as blue supergiants," Gompertz said. "On the other hand, it may be the remnant that’s different. If this star produced a magnetar, it may be that 'normal' GRBs just collapse to black holes."

The team thinks that the big question with GRBs like this one is whether they are members of the normal GRB population or a different kind of event. Having this second event, in addition to GRB 111209A, with an unusually bright supernova potentially adds weight to the idea that these are different stars. But the problem is not yet solved.

"The incredibly late flares also strain our models for how jets are powered," Gompertz said. "It will be fascinating to see how future theoretical work can incorporate a power source from a collapsing star that can last for a month!"

There could be even longer-lasting GRBs out in the cosmos that we have thus far failed to detect. Gompertz explained why such detections are tricky.

"GRBs this long are hard to detect because most of the satellites we use to find them are in low Earth orbit, which means the target is periodically blocked every 90 minutes or so," he explained. "That's fine for your typical 10- to 100-second-long GRB but becomes a nuisance when tracking much longer events. Long-lasting GRBs also seem to be more active in the X-rays rather than the gamma rays."

The University of Birmingham researcher added that newly launched missions like the Einstein Probe, which is more sensitive in X-rays than gamma rays, may be the perfect instruments to find more ultra-long GRBs.

"With any luck, we’ll get a few more of these to chase so that we can start trying to understand the incredible power sources that drive them," Gompertz concluded.

#589. Astronomers uncover a mysterious magnetic web above the Sun

Scientists have uncovered a surprisingly complex network of magnetic structures hidden above regions of the Sun that appear relatively calm. Using the Sunrise-III solar observatory, which traveled high into Earth's stratosphere aboard a balloon in 2024, an international research team led by the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan (NAOJ) discovered intricate, thread-like magnetic features that had previously been difficult to observe.

The findings reveal that even the Sun's seemingly quiet regions contain elaborate magnetic structures. They also offer new clues to how energy travels upward through the solar atmosphere and contributes to its heating.

A Hidden Web of Magnetic Threads Above the Sun

Sunrise-III detected numerous thin, elongated magnetic structures woven into a larger network of magnetic fields above a quiet region of the Sun. This network, known as the magnetic canopy, extends through the chromosphere, a layer of the solar atmosphere located above the Sun's visible surface.

The canopy develops as magnetic fields concentrated at the solar surface (photosphere) spread outward and arch upward into the chromosphere (chromosphere). Rather than forming a relatively smooth, uniformly expanding structure, however, the new observations reveal that the canopy contains many narrow, thread-like features.

To investigate how these structures form, researchers used computer simulations that successfully reproduced the patterns recorded by Sunrise-III. The results suggest that movements on the solar surface twist magnetic field lines, creating the intricate structures observed higher in the atmosphere.

The observatory also examined another quiet region near the solar limb (visible edge of the Sun). There, it measured magnetic fields within spicules, narrow jets of solar material that extend upward from the surface. These observations revealed how the distribution of magnetic fields changes with increasing height above the Sun's visible edge.

Why the Sun's Quiet Regions Are Difficult to Study

Although the Sun is constantly active, much of its surface lacks prominent features such as sunspots. Scientists describe these comparatively inactive areas as "quiet." They cover most of the Sun, making them especially important for understanding how our star behaves.

However, quiet does not mean magnetically inactive. These regions contain magnetic fields that are generally weaker than those found in more active areas of the Sun. Their subtle structures are difficult to measure, especially with telescopes on the ground, where turbulence in Earth's atmosphere can blur fine details.

Sunrise-III was designed to overcome this obstacle by carrying a powerful solar telescope far above most of the atmosphere.

A Balloon-Borne Telescope Reveals New Solar Details

Sunrise-III is an international research project built around a 1-meter telescope carried by a high-altitude balloon. During its 2024 mission, the observatory floated approximately 35 kilometers (about 22 miles) above Earth in the stratosphere, where atmospheric interference is greatly reduced.

This elevated vantage point allowed the telescope to capture exceptionally detailed measurements of the Sun's magnetic fields.

The balloon's gondola carried three scientific instruments developed through contributions from different countries. One of these was SCIP (Sunrise Chromospheric Infrared spectroPolarimeter), a near-infrared instrument developed under NAOJ's leadership.

SCIP is designed to measure magnetic fields continuously across different layers of the solar atmosphere, from the photosphere to the chromosphere. This capability allowed researchers to examine how magnetic structures extend and change with altitude rather than studying only a single layer.

New Clues to How the Sun's Atmosphere Is Heated

By observing from the stratosphere, Sunrise-III provided researchers with an unusually detailed picture of magnetic fields above the Sun's quiet regions. Its measurements revealed that the chromosphere contains far more intricate magnetic structures than a simple expanding canopy would suggest.

Understanding these structures could help scientists investigate how energy generated by motions near the solar surface is transferred into higher layers of the atmosphere.

The discovery offers an important step toward explaining how magnetic activity contributes to heating the Sun's atmosphere, including in regions that appear calm when viewed from afar.

#579. Scientists propose a way to detect dangerous “mirror life” before it exists

Scientists have proposed what they believe is the first way to detect "mirror life," a hypothetical form of life built from molecules that are reversed versions of those found in living organisms on Earth.

Researchers from the University of Strathclyde and Durham University have developed a proposed detection technique that uses molecules capable of emitting light with distinct left and right-handed properties. By analyzing these light signals, scientists may eventually be able to distinguish conventional organisms from their mirror-image counterparts.

Although mirror life does not currently exist, some experts consider its potential creation a serious biological safety concern. These artificial organisms could behave in unpredictable ways when interacting with Earth's ecosystems and might evade certain natural defenses that protect people, animals, and plants.

The likelihood of mirror life emerging is considered low, but its possible consequences could be severe enough to justify developing detection methods in advance.

Why Mirror Life Could Pose a Biological Threat

Concerns about mirror life have prompted scientists to recommend restrictions on research intended to produce organisms capable of reproducing using mirror-image biological molecules.

The potential dangers have also attracted attention from governments and international organizations, including the UK Government Office for Science and the United Nations Secretary General's Scientific Advisory Board.

The underlying science involves a property of molecules known as chirality, often described as molecular "handedness." Just as a person's left and right hands are mirror images that cannot be perfectly aligned, some molecules can exist in two forms that have identical chemical components but different three-dimensional arrangements.

These subtle structural differences can have major biological consequences. Two mirror-image versions of a molecule contain the same atoms connected in the same order, yet the human body may respond to them very differently. For example, one form of a medication could successfully treat a disease, while its mirror-image counterpart might be ineffective or even harmful.

Scientists are concerned that organisms assembled from reversed biological building blocks could interact with existing life in unexpected ways.

Importantly, the researchers emphasize that mirror life remains entirely hypothetical. Their proposed detection method is intended as a precaution, not a response to an existing biological threat.

The proposed approach relies on a phenomenon called circularly polarized luminescence (CPL), which can reveal the molecular handedness, or chirality, of certain substances and biological structures.

Light can exhibit different polarization patterns, including forms that rotate in opposite directions. By studying these patterns, researchers can obtain information about the three-dimensional arrangement of molecules.

The underlying concept has a surprisingly familiar application: the polarized light technology used in modern 3D movie glasses to create the illusion of depth.

The research, published in Royal Society Open Science under the title 'Circularly Polarized Luminescence (CPL) as a Contingency Assay for "Mirror Life" Bio-Hazards', was led by Dr. Lewis MacKenzie of the University of Strathclyde's Department of Pure and Applied Chemistry, working with Professor Robert Pal of Durham University.

Dr. MacKenzie said: "We don't expect mirror life to appear tomorrow, but if it did arise, the consequences could be severe, and it makes sense to develop detection methods before they are needed. Professor Pal and I had been working on CPL technologies before recent concerns became prominent, and guarding against mirror life was a clear fit for our research."

A Molecular Fingerprint for Detecting Mirror Life

To identify a possible mirror organism, the researchers suggest using specialized fluorescent probes that attach to particular structures inside cells.

Once bound to their targets, these probes would generate characteristic CPL signals. Scientists could analyze the resulting patterns to determine whether a biological sample contains the molecular handedness associated with ordinary terrestrial life or its reversed equivalent.

In effect, the light signals could serve as a molecular fingerprint, revealing differences that might otherwise be difficult to identify.

However, relying on a single fluorescent probe could produce misleading results. To improve reliability and reduce the risk of false readings, the team proposes combining several probes designed to recognize different cellular structures.

By examining multiple signals together, researchers hope to develop a more dependable way to distinguish conventional organisms from hypothetical mirror life.

Testing the Technology Without Creating Mirror Organisms

One important advantage of the proposed technique is that scientists could begin investigating and refining it using ordinary biological samples. There would be no need to manufacture mirror organisms simply to explore whether the underlying detection principles work.

The researchers also believe the method could eventually be incorporated into several types of scientific equipment, potentially allowing mirror-life screening in different environments.

Professor Pal has already demonstrated laboratory-scale CPL detection technologies involving microscopes, cameras, and biological cell plate readers, which are instruments commonly used to examine large numbers of cell samples.

However, the complete detection method specifically designed to identify mirror life has not yet been experimentally demonstrated. Further research will be necessary to determine whether the proposed approach can reliably distinguish between different forms of molecular handedness in biological samples.

Professor Pal added: "CPL detection relies on the same fundamental concept that allows modern 3D cinema glasses to create a stereoscopic image.

"This CPL detection approach can be adapted to many types of systems for analysis of cells, including biological plate readers, microscopes, and cameras. This means our mirror life assay approach could be generalized to many environments depending on end user needs."

#578. Webb spotted a subtle difference in the interstellar comets ice

Astronomers caught something unusual about the ice of a comet that came from another star system.

A Southwest Research Institute team pointed NASA's James Webb Space Telescope at interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS three times between Aug. 2025 and April 2026, catching it before and after its closest pass by the sun.

The space telescope spreads a comet's light into a rainbow, including infrared light that human eyes can't see. Water ice soaks up light at a few specific spots in the rainbow. That pattern makes it possible to detect water ice the way a fingerprint can identify a person.

Ice normally leaves one deep mark and two faint ones, and most comets around our sun show all three. But in 3I/ATLAS, the two faint marks barely registered, if they showed up at all. Researchers ran computer simulations and found that light scatters off comet grains in a similar way if tiny bits of ice are blended with more dust. Because the dust blocks light before it penetrates far through the ice, the lighter two marks tend to vanish.

"At first glance, 3I/ATLAS looks different from the icy comets we know," said Silvia Protopapa, lead author of a new paper in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, in a statement. "But when we placed it alongside distant icy worlds in our own solar system, it no longer looked out of place."

While the alien comet's dusty ice looked strange compared to the form usually seen in solar system comets, the team noticed that its makeup actually lines up with ice on some frozen worlds far past Neptune. That suggests ice may form in similar ways around different stars, as tiny grains that range from nearly pure to heavily dusty. If so, the raw materials for building planets may look much alike from one star to the next.

Comets like this one start out in planet-forming disks — the flat, spinning clouds of gas and dust that surround young stars. As worlds assemble, gravity can hurl leftover icy chunks clear out of a system. That may explain how some interstellar objects have lost their way from home.

#568. Scientists discover a hidden flaw that challenges 40 years of superconductor science

Scientists at the University of Warwick have uncovered a surprising secret inside a superconductor that researchers have studied for 40 years. Long believed to have a uniform crystal structure, the material actually contains a complex patchwork of different atomic arrangements. Using an advanced 3D imaging technique, the team was able to see deep inside the crystal for the first time, revealing hidden features that challenge decades of scientific assumptions.

High-temperature superconductivity ranks among the most important scientific breakthroughs of the past four decades. Superconductors can carry electrical current without any resistance, eliminating the energy losses that normally occur as electricity travels through conventional materials. They also have the potential to support extraordinarily powerful magnets, making them promising for future power grids, medical scanners, and quantum computers.

The new findings, published in Physical Review Letters, come from researchers in Warwick's Department of Chemistry working with the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in France. Their investigation focused on a class of copper-based superconductors known as cuprates, which can conduct electricity without resistance at unusually high (though still very cold) temperatures.

Advanced 3D Imaging Reveals a Hidden Crystal Structure

For four decades, scientists have generally built their theories of cuprate superconductivity around the assumption that these materials have the same crystal structure throughout their interiors. The new research shows that this fundamental assumption does not always hold.

To investigate, the team used scanning 3D X-ray diffraction, a sophisticated imaging method that reveals how atoms are arranged deep within a crystal. The approach is similar in principle to a medical CT scan, except that it maps atomic structures rather than tissues and organs.

This allowed the researchers to construct a detailed three-dimensional picture of the superconductor's interior, exposing structural variations that conventional techniques had not revealed.

Instead of finding a uniform crystal, they discovered a patchwork of regions containing two slightly different atomic arrangements. Even more surprising were the boundaries separating these regions, which were hundreds of times wider than expected.

These unusually broad transition zones were not simply narrow dividing lines between neighboring structures. They were extensive enough to behave almost like distinct structures themselves, potentially influencing how electricity moves through the material.

A 40-Year Assumption About Superconductors Overturned

Professor Mark Senn, who led the study at the University of Warwick's Department of Chemistry, explained why the findings could have major implications for superconductivity research.

"For forty years, the working assumption has been that these materials are the same all the way through, and nearly all the theory is built on that picture. We've shown it doesn't hold. The crystal is fundamentally patchworked and textured, with unusually wide boundary regions that likely work against superconductivity rather than just sitting alongside it.

"This might explain why some materials perform better than others and means some existing bulk measurements will need to be reinterpreted and future theoretical models built that incorporate this structural complexity."

The discovery suggests that the internal boundaries may actively interfere with superconductivity rather than simply coexist with it. If that is the case, the hidden structural differences could help explain why certain superconducting materials perform better than others.

It also means that some previous experiments, particularly those measuring the overall properties of these materials, may need to be reconsidered. Future theories will likely need to account for the complex structures inside real crystals rather than treating them as perfectly uniform.

What the Discovery Means for Future Superconductors

The researchers suspect that similar hidden structural patterns may be widespread among other cuprate superconductors. They could also occur in related materials being investigated for their ability to become superconducting under extreme pressure.

Beyond challenging existing theories, the study demonstrates the power of a new generation of imaging technology.

The advanced measurements were made possible by a recent 150 million euro upgrade to the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, which provides extremely intense X-ray beams for studying materials in extraordinary detail.

This technology now allows scientists to examine the three-dimensional interiors of many different materials with a level of precision that was previously unavailable. By revealing how hidden atomic structures affect a material's behavior, the technique could provide valuable insights across materials science.

For superconductivity research, the implications are particularly important. Understanding how internal crystal structures influence electrical performance could eventually help scientists identify ways to improve superconducting materials, bringing more advanced energy systems, powerful magnets, medical imaging devices, and quantum technologies closer to reality.

The telescope launched to Earth orbit in November 2004, tasked with studying gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) — the most powerful explosions in the cosmos. Swift was designed to operate for two years, but it continued studying the high-energy universe for more than two decades.

"In that time, Swift played a major role in advancing our understanding of the changing universe and was part of a revolution in astrophysics that showed how critical rapid-response observations are to the entire community," Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director of the Astrophysics Division at NASA Headquarters in Washington, said in the statement.

NASA had hoped to keep Swift going even longer. In September 2025, the agency awarded a contract to the Arizona company Katalyst Space Technologies to rescue the observatory, which was falling back to Earth thanks to atmospheric drag.

Swift has no thrusters capable of counteracting this drag. So Katalyst built a refrigerator-sized spacecraft called LINK, which aimed to rendezvous with Swift, grapple it with three robotic arms and boost it to a higher orbit, allowing the observatory to continue its science work.

NASA and Katalyst stressed from the start that success was far from guaranteed. After all, LINK's mission was unprecedented: No private spacecraft had ever captured and boosted a satellite that wasn't designed to be serviced in this way. And the rate of Swift's orbital decay demanded a rapid timeline: LINK launched on July 3, a little over nine months after Katalyst won the $30 million NASA Swift-rescue contract.

LINK was not able to execute its very challenging mission. About three weeks after liftoff, it suffered problems with its propulsion system and orientation-maintaining reaction wheels, which caused it to start spinning at an unexpected rate. These issues prevented LINK from capturing and boosting Swift, but the private craft did manage to get within 7.5 to 9.3 miles (12 to 15 kilometers) of the observatory and photograph it.

"From the beginning, this was a high-risk, high-reward mission," Domagal-Goldman said in a statement on Sept. 25, the day that LINK fell back to Earth.

"And while we'll be sad to see Swift's mission come to a close, we knew this boost effort would be worthwhile either way — advancing U.S. spacecraft servicing technology, challenging us to meet unprecedented mission timelines, and testing how we operate satellites to extend their time in low Earth orbit," he added.

Swift — which was officially renamed the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory in 2018, after its first principal investigator, who died a year earlier — gathered its last scientific data on Oct. 20, according to NASA's Friday update.

During its long and storied career, the space telescope observed more than 2,000 GRBs. And it did other work, too, studying more than 1,400 supernova explosions, as well as eyeing asteroids, comets and black holes and conducting an all-sky survey in X-ray light. The mission's data has contributed to about 7,000 published scientific papers to date, according to NASA officials.

And that number is sure to keep rising far into the future.

"Swift leaves us with an extraordinary archive of multiwavelength observations that will fuel new discoveries for decades more," Domagal-Goldman said in the Oct. 23 statement.

The immune system is central to human health. When it fails, people can become more susceptible to infection and cancer. When misdirected, it can attack the body’s own tissues. And as it ages, it can contribute to chronic disease.

People of the same age can have strikingly distinct immune systems, shaped by genetics and by environmental and lifestyle factors, including air pollution, smoking, diet and past infections1,2. Growing evidence suggests that variation in the immune system’s ‘biological age’ — the rate at which it has aged, which is distinct from chronological age — matters for health. This variation has been linked to neurodegeneration, cancer and mortality risk3.

Immune cells have a surprising role in exercise endurance

Immune cells have a surprising role in exercise endurance

Yet, clinicians have no routine way of measuring how healthy someone’s immune system is. That’s because the immune system is a dynamic network that can change over time. It involves specialized organs that support immune-cell development and function, as well as immune cells that can circulate throughout the body and reside in tissues4. Signalling molecules, such as cytokines and chemokines, allow these immune cells and tissues to communicate.

Clinical tests provide only a fragmented picture of the immune system. A physician might examine an individual’s white blood cell count to establish whether circulating immune cells are at unusually high or low levels. They might also measure blood biomarkers such as C-reactive protein, which can indicate inflammation. But neither measurement is specific to a particular health condition, and they do not indicate whether the immune system as a whole is resilient or in decline, or if it is capable of mounting an effective response to infections and other challenges.

But advances in immunology, molecular profiling, medical imaging and artificial intelligence are opening up fresh possibilities for measuring the state of people’s immune systems. The stage is set for researchers to develop the immunome: a comprehensive characterization of immune health across cells, tissues, organs and molecular interactions (see ‘Defining the immunome’).

A raft of assays can provide information about the state of a person’s immune system.

Large-scale analysis of circulating proteins and antibodies

Patterns of immune signalling and antibody recognition beyond individual clinical markers

Types, abundance and molecular states of immune cells

Diversity and composition of immune-cell populations

Detection of quantifiable features from medical images of immune-related organs

Structural and compositional features of the thymus, spleen and bone marrow

Locations of immune cells and how they are organized relative to other cells

Measurements of immune-system activity before and after immune challenges

Strength, timing and persistence of responses to challenges such as vaccination

Long-term modelling of immune health using artificial intelligence

Several types of data can be combined to predict how ageing and disease change a person’s immune health

Research initiatives such as the Human Immunome Project, which one of us (J.S.T.) co-leads, are beginning to provide the data needed. What remains unclear is whether this information could be used to improve patient care, and how that could be achieved. Which measurements best capture clinically important aspects of a person’s immune health? How stable are these variables over time? And, most importantly, can assessing them improve clinical decision-making and, ultimately, disease outcomes?

Measuring every aspect of the immune system will not be feasible for every person. Instead, researchers should try to identify key features that can be readily assessed in the clinic to flag aspects of the immune system that are weakened, or declining quickly when they shouldn’t be. Such assessments could open avenues for disease prevention, treatment guidance and methods to monitor interventions.

For instance, these features might enable physicians to recognize cases of accelerated immune ageing and spot individuals who need close disease monitoring. For cancer treatment, measures of immune health could help to identify people who are most likely to benefit from therapies that rely on the immune system5,6, as well as those with a high risk of adverse reactions.

Such measurements could also help researchers to understand why people of similar ages and apparently similar health sometimes have substantially different responses to the same infection — a phenomenon that was apparent during the COVID-19 pandemic. Antibody responses to the same vaccine likewise vary widely between individuals2,7. If this variability could be measured, clinicians could use it to tailor preventive measures, treatments and monitoring to an individual’s immune system.

Researchers, health-care systems and funders must work to find the smallest, most scalable set of measurements that can capture meaningful differences in immune function and guide clinical decisions. Here, we outline a road map for moving the immunome from the laboratory to the clinic.

First, scientists should generate longitudinal data sets using large research populations and biobanks. Gaining a full understanding of immune health will require measuring several components of the immune system alongside broader indicators of health and disease in the same people over time.

Analysis of genes and gene expression in single cells from large research populations and biobanks could help researchers to understand which immune-cell groups are present in distinct populations. This could reveal which genes and signalling pathways are active in particular populations of cells, and what combination of markers best distinguishes between healthy, declining and dysfunctional immune states2,3,8. Analysing blood proteins can indicate whether a person is experiencing inflammation and whether their immune system has been activated. And studying the receptors on immune cells called lymphocytes can reveal the diversity of cells available to recognize threats. These receptors determine which pathogenic antigens the cells will identify; greater diversity generally allows the immune system to detect a broader range of threats.

Researchers must also study how the immune system changes over time to respond to challenges. Studying people before and after they are vaccinated is one standardized way to do this. It’s already clear that a person’s immune state before vaccination can predict the strength and characteristics of the immune response that follows2,9. The task now is to find out whether a person’s response to routine vaccination could provide a practical read-out of their immune competence10, and whether it would be possible to modify the immune health of an individual before interventions such as vaccination or cancer immunotherapy to improve their response2,5.

Data sets used to probe the immunome must represent the diversity of human immunity, because genetics and environmental factors alter immune states1. For example, malaria vaccine efficacy can differ markedly between populations across continents7,11. Yet, many existing immune-profiling data sets are concentrated in populations from high-income nations. When studying diverse populations, researchers around the world should use the same protocols so that results from various regions can be compared.

A big challenge will be making sense of complex data sets. AI tools can identify patterns across thousands of immune measurements, but those patterns might reflect differences between populations, laboratories or technologies rather than underlying immune health. Models will therefore need to be tested across independent populations and over time to establish which signals are robust and clinically meaningful.

Scientists working on the Human Immunome Project are developing and applying AI approaches to immunome data collected from various populations. An important goal for the project is to create reference maps and foundation models that scientists can use for tasks such as identifying small, manageable sets of measurements that would be useful in clinical settings.

With large-scale data in hand, researchers should work towards simplification. The success of the field should be judged not by how many immune variables can be measured, but by whether it is possible to improve clinical decision-making with just a small, manageable set of them. Tests used in the clinic would ideally be cheap and should not put extra demands on patients. Before any AI tools can be used routinely, they will need to be tested across a range of hospitals and populations. Researchers must establish whether a tool’s outputs truly improve treatment decisions.

One example of a tool that might be useful in the clinic is an AI model capable of extracting information about immune health from routine blood tests and clinical information such as age, sex, body weight and medical history. In a study published last year6, such an approach was able to predict health outcomes after immunotherapy treatment for people with cancer.

#561. What pet cemeteries reveal about being human

Editor’s Note:  Eric Tourigny is a lecturer in historical archaeology at Newcastle University, UK. His research interprets osteological and material culture remains alongside historic texts to examine changing human-animal relationships in Europe and North America over the past 500 years. The views expressed in this commentary are his own. Read more opinion on CNN.

Why do we bury our departed loved ones in a cemetery? A primary purpose is to provide survivors with an opportunity to grieve and gain a sense of closure. The cemetery is a place for the living as much as it is a place for the dead.

But what about when the dearly departed are not human – but our pets?

The Spanish city of Barcelona recently announced that it will be investing in the establishment of the country’s first public pet cemetery. Set to open next year, it will offer both burials and cremations – with an estimated 7,000 carried out each year.

For me, as someone who has spent years researching the development of pet cemeteries elsewhere in the world, this news came as a shock. Barcelona is a densely populated city with limited privately owned land – one where 50% of families own a pet.

How did a city that is home to 180,000 dogs not already have a public pet cemetery? Until now, the service was only provided by the private sector, according to Barcelona’s Councilor for Climate Emergency and Ecological Transition, Eloi Badia. He added that the municipal-funded initiative was sparked by “constant public demand.”

After all, public pet cemeteries have been around in Europe and the Americas since the late 19th century. Britain’s first public pet cemetery appeared in London’s Hyde Park in 1881. New York’s Hartsdale pet cemetery was founded in 1896, followed a few years later by Paris’ ornate Cimetière des Chiens in 1899.

I became interested in the history of modern pet burial practices while investigating the archaeological record of a centuries-old house in Toronto. I came across a (very) large dog buried in the back garden which, according to the historical record, was occupied between 1840 and 1870.

This dog survived to an elderly age but, sadly, suffered from degenerative joint disease and severe infections during his last months. His ailments progressed to such a state that suggest he received some level of care in his final weeks. He then was buried in a personal plot behind the family home.

This elderly dog led me to think about the different ways people interact with the bodies of their pets after death. Could this behavior be reflective of the relationships they held with their animals in life? In this instance, why take the time to carefully bury a dog in its own space when other, arguably easier options existed?

This, after all, was an era when people often disposed of their dead pets in the river, or might have sold their bodies for meat and skin.

Good hygiene is an obvious reason to choose a burial – no one wants decomposing animal bodies in the street or their garden – but that wouldn’t immediately warrant a personalized, dedicated burial and gravestone.

The most straight forward option would be to dispose of a deceased animal with the household waste. But such treatment would evidently feel less ceremonious and would not offer appropriate emotional closure to what was likely an important relationship.

Like the burial of people, the burial of pets is an intimate cultural practice, one that changes over time and is reflective of the changing relationships that a society has with its cherished creatures.

My study of historic gravestones and epitaphs in Britain from the Victorian period to today shows this changing human-animal relationship. In the 19th century, gravestones were often dedicated to a “loving friend” or “devoted companion,” suggesting pets were mostly considered important friends.

By the early 20th century, pets had become members of the family – evidenced by the appearance of family surnames on the gravestones, and loving epitaphs written by “Mummy and Daddy.”

Society’s changing attitude towards the role of animals in the afterlife can also be found. Fast-forward a few decades later, and gravestones were more likely to reference a reunion then earlier ones. For example, the owners of Denny, the “brave little cat” buried in an east London cemetery in 1952, wrote on his epitaph “God bless until we meet again.”

I wonder what the epitaphs in Barcelona’s new cemetery will reveal about modern Catalan relationships with animals.

Over time, our ways of treating our animal dead appear to reflect an even closer relationship in life. Once strictly forbidden by law, the last decade saw many jurisdictions, like the state of New York, allowing the co-burial of cremated animals and people, which will undoubtedly lead to changing funerary and commemoration practices for both humans and animals.

For me, the most remarkable similarity between modern and historic pet cemeteries is the striking evidence for the heartbreak and taboos around grieving for animals.

The connection that someone has with their pet can be just as strong and just as significant as their relationships with other humans. Yet today, as in over 100 years ago, individuals continue to struggle to find the appropriate outlet to express their pain, hiding heartache for fear of the social repercussions that might come with public acknowledgment of the existence of such a bond.

The RSPCA reassures the public on its website that they should not feel shame for their grief. In the UK, charities like the Blue Cross and Rainbow Bridge Pet Loss Grief Center offer counseling to bereaved humans.

Close relationships between people and animals have existed for millennia, but in western European cultures, there were few acceptable ways to mourn that relationship. As society becomes more accepting of the importance of human-animal relationships to our collective wellbeing, it is not surprising to see us follow rituals resembling those we use to mourn the loss of our closest human relationships.

At an estimated cost of €200 ($217) per service at the new Barcelona cemetery, it is important to acknowledge that this opportunity to grieve will not be financially available to everyone in the city.

This will not be a space for all of the city’s pets. Pet owners may opt to keep cremated remains within their home or spread out ashes in a meaningful location instead. Online forums and digital pet cemeteries also provide other opportunities to commemorate the relationship and express grief.

Whether one chooses a pet cemetery or not, there are many acceptable ways to express your grief – and to remember your relationship with the important animals in your life.

#559. Could lowering high blood pressure be as simple as stimulating your ear?

A small device that delivers gentle electrical pulses through the ear could offer a new approach to treating stubborn high blood pressure. In a clinical trial led by researchers at Queen Mary University of London, the technology proved safe, well tolerated, and practical for patients to use at home.

Although the treatment did not significantly lower blood pressure compared with a placebo device across all participants, researchers observed encouraging results in patients with more severe hypertension. The findings suggest that stimulating nerves in the ear could eventually help certain people whose blood pressure remains elevated despite taking medication.

The results come from the SCRATCH-HTN trial, published in Hypertension, a journal of the American Heart Association. Researchers emphasize that larger studies are needed to determine whether the approach can provide meaningful benefits and which patients might respond best.

How Ear Nerve Stimulation Could Lower Blood Pressure

The experimental treatment uses a technique called transcutaneous autonomic neuromodulation (tAN), which aims to influence the autonomic nervous system. This network of nerves automatically controls essential bodily functions, including heart rate, digestion, and blood pressure.

By delivering mild electrical signals to nerves in the ear, researchers hope to influence the body's natural mechanisms for regulating blood pressure without requiring an invasive procedure.

The handheld device, developed by Afferent Medical Solutions, uses two small clips positioned on the tragus, the small flap of cartilage located just in front of the ear canal. These clips deliver gentle electrical stimulation, allowing patients to administer the treatment themselves without visiting a clinic.

Clinical Trial Tests the Device in 63 Patients

The clinical trial, funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), enrolled 63 people with uncontrolled hypertension. All participants were already taking at least one medication to lower their blood pressure, but their readings remained above recommended levels.

Researchers randomly divided the participants into two groups. One received active nerve stimulation, while the other used a sham device designed to serve as a comparison. This approach helped investigators assess whether the electrical stimulation produced benefits beyond those associated with using the device itself.

Participants used their assigned devices for 30 minutes daily during the first two weeks. For the following 10 weeks, they reduced their sessions to once per week.

The results showed that the treatment was safe and generally comfortable for participants. Most also followed the prescribed schedule, suggesting that the technology could be practical for regular use at home.

However, when researchers compared blood pressure changes across the entire study population, they found no statistically significant difference between patients receiving active stimulation and those using the sham device.

Promising Results in Patients With Severe Hypertension

Despite the lack of a significant overall benefit, the researchers identified a potentially important finding among participants who entered the trial with more severe hypertension.

A prespecified subgroup analysis, meaning the researchers had planned this comparison before examining the results, revealed a greater reduction in blood pressure among these patients when they received active nerve stimulation rather than the sham treatment.

This finding raises the possibility that the technique may work better for people with particularly difficult-to-control blood pressure. However, the trial was relatively small, and the subgroup results require confirmation in larger studies before any conclusions can be drawn about its effectiveness.

Dr. Ajay Gupta, Chief Investigator, Clinical Reader at Queen Mary University of London and Consultant Physician at Barts Health NHS Trust, said:

"This is an exciting, highly patient-acceptable technology that could ultimately provide a cheap, scalable solution for millions. Although the trial did not achieve significance across the broad unselected group, those with more severe hypertension exhibited a clear blood pressure reduction in the active group compared to sham.

"As the first and most thorough study of its kind, this points towards important new research directions that could help us understand whether non-invasive neuromodulation can benefit particular groups of patients with uncontrolled hypertension."

A Potential New Option for Hard-to-Control Blood Pressure

High blood pressure, also known as hypertension, is a major contributor to cardiovascular disease and increases the risk of heart attacks and strokes. It places additional strain on blood vessels and the heart, potentially causing serious damage over time.

Although medications help many patients manage their condition, a substantial number continue to have blood pressure readings above recommended targets even while receiving treatment.

This ongoing challenge has encouraged scientists to explore additional approaches that could complement existing therapies.

Ear nerve stimulation is one such possibility. Because the device is small, does not require surgery, and can be used at home, it could offer a convenient option if future research establishes that it reliably lowers blood pressure in certain patients.

For now, the findings represent an early step rather than proof of an effective new treatment. The researchers say larger clinical trials will be essential to confirm the encouraging results in patients with severe hypertension and identify which groups, if any, could benefit most from this approach.

#552. NASA’s asteroid Bennu samples reveal a 4.5-billion-year-old secret

Samples collected from asteroid Bennu by NASA are revealing surprising new details about how our Solar System formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago. A new chemical analysis challenges earlier theories about the asteroid's birthplace and suggests that Jupiter played an important role in shaping the materials from which Bennu and other ancient objects formed.

Bennu completes one orbit around the Sun every 1.2 years and makes a relatively close approach to Earth about every six years, coming within roughly 300,000 kilometers (186,000 miles). NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission traveled to the asteroid, collected material from its surface, and successfully delivered the samples to Earth in 2023.

On September 23, 2023, a capsule carrying approximately 120 grams of asteroid material landed in the Utah desert. Scientists around the world subsequently received small portions of the precious cargo for detailed study.

Among them was Maria Schönbächler, Professor of Isotope Geochemistry at ETH Zurich, whose laboratory received half a gram of Bennu material. Her team immediately began examining the samples for clues about the asteroid's history.

The research, now published in Science Advances, has uncovered a distinctive chemical signature that not only helps identify Bennu's origins but also provides new evidence about the processes that shaped the early Solar System.

Bennu's Chemical Fingerprint Reveals Its Cosmic Relatives

To investigate Bennu's origins, the ETH Zurich researchers examined isotopes of three elements: iron, titanium, and chromium.

Isotopes are different forms of the same chemical element that contain varying numbers of neutrons, giving them slightly different masses. By measuring the relative amounts of these isotopes, scientists can identify a material's chemical fingerprint. These patterns provide clues about where an asteroid's ingredients originated and, in some cases, when they formed.

The analysis revealed that iron and titanium are distributed remarkably evenly throughout Bennu's material. It also uncovered a close chemical relationship between Bennu, the asteroid Ryugu, and a rare group of primitive meteorites known as CI meteorites.

CI meteorites are carbon-rich remnants of the early Solar System that have undergone relatively little alteration over billions of years. They are exceptionally uncommon among meteorites recovered on Earth.

Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites share strikingly similar isotopic fingerprints, suggesting that their original materials came from the same reservoir of ancient cosmic dust. Their chemical signatures also differ substantially from those of other known asteroids, meteorite groups, and planets.

These similarities offer an important clue about where these objects formed and what conditions existed during the Solar System's earliest stages.

Scientists Discover Bennu's Unexpected Birthplace

For years, researchers generally believed that primitive asteroids like Bennu originated in the outer Solar System, perhaps in the same distant regions where comets formed. They also suspected that Bennu's original material came together relatively late in the Solar System's development.

The new findings challenge both assumptions.

Instead, the researchers propose that the material that became Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites accumulated near a boundary known as the water-ice line.

This boundary marks the region in the disk surrounding the young Sun where temperatures were low enough for water vapor to freeze into ice. Around 4.5 billion years ago, it may have been a particularly important meeting place for material traveling from different parts of the developing Solar System.

Dust originating in both the inner and outer regions mixed near this boundary. Water ice helped the smallest particles stick together, allowing them to gradually accumulate into larger bodies.

"Bennu is a hybrid: the material does not clearly match either the inner or the outer Solar System," says Schönbächler.

Its unusual chemical composition suggests that Bennu formed from a combination of materials originating in both regions rather than from a single, isolated source.

Jupiter May Have Played a Crucial Role in Bennu's Formation

The researchers also developed a scenario explaining how this mixing could have occurred, with Jupiter emerging as a key player.

Jupiter began forming extraordinarily early in Solar System history, within approximately one million years of the Sun's birth. At that time, the young Sun was surrounded by a rotating disk of gas, dust, and ice.

Within this disk, countless small particles gradually collided and stuck together, building larger objects through a process called accretion. Over time, some of these growing bodies became asteroids and planets.

As Jupiter rapidly increased in mass, its immense gravitational influence began reshaping the distribution of material throughout the disk.

The researchers suggest that the growing gas giant acted somewhat like a structural barrier, preventing most larger particles from crossing its orbital region while still allowing finer dust to move around it.

This fine material, originating from different parts of the disk, could then mix extensively near the water-ice line.

According to the proposed scenario, the parent bodies of Bennu and Ryugu, along with the objects that produced CI meteorites, formed from this thoroughly mixed material.

Jupiter may therefore have helped determine not only where these ancient asteroids formed but also which ingredients became incorporated into them.

Ancient Dust and Ice Reveal the Ingredients That Built Earth

The proposed formation scenario could also explain two of Bennu's most intriguing characteristics: its abundance of water-bearing material and its chemical resemblance to the Sun.

Near the water-ice boundary, ice could evaporate as temperatures changed, releasing water vapor that later condensed again in cooler regions. This process would have helped concentrate water in the material from which Bennu formed.

Meanwhile, Jupiter's influence may have limited the movement of larger particles, leaving the asteroid's parent material dominated by fine dust.

Because these tiny particles could circulate and mix efficiently throughout the disk, they preserved a chemical composition that closely resembles the overall elemental mixture found in the Sun.

The process is somewhat similar to the way fine household dust spreads and settles across many different surfaces rather than remaining in one location.

This extensive mixing could explain why Bennu's material retains such a representative sample of the ingredients present during the Solar System's earliest history.

"Bennu may offer our best glimpse of the original mix of chemical elements from which the terrestrial planets were ultimately built," as Schönbächler states.

Bennu is considered an exceptionally primitive asteroid because its material formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago and has experienced relatively little chemical alteration since then.

That makes its samples particularly valuable for reconstructing the conditions that existed before Earth and the other rocky planets finished forming.

By studying the asteroid's chemistry, scientists can better understand how the Solar System developed and which processes determined the composition of its planets.

Bennu's water-bearing minerals and organic compounds also make it important for investigating how early Earth obtained some of the chemical ingredients necessary for life.

Although these findings do not establish exactly how those materials reached our planet, they provide valuable evidence about where such ingredients originated and how they were distributed throughout the young Solar System.

A New Mission to Mars' Moon Could Reveal More

Despite the new discoveries, several questions remain unanswered.

"We are now wondering whether other asteroids have the same isotopic signature as Bennu and Ryugu," Schönbächler goes on to state.

Researchers also want to determine more precisely how Jupiter influenced the movement of dust and whether its gravitational effects were responsible for favoring the accumulation of particularly fine particles.

Additional asteroid samples could help scientists test the proposed formation scenario and establish whether Bennu's chemical signature is shared by other ancient bodies.

With the Bennu analysis now complete, Schönbächler is looking ahead to another opportunity to examine material from the early Solar System.

Japan's space agency, JAXA, is preparing a sample-return mission to Phobos, one of Mars' two moons. The mission is scheduled to launch at the end of October 2026, with the goal of collecting material from the Martian moon and bringing it back to Earth.

"It would be exciting to also obtain this material to analyse it in my laboratory," she says.

Schönbächler plans to apply to JAXA for access to the samples. However, the scientific community will need to be patient. The capsule carrying material from Phobos is not expected to return to Earth until 2031.

Those future samples could provide another important piece of the puzzle, helping researchers determine how different regions of the early Solar System exchanged material and ultimately produced the planets, moons, and asteroids we observe today.

#541. Astronomers may have discovered the first moon beyond our Solar System

Astronomers may have found the first moon beyond our Solar System, but the mysterious object is unlike any moon we know. Observations from the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope (ESO's VLT) have uncovered evidence of an enormous moon-like body in the CD-35 2722 star system. Rather than orbiting a planet, this unusual object circles a brown dwarf, a celestial body more massive than a planet but not massive enough to become a star.

If confirmed, the discovery could mark the first detection of a natural satellite outside our Solar System, potentially changing how astronomers define moons and planets.

Kevin Hoy, an ESO student based in Chile and lead author of the study published in Nature, spent months examining the CD-35 2722 system. He describes its unusual arrangement as "super weird" compared with our own Solar System.

At the center of this relatively young system is CD-35 2722, a star with approximately half the Sun's mass. Orbiting the star is a brown dwarf, an object that occupies the murky boundary between planets and stars. Brown dwarfs are generally too small to sustain the hydrogen fusion that powers ordinary stars, yet they are more massive than planets.

The newly identified object orbits this brown dwarf, creating a remarkable arrangement in which a massive satellite circles an object that itself revolves around a star.

"This system is somewhat hard to define using Solar-System-based words like 'planet' and 'moon'," states Hoy, who is also affiliated with the Universidad Diego Portales and the Millennium Nucleus of Young Exoplanets and their Moons (YEMS) in Chile.

The researchers refer to the newly detected object as an exosatellite. It has at least as much mass as Jupiter, while the brown dwarf it orbits is more than 30 times as massive as Jupiter. This makes the possible moon far more massive than any natural satellite in our Solar System.

"The exosatellite is clearly massive enough to be a planet, but it does not orbit a star, though it orbits an object that orbits a star," says Hoy. "Being the third wheel in this system makes us want to call it a moon, even if it is nothing like the small, rocky moons we have in our system."

A Giant Exomoon That Challenges Cosmic Definitions

The unusual discovery highlights a fundamental problem with applying familiar astronomical labels to objects found in distant star systems.

An exosatellite is a natural satellite located beyond our Solar System, while the term exomoon generally refers to a natural satellite orbiting a planet or another celestial body outside our Solar System. However, astronomers have not established a universally accepted definition of an exomoon.

That distinction becomes especially important in the CD-35 2722 system, where the possible moon is itself massive enough to be considered a planet under some classification schemes.

Alice Zurlo, director of YEMS and a collaborator on the study, explains: "The satellite we report is a giant gaseous body orbiting a highly massive companion, itself several times the mass of Jupiter."

The arrangement is very different from the familiar structure of our Solar System, where planets orbit the Sun and moons orbit planets.

"We have a clear delineation between the planets and the Sun in the Solar System, so defining things like moons is simple. In the CD-35 2722 system, where we are blurring the lines between stars, planets, and moons, the whole thing becomes more complicated to describe," adds Zurlo, who is also an astrophysicist at Universidad Diego Portales.

Scientists Search for the First Confirmed Exomoon

Astronomers have spent years looking for moons around objects beyond our Solar System, but no candidate has yet been detected with enough certainty to establish a confirmed discovery.

That makes the latest findings particularly significant. Although scientists have identified more than 6,000 exoplanets, only a handful of possible exomoons have emerged from observations, and the evidence supporting them remains limited.

Earlier in 2026, a research team led by Quentin Kral reported possible signs of a satellite in the HD 206893 star system. Their observations, obtained with ESO's Very Large Telescope Interferometer, suggested that a moon-like companion might exist, but they did not establish a definitive detection.

The CD-35 2722 system now offers another promising opportunity, supported by measurements of how the suspected moon affects the motion of its larger companion.

How Astronomers Detected the Possible Alien Moon

To investigate CD-35 2722, Hoy, Zurlo, and their colleagues used the CRIRES+ instrument installed on ESO's Very Large Telescope.

The team applied a technique known as the radial velocity method, which was also used to discover the first exoplanet orbiting a Sun-like star.

This approach works by measuring extremely small changes in an object's motion. When two celestial bodies orbit one another, their mutual gravitational attraction causes both to move around a shared center of mass. Even a much smaller companion can produce a detectable wobble in the larger object's motion.

By examining subtle changes in the brown dwarf's motion, the researchers identified what they consider strong evidence that another massive body is orbiting it.

"As exotic as it is, this system is truly unique and represents a breakthrough: the first plausible detection of an exosatellite," says Zurlo.

Confirming moons beyond our Solar System would do more than add another category of objects to astronomical catalogs. These discoveries could help researchers understand how planetary systems form, how their components interact, and why some systems develop structures dramatically different from our own.

Future observations could also reveal whether massive satellites like the one proposed in CD-35 2722 are rare exceptions or part of a much broader population of unusual celestial objects.

ESO's upcoming Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is expected to play a major role in that search. Equipped with an enormous 39-meter mirror and advanced scientific instruments, the telescope will give astronomers the ability to detect smaller exomoons that are currently beyond the reach of existing observatories.

As scientists begin identifying more satellites in distant planetary systems, they may discover that familiar distinctions between stars, planets, and moons are not as universal as once assumed.

A satellite is any object that travels around another object. Satellites can be natural (like our own moon) or artificial (like a spacecraft).

An exosatellite is a satellite located outside our Solar System. The term exomoon generally describes a natural satellite orbiting a planet or another object beyond our Solar System, although astronomers have not agreed on an official definition.

The possible companion in CD-35 2722 illustrates why that definition matters. With a mass comparable to or greater than Jupiter's, it resembles a planet in size and composition, yet its orbital relationship with the brown dwarf makes it a candidate for an entirely different classification.

#538. Brutal, revolutionary empire builder: Genghis Khan's legacy

Berlin is host to a major exhibition on the founder of the Mongol Empire and his successors. Here's how Genghis Khan disrupted the course of history.

Genghis Khan is one of history's most radical figures. A blood-thirsty invader whose conquests caused the deaths of an estimated 40 million people, he was also a visionary political thinker who established the largest contiguous land empire in history.

The founder of the Mongolian Empire died in 1227. Ahead of the 800th anniversary of his death, Berlin's James-Simon-Galerie explores his legacy and the "Mongol Century" (1250–1350) in the exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols."

The ruler remains "incredibly relevant" to this day, says Matthias Wemhoff, the director of the exhibition project.

"What the era of Genghis Khan — the 'Mongol century,' if you will — vividly demonstrates is how quickly established structures can collapse, how rapidly vast empires can vanish from the map and how swiftly the entire landscape can be utterly transformed," the archaeologist tells DW.

The charismatic leader's unprecedented impact on the world also evokes present-day disruptive individuals who are triggering sudden, unpredictable societal shifts, says Wemhoff: "There's always been this question: Are world politics shaped by individuals or rather by groups and sociological processes?"

In this case, the answer is clear, says the museum director, who also co-wrote a 2026 book on the nomadic leader.

"If Genghis Khan hadn't existed, the course of world history would truly have been entirely different — so much of it is inextricably linked to his person," Wemhoff says.

Genghis Khan was born as Temujin around the year 1162. His father, a Mongol chieftain called Yesugei, died when he was still a child. His family was abandoned by its tribe. To secure his familial position, Temujin killed his older half-brother, Bekter, when he was still a young teenager.

Thanks to his charismatic personality, he built alliances and found followers who helped him become the sole ruler on the Mongolian steppe. He formally adopted the title of Genghis Khan in 1206.

At its peak, the Mongol Empire covered approximately 24 million square kilometers (about 9.3 million square miles), stretching from China and Korea to parts of Eastern Europe and the Middle East, and accounting for over 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Wemhoff lists various innovations under his rule: For one, Genghis Khan transformed warfare. Commanders were promoted for skill, discipline and loyalty rather than based on aristocratic rank or tribal loyalties. Soldiers were organized into decimal units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

Despite being illiterate by modern standards, he also pushed for the introduction of the first written script for the Mongolian language, which allowed to build a state bureaucracy, track population and tax registers, record decrees, issue legal decisions and handle diplomacy.

A set of laws were established that replaced fragmented tribal customs with a unified legal system.

Trade was simplified through his adoption of a paper currency.

A policy of religious freedom was also established across the Mongol Empire, allowing all individuals to practice their chosen faith without state coercion.

Before the Mongol Empire, the Silk Road was fragmented by local conflicts and heavy taxes. Mongol rule placed the entire route under a single authority, making long-distance travel vastly safer.

The Mongol Empire is known as a period of relative political stability, economic growth and cultural exchange, an era historians call the "Pax Mongolica."

But "it all came with incredible levels of cruelty. The millions of people who fell victim to the Mongols — the mass executions, the abuse of women — this happened on a scale previously unheard of," notes Wemhoff.

The exhibition opens with a raid that happened after Genghis Khan's death, but took place just 260 kilometers (around 160 miles) away from Berlin: the 1241 Battle of Legnica, in Silesia, Poland.

It embodies the brutality and intensity of the Mongols' methods.

There came the "highly mobile, agile Mongols on small horses — lightly armored in lamellar gear, wearing helmets that left the face exposed, and wielding bows capable of firing 20 or more arrows in a single minute," points out Wemhoff.

European knights, heavily armored and mounted on large, massive warhorses, didn't stand a chance against their enemies.

"When a hail of arrows rained down on them, the horses eventually went down, and the knights ended up lying on the ground, stuck on their back, much like a June bug," he adds. "The two styles of combat were simply mismatched."

The Mongols' western campaigns shifted historic power centers in a way that still impacts world politics today.

For example, after the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus', which was the first East Slavic state, its thriving capital Kyiv was reduced to a depopulated ruin. Moscow became a key regional power, partly because it chose to cooperate with the Mongols rather than pursue resistance.

"In our Western European collective memory, the Mongol invasion plays hardly any role. It's something of an anecdote," says Wemhoff, noting that the era of Genghis Khan and his immediate successors is barely mentioned in standard Western European school curricula.

Germans are mainly familiar with the name of the empire's founder through the 1979 Euro-disco pop band Dschingis Khan, which represented West Germany at the Eurovision Song Contest with a campy song of the same name that reduced complex history to extreme tropes.

But in eastern Europe, the Mongol invasions "changed everything fundamentally," says Wemhoff. "And so fundamentally that we still feel the consequences of it today."

Technologies developed and tested in orbit to remove space junk and fix broken satellites can be speedily repurposed to build fleets of bodyguard spacecraft to protect important satellites from adversary attacks, space clean-up company Astroscale thinks.

Astroscale made history with its 2024 up-close in-orbit inspection of a discarded Japanese rocket stage during the pioneering ADRAS-J mission in low Earth orbit (LEO), the region of space below altitudes of 1,200 miles (2,000 kilometers).

The firm's ELSA-D mission, which ran from 2021 to 2024, was the first commercial endeavor to demonstrate orbital capture technologies in LEO. Now, this technology can come in handy as Western space assets face increasing threats from Russia.

Speaking on the sidelines of an event in London earlier this month, Alex Godfrey, Astroscale's head of defense, said the company could have a protector spacecraft ready for launch in under three years.

"In in-orbit servicing, we have to achieve very high levels of agility to be able to dock and close to a distance of zero meters to refuel something or remove it from orbit," Godfrey told Space.com. "With space control, you're rarely getting quite as close, so in some ways it's simpler because you are further away."

Space control — a military term for ensuring the space around Earth remains orderly and peaceful — is quickly becoming a hot talking point. Godfrey's remarks came just as the world witnessed in shock as Russia made close-up maneuvers around a radar satellite owned by Finland headquartered Earth-observation company ICEYE. The October incident, described by experts as the first rendezvous of two active satellites in low Earth orbit conducted without the consent of the target, raised concerns Russia may be preparing to damage the satellite, which is part of a fleet providing critical intelligence to Ukraine.

U.S. Under Secretary of State Thomas G. DiNanno described Russia's actions on X as "threatening behavior" and "consistent with on-orbit weapons operations."

The ICEYE incident is only the latest in a long string of activities conducted by Russia in space designed either to eavesdrop on or interfere with the activity of critical Western space assets. The Luch-1 and Luch-2 satellites have been stalking Europe's and America's communications satellites in geostationary orbit — the busy region 22,000 miles (36,000 km) above the planet where satellites have a fixed view of a portion of the globe—since 2014. Germany's military space command chief Major General Michael Traut admitted earlier this year that the Luch spacecraft have been spying on European strategic communications in space for years.

A bodyguard satellite would make sure no such intruder gets dangerously close. Godfrey said that the cost of a bodyguard mission would be on the order of a single percent of the value of a military-grade geostationary communications constellation such as the U.K.'s £8.4-billion Skynet fleet.

"To build a bodyguard satellite is absolutely within the capability and the budget of a single country like the U.K.," said Godfrey. "It would be similar to a carrier strike group where you have a [naval aircraft] carrier, but that carrier never leaves the port on its own. It has an escort of much smaller, lower-cost systems that move around to protect it."

Although Astroscale, headquartered in Japan but with significant presence in the U.K., is a leader in the commercial orbital rendezvous and proximity operations technology, no Western players currently match the deployed capabilities of Russia and China. Europe, in particular, is under-equipped for defense and military operations in space, according to the Secure World Foundation's Global Counterspace Capabilities Report. With Russia's accelerating orbital forays, the clock appears to be ticking.

Godfrey proposes a pan-European fleet of orbital bodyguards patrolling clusters of European assets to ward off future Russian attempts to jam or damage the satellites.

"The UK, for example, has multiple assets in different positions around the geostationary ring," Godfrey said. "That is difficult to protect with a single spacecraft. So what I encourage nations to think about is how to form a geo-patrol force through collaboration. If France has assets in a similar location to the UK and maybe Germany, then it makes sense for one spacecraft to protect two or three allied spacecraft, and then, in another region, another country takes responsibility for the protection."

Nick Shave, the UK managing director at Astroscale, told Space.com that the company sees interest in its technologies and is "in discussions" with "companies and customers across Europe" although it has not signed any contracts to develop bodyguard satellites.

"We have spent a good amount of time talking over the last few years, but now we have to go and really deliver," Shave said. "The UK government is promising funding for space control development from 2030 onwards. So what needs to happen now is really preparing the ground so that we've got the right capabilities, the right investment and the right demonstrator capability, by 2030."

In the meantime, Russia's COSMOS 2612 stalker satellite keeps lingering 298 miles (480 km) from the ICEYE intelligence satellite in LEO, keeping the Western space community on edge.

#532. A hidden bias in human language challenges 70 years of science

Researchers at the University of Vermont have discovered a surprising pattern in human language that challenges a scientific theory widely accepted for more than 70 years. Their findings suggest that the meaning of words is shaped by something more fundamental than emotion: our understanding of power, danger, and order.

The study, published in Science Advances, introduces a new approach called "ousiometrics," which uses mathematical methods to measure the essential meaning of language. Its most striking finding is that the words people use show a consistent and widespread preference for safety over danger.

This hidden safety bias could transform how researchers study language, understand human behavior, and develop artificial intelligence systems that interpret what people say and write.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Safety Bias in Human Language

Since the 1950s, much of the scientific understanding of word meaning has relied on a framework developed through pioneering research by psychologist Charles Osgood and others. Known as the VAD model, it describes meaning through three emotional dimensions: valence (positive vs. negative), arousal (excited vs. calm), and dominance (controlling vs. submissive).

This approach became influential in psychology, linguistics, and artificial intelligence, particularly in systems designed to identify the emotional tone of written language.

However, the University of Vermont researchers found evidence that this long-established model overlooks important aspects of how meaning is organized.

Their analysis examined billions of individual uses of more than 20,000 words across a wide range of real-world texts. Using advanced computational techniques, the team identified a different underlying structure. They found that the three dimensions in the traditional VAD model are not independent of one another, as previously assumed.

Instead, their results indicate that word meaning can be more effectively described through three separate dimensions: power (weak vs. powerful), danger (safe vs. dangerous), and structure (ordered vs. chaotic).

The research, supported by the US National Science Foundation, Google, MassMutual, and other funders, found that this new framework accounted for more than 90% of the variation in measured word meaning. The traditional VAD approach explained approximately 72%.

That improvement could be especially important as large language models, automated content moderation, and other AI technologies increasingly depend on understanding human communication.

Why Human Language Favors Safety Over Danger

One of the study's most unexpected discoveries emerged when the researchers examined how words actually appear in everyday communication.

Across books, news reporting, social media, and spoken language, words associated with safety appeared much more frequently than those associated with danger.

This finding offers a new explanation for a familiar phenomenon called the Pollyanna principle, which describes the tendency for human language to lean toward positive rather than negative words.

For decades, researchers generally interpreted this pattern as evidence that people favor positive emotional expressions. The new analysis suggests that something more fundamental may be responsible.

According to the researchers, the apparent preference for positivity largely reflects an underlying preference for safety.

"The Pollyanna principle's positivity bias," the study concludes. "is, in fact, a one-dimensional projection of an underlying safety bias."

"This is a big observation that comes out of this work," said Peter Dodds, director of the UVM's Complex Systems Institute and senior author of the study. "Expressions of safety are crucial to all language."

Could Human Language Be Shaped by Survival?

The discovery raises an intriguing possibility: the structure of human language may reflect evolutionary pressures associated with staying alive.

From this perspective, words do more than communicate feelings. They also help people recognize threats, evaluate uncertainty, establish trust, and coordinate actions with others.

Being able to distinguish between safety and danger is essential to survival. The researchers suggest that this basic human priority may also influence how meaning is organized in language.

Throughout daily life, people constantly exchange information about whether individuals, places, situations, and behaviors are threatening or safe. The new findings suggest that these distinctions may be more central to communication than previously recognized.

They also offer a different interpretation of why positive language is so common. Rather than simply expressing happiness or approval, many positive words may convey a sense of security, stability, or predictability.

Julia Zimmerman, a postdoctoral researcher in UVM's Computational Story Lab and a co-author of the study, said the three newly identified dimensions reflect experiences that are fundamental to human life.

"Power, danger, and structure," she said, "are relevant to every person that's ever lived."

Researchers have also long recognized that people tend to favor language associated with goodness and low aggression. The team argues that these familiar tendencies may arise from the same deeper pattern.

"We now understand," the team writes, that these are "shadows of an underlying linguistic safety bias."

Although the results are consistent with a possible connection between language and survival, they do not establish that evolutionary pressures directly caused the safety bias.

A Discovery That Could Change How AI Understands Language

The findings have implications that extend well beyond linguistics.

Many artificial intelligence systems use sentiment analysis to determine whether language expresses positive, negative, or neutral feelings. Some approaches also draw on the traditional VAD framework to measure emotional meaning.

If that framework does not fully capture the underlying structure of language, systems built around it could overlook important distinctions in what people communicate.

The researchers suggest that incorporating power, danger, and structure could eventually help AI models interpret language more accurately and explain their assessments more clearly. This may be particularly useful when analyzing trust, risk, and decision-making.

In linguistics, the findings offer a different way to understand the foundations of meaning. Instead of treating emotional tone as the primary organizing principle, the new framework emphasizes distinctions related to strength, safety, and predictability.

Psychology could also be affected. Decades of studies have used the VAD model to investigate emotions, perception, and behavior. A different set of fundamental dimensions could encourage researchers to reconsider how some of those findings are interpreted.

The discovery may also interest neuroscientists. The human brain is highly sensitive to threats and signals of safety, and the newly identified linguistic bias suggests that these biological priorities may be reflected in language. This potential connection could help researchers explore relationships between brain activity and symbolic communication, although further research would be needed to establish them.

A New Tool Measures the Hidden Meaning of Words

To investigate these patterns, the researchers created analytical methods designed to measure meaning across enormous collections of written and spoken language.

One of their central innovations is the "ousiometer," a tool that rapidly evaluates the essential meaning of large bodies of text and produces an average meaning score.

(The word "ouisa" comes from Ancient Greek and is a root for the English word "essence.")

The ousiometer builds on an earlier tool developed by the research team called the "hedonometer" (a happiness meter). While that instrument measures the overall emotional tone of language, the new approach examines broader patterns of meaning.

The researchers demonstrated that the ousiometer can analyze material from remarkably different sources, including Jane Austen's novels, Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, the New York Times, Wikipedia, Twitter, and transcripts of talk radio programs.

The tool can also track how meaning shifts as a story unfolds.

For example, the researchers analyzed an English translation of Victor Hugo's Les Misérables and mapped its changing meaning throughout the novel.

They called this changing pattern an "ousiometric trajectory." The visualization resembles the twisting shape of a multicolored protein, with the narrative moving across a grid based on four contrasting pairs: dangerous/safe, weak/powerful, gentle/aggressive, and bad/good.

By following this path, researchers can observe how the underlying meaning of the text changes from one section of the story to another.

Why Counting Word Frequency Changes the Results

Another important innovation involved distinguishing between words themselves and how often people actually use them.

In linguistics, researchers describe individual words as categories called "types," while each appearance of a word in text is known as a "token."

(For example, as a category "apple" is a type and every time the word "apple" is used in a sentence is a token.)

This distinction matters because a word that appears millions of times contributes much more to everyday communication than one that is rarely used.

Earlier research often gave each word equal importance, regardless of its frequency. The Vermont team instead incorporated how often words appear in real communication, allowing patterns to emerge that might otherwise remain hidden.

This approach was particularly important for identifying the widespread preference for safety-related language.

The ten-member research team was led by Peter Dodds and Chris Danforth, professors in UVM's College of Engineering and Mathematical Sciences. Their collaborators included researchers from the Santa Fe Institute, the Complexity Science Hub in Austria, Howard Hughes Medical Center, the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Adelaide, and MassMutual Data Science.

Rethinking What Human Language Reveals About Us

Understanding this hidden safety bias could have consequences for how researchers study communication across society.

If words consistently reflect a preference for safety, that tendency may influence how stories are told, how information spreads, and how people interpret events and experiences.

The findings could eventually inform research into political communication, mental health messaging, and AI systems designed to interact with people.

More broadly, the study challenges the idea that human language can be understood primarily through emotions such as happiness, fear, and excitement.

Instead, the researchers propose that meaning has a deeper structure connected to power, danger, and order. Their findings suggest that language may reflect the challenges humans face when navigating uncertain environments, maintaining relationships, and identifying threats.

By revealing this previously overlooked structure, the team offers a new way to understand what words communicate and why certain kinds of meaning dominate everyday conversation. Human language, their research suggests, may be more closely connected to our fundamental need for safety than scientists have traditionally recognized.

Increasing amounts of US capital are flowing into Germany's traditional industrial firms and family-owned businesses. As many business owners struggle to find successors, more companies are becoming acquisition targets.

What do the long-established German companies Birkenstock, Viessmann, Linde and ebm-papst have in common? All have fallen into American hands.

Traditional German family-owned businesses and small-to-medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) have become increasingly attractive to US companies and investors.

"From what we are seeing in the market, the interest from US investors in German companies is definitely still there," said Samar-Ahmad Albrecht-Mahmood of Veracity Capital Investments, which advises companies looking to invest in Germany.

German companies, particularly those in the industrial and manufacturing sector, remain attractive, "because many of them are highly specialized, have strong customer relationships and very good technical know-how," he told DW. "In many cases, these are businesses that have built up a strong position in their niche over decades."

At the same time, many German business owners are reaching an age where they are thinking about selling their business, Albrecht-Mahmood underscored, pointing out that "there is often no successor within the family."

#523. Scientists discover how pancreatic cancer rewrites its DNA to escape treatment

Scientists have uncovered new details about how pancreatic cancer constantly changes its genetic makeup, allowing it to become more aggressive, spread throughout the body, and resist treatment. By examining nearly half a million individual cancer cells, researchers identified genetic changes that could reveal new ways to fight one of the deadliest forms of cancer.

The study, conducted by scientists at NYU Langone Health and its Perlmutter Cancer Center, focused on pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, a particularly difficult cancer to treat. Nearly 85 percent of patients die within five years of being diagnosed.

Although researchers have already linked more than half a dozen genes to the disease, much remains unknown about how pancreatic cancer develops and adapts. By examining tumor cells individually rather than studying them in large groups, the team gained a clearer understanding of how different populations of cancer cells change their DNA as the disease progresses.

How Pancreatic Cancer Evolves To Escape Treatment

The researchers discovered that pancreatic tumors contain a variety of distinct cell populations known as clones. Each clone consists of cancer cells with similar genetic characteristics, but their behavior can differ dramatically.

Some clones develop changes that help them withstand cancer treatments, while others become better equipped to invade surrounding tissues and spread to other parts of the body. These findings suggest that the cancer's ability to continually reorganize its genetic material plays a central role in both treatment resistance and metastasis.

Previous studies generally divided pancreatic cancer cells into two main categories: classical (less aggressive) and basal (more aggressive). However, because those studies examined large collections of cells together, scientists could not fully explain how cancer cells move from one category to the other.

The latest findings help explain this process. Genetic changes occurring within individual tumors allow cancer cells to shift between these two states, potentially making the disease more dangerous as it develops.

Scientists Identify a Gene That Could Make Pancreatic Cancer Less Aggressive

Published on October 13 in Nature Genetics, the study identified several genetic changes associated with pancreatic cancer progression. One particularly important finding involved MYC, a gene that showed heightened activity in some of the most aggressive cancer cell populations.

In additional laboratory experiments, the researchers blocked MYC activity in aggressive basal pancreatic cancer cells. Remarkably, the cells shifted back toward the less aggressive classical state.

The change also made the cancer cells more exposed to immune cells, suggesting they could become easier for the immune system to recognize and attack. Although further research is needed, the results point to MYC as a promising target for future pancreatic cancer treatments.

"Our study provides the most granular view to date of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, revealing how ongoing genetic reshaping drives tumor evolution over the course of disease," said study lead investigator Despoina Kalfakakou, PhD, a postdoctoral research fellow at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and Perlmutter Cancer Center. "These findings suggest that targeting the mechanisms underlying tumor evolution, including MYC, may help restrain the cancer's rapid progression."

"Our research also suggests that single-cell clonal analysis offers a better approach to understanding complex cancers, like pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, as it develops over time, by mapping how tumors evolve and adapt at each stage of disease progression," said study co-senior investigator Aristotelis Tsirigos, PhD, a professor in the Departments of Medicine and Pathology at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and a member of Perlmutter Cancer Center.

"More broadly, our study results highlight the critical role of clonal diversity, genetic plasticity, and interactions with the tumor's microenvironment in shaping human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma biology," said Dr. Tsirigos, who is also co-director of the Division of Precision Medicine and director of NYU Langone's Applied Bioinformatics Laboratories.

Why Pancreatic Cancer Can Evade the Immune System

The investigation also provided important clues about pancreatic cancer's ability to hide from the immune system, a characteristic that may help explain why the disease often responds poorly to immunotherapy.

Researchers discovered that certain cancer cell clones showed increased activity of the TIGIT gene, which is associated with proteins that help tumors escape immune detection.

At the same time, these clones exhibited reduced activity of the PD1 gene. This combination may help explain why immunotherapies designed to target PD1 have shown limited effectiveness against pancreatic cancer.

Understanding these differences between cancer cell populations could help scientists develop treatments that interfere with the specific mechanisms tumors use to avoid immune attack.

According to Dr. Tsirigos, clinical trials investigating drugs that inhibit MYC or TIGIT are already underway for pancreatic ductal carcinoma and other cancers. He said the latest findings underscore the need for larger studies examining the different states cancer cells can adopt and how treatments might be tailored to target them.

Nearly Half a Million Cancer Cells Reveal Hidden Tumor Diversity

To investigate how pancreatic cancer changes over time, the scientists examined gene activity (using transcriptomics) in 443,451 individual cells collected from 62 tumors belonging to 56 men and women diagnosed with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Six of these patients also provided tumor samples both before and after chemotherapy, allowing researchers to examine changes associated with treatment.

Unlike traditional approaches that measure changes in gene activity across large groups of cells, the new analysis examined individual cells. This provided a much more detailed picture of the different cancer cell populations living within the same tumor.

The results showed that individual tumors contained approximately two to 11 distinct clones, consisting of varying combinations of classical and basal cancer cells.

By tracing the genetic origins of these clones and comparing patterns of gene activity, the researchers connected differences in DNA copy numbers with the types of cells present in each tumor.

This approach allowed them to identify which genes, including MYC and TIGIT, were particularly active at different points during cancer progression.

Together, the findings provide a more detailed understanding of how pancreatic tumors evolve, resist treatment, and spread. They also suggest that interfering with the genetic mechanisms driving these changes could offer new opportunities to combat a disease that remains exceptionally difficult to treat.
