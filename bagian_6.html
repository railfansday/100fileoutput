When the next-gen Tesla Roadster finally debuted, it completely shattered conventional automotive reveals. Instead of simply rolling onto a stage, the prototype climbed a wall, executed a ramp-less flying kickflip, and boasted a zero-to-60 mph sprint in under a single second. It felt more like a sci-fi movie than a car launch.

To pull off the wall-climbing stunt, the vehicle essentially operates as a massive vacuum on wheels. By utilizing four 20-kilowatt fans to suck air from beneath a sealed aerodynamic skirt, the car generates an 11.5% vacuum. This creates roughly 10,360 pounds of artificial downforce, more than enough suction to comfortably keep a 4,400-pound vehicle glued to the ceiling without breaking a sweat.

While the stunts seemed like pure marketing magic, the underlying math actually checks out. According to the deep dive by Engineering Explained in the video above, the Roadster’s flying capabilities are firmly rooted in real-world physics. The kickflip relies on composite storage tanks holding 550 pounds of highly compressed nitrogen at a staggering 10,000 psi. By routing this cold gas through ten strategically placed thrusters, the system generates a massive 18,000 pounds of thrust, giving the car enough lift to physically hover for several seconds.

The acceleration math is equally staggering. The flagship “Rocket” trim utilizes a massive 158-gallon nitrogen capacity to generate up to 5.5 Gs of acceleration, yielding a theoretical 0-to-60 mph time of just 0.55 seconds. When combined with reports that the Roadster targets 330 mph for the production car speed record, it makes total sense why even the Ferrari CEO gave the reveal a perfect 10 out of 10.

The math confirms the car has enough onboard nitrogen to sustain a hover for about 6.7 seconds. Given that the kickflip stunt only lasted around four seconds, the flight capability is absolutely legit. It’s an incredible display of cross-pollinated tech, leading many to wonder if the flying Roadster reveal was actually a SpaceX ad cleverly disguised as a car launch.

The engineering equations unequivocally validate Tesla’s jaw-dropping stunts, but proving a concept on stage is vastly different from delivering a reliable mass-market vehicle. For instance, the fans and aerodynamic skirts required for extreme downforce will be substantial wear items, constantly dragging on the asphalt and ingesting fine road debris. Owners would regularly need to replace these complex components to maintain the car’s gravity-defying parlor tricks.

Tesla has a notoriously spotty track record of delivering on initial promises, from the Cybertruck’s missing seating configurations to long-delayed battery extenders. The physics behind the air thrusters and sub-second sprint times are mathematically sound, proving the engineering team certainly did their homework. Until this rocket-equipped supercar actually lands in a customer’s driveway with all its advertised features intact, maintaining a healthy dose of skepticism is the most logical approach.

#5519. What The Battery Show Told Us About the Next Chapter of Electrification

Each fall you can learn a lot about the battery industry by walking through the halls of Huntington Place in Detroit. This year’s Battery Show North America, held October 12th – 15th at Huntington Place, brought together thousands of engineers, executives, and suppliers from cell chemistry and vehicle integration to manufacturing, supply chain, and grid storage.

What stood out wasn’t a single product or chemistry. It was a shift in the questions being asked. We saw less of “what’s possible?” and more of “what’s proven?” That shift matters for every OEM and fleet planning its next electrification program.

Energy storage dominated the agenda. Pacific Northwest National Laboratory’s keynote spotlighted how automakers including Ford, GM, and Volkswagen have converted existing EV battery manufacturing assets to serve surging grid storage demand. Across the industry, battery makers are moving from passenger EVs toward energy storage, defense, industrial, and rail applications.

Some will read this as electrification losing momentum. We see it differently. The industry is sorting out where batteries create clear, measurable value today. Commercial and industrial applications, with predictable duty cycles and a total cost of ownership that can be calculated, sit squarely in that zone.

The opening session came away optimistic about the U.S. battery industry, and the most important word in the ExxonMobil keynote was “competitive.” The goal isn’t to wall off the market but to build an American supply chain that wins on quality, reliability, and delivery.

For OEMs, domestic content now matters on two fronts. It’s a compliance requirement under Buy America and BABA, and it’s a way to gain shorter lead times, closer engineering collaboration, and greater supply certainty.

One of the clearest signals at the battery show wasn’t a single product but in how quickly the ecosystem around batteries is adapting. Cell testing labs and validation partners are retooling to support requirements well beyond passenger vehicles, from grid storage to off-highway equipment to defense.

That adaptability runs from research to production. Pacific Northwest National Laboratory’s Grid Storage Launchpad, a 93,000-square-foot facility backed by the Department of Energy, includes its own prismatic cell production line and was built to close the gap between concept and commercialization. The University of Michigan’s expanded Battery Lab is designed to serve applications ranging from EVs and grid storage to drones and defense.

This is American industrial manufacturing doing what it does best: adapting and improving. The talent, equipment, and expertise built for the automotive industry now serve a much broader range of industries meaning a deeper bench of domestic partners to test and validate battery systems.

If one idea echoed across the sessions, it was this: claims don’t move markets, deployments do. On a panel about scaling energy storage, Peak Energy’s chief scientist said it directly: “What’s really going to drive adoption is just proven deployments.”

So, what comes next? Every technology on display, from new chemistries like solid-state and sodium-ion to wireless battery management, advanced cooling, and second-life storage, now faces the same test: validation, scale, and performance in application. That test isn’t passed in a conference hall or on a spec sheet. It’s passed over thousands of operating hours, in real-world duty cycles, where reliability shows up as uptime.

That’s the right standard. A battery pack is more than its cell. Modules, thermal management, battery management software, enclosures, wiring, connectors and design all determine whether a system performs on day one and beyond.

Heavy-duty vehicles and equipment demand exactly this kind of proof. Vibration, heat, long shifts, and years of hard service leave no room for a battery that only looks good on a spec sheet.

That’s the work Proterra is currently powering. With more than 2,000 commercial vehicles on the road and over 50 million service miles powered, our track record comes from the real world. Our Onyx Battery Platform is engineered to power the most demanding of applications from Class 8 trucks, school bus, transit, industrial equipment and beyond. Its modular architecture allows battery technology proven on the highway to be adapted for additional markets like off-highway, defense, rail, marine and more so OEMs can start from a platform with real-world deployments behind it instead of a clean sheet.

We’re also acting on the supply chain conversation that dominated Detroit. Proterra now offers a U.S. cell option for the Onyx platform, with shipments beginning in 2027. Beyond the cell, we’re investing to onshore and nearshore critical pack and module components, as well as other major internal subsystems. Anchored by that U.S. cell, our onshoring effort is expected to increase American content by more than 600%. As CEO Chris Bailey put it, “The market is asking for greater supply chain certainty and stronger domestic content. This investment ensures we can deliver both while continuing to lead in performance and safety.”

Detroit made one thing clear. The next chapter of electrification belongs to the partners who deliver domestic content, proven real-world performance, and complete battery systems built for the most demanding jobs. That’s the standard we hold ourselves to every day.

#5515. Optivolt Raises $32M to Build the Brain for Solar Panels of the Future

Series A led by VXI Capital, with backing from the people who built modern solar, including founding Enphase CEO Paul Nahi, former SunPower CEO Tom Werner and former New York energy czar Richard Kauffman

Nine years in the making, Optivolt’s energy intelligence turns passive solar hardware into adaptive power systems by managing energy in real time from within the panel

San Francisco — Optivolt, a power electronics company building the intelligence layer for solar, yesterday announced a $32M Series A led by VXI Capital, a new hard tech fund founded by the former CTO of the Defense Innovation Unit of the U.S. Military, with participation from RA Capital, Valo Ventures, and Atlas Innovate. This round brings Optivolt’s total capital raised to date to $42M.

The solar panel has become a commodity. Optivolt gave it a brain. Its Power Balancer embeds active power electronics directly into the panel, managing energy among cell groups in real time to recover power otherwise lost to shade, weather, dirt and uneven degradation. This creates a foundation for adaptive, connected solar systems. With more than 20,000 panels incorporating the Power Balancer already shipped, the company will use the new financing to expand manufacturing and bring the technology to solar systems at every scale, from individual homes to utility-scale projects designed to help power AI data centers.

“Solar became cheap enough to compete. I founded Optivolt to make it intelligent enough to power the world,” said Rohit Kalyanpur, founder and CEO of Optivolt. “For nine years, we’ve rebuilt the solar panel from first principles, embedding aerospace-grade power electronics so it can respond to Earth as it actually is, not the ideal conditions of a lab. The result is the first solar panel system that thinks for itself – one that adapts to shade, weather, dirt and degradation in real time, recovering energy conventional solar leaves behind. The next step is putting it everywhere.”

“The next era of energy will not be built by simply installing more of the same passive solar panels we already have. It will be built by making solar intelligent enough to become the backbone of the global power system,” said Orin Hoffman, Managing Director at VXI Capital. “Optivolt has spent nine years building the architecture for that future. This financing is about taking it from a technical breakthrough to the platform on which the next generation of solar is built.”

“At Enphase, we transformed rooftop solar by managing power at the output of each panel. Today, rising panel power, the widespread adoption of storage, and the urgent need to reduce costs demand a new approach. Optivolt is bringing a new class of power electronics inside the panel itself to unlock the next leap in performance and economics,” said Paul Nahi, the founding CEO of Enphase and Optivolt investor. “I helped build the last generation of solar power electronics. At Optivolt we are building the next.”

Despite decades of progress, the conventional solar panel remains fundamentally passive hardware. Solar panels are rated under controlled laboratory conditions, but operate in a world of shade, dirt, debris and uneven degradation. Inside a conventional panel, one underperforming cell can constrain the output of an entire cell group – or cause a bypass diode to take that group out of production altogether. A localized problem can therefore suppress the output of a much larger section of the panel.

Optivolt redesigned the electrical architecture inside the panel around a power electronics brain. Its Power Balancer redistributes energy among the panel’s cell groups in real time, addressing mismatch before it becomes a larger loss. It replaces conventional bypass diodes and builds rapid shutdown and monitoring into the panel, allowing solar systems to be designed without separate module-level optimizers, microinverters or rapid-shutdown devices.

Putting active power electronics inside a solar panel created a fundamental engineering problem: heat. To solve it, Optivolt assembled a team with experience designing power systems for satellites, bringing the discipline of aerospace – where every watt matters and hardware must survive without repair – to a product built to spend decades inside a solar panel. That team developed the Power Balancer, which operates at 99.4 percent conversion efficiency under typical conditions.

Optivolt holds 19 issued patents and has 27 additional patent applications pending, covering key elements of its panel-integrated power electronics architecture. Optivolt has earned UL certifications covering its integrated junction-box enclosure, panel-integrated electronics, rapid-shutdown system and solar-module safety.

In Optivolt’s internal testing, panels incorporating the technology produced up to six times as much power as panels using bolted-on optimizers under defined shade scenarios.

“At SunPower, we spent nearly three decades pushing the limits of how efficiently a solar panel could turn sunlight into electricity. Optivolt is attacking the other half of the problem: how much energy that panel actually delivers in the real world once it leaves the factory,” said Tom Werner, former CEO of SunPower and Optivolt investor. “The next great solar panel won’t just produce more power under ideal conditions. It will deliver more energy across the imperfect conditions of its working life.”

“I’ve spent most of my career making clean energy financeable at scale – building new financing tools, redesigning markets and driving deployment costs down,” said Richard Kauffman, former Chairman of Energy and Finance for New York and an Optivolt investor. “But finance can only take the industry so far. When the same solar project produces more energy over its lifetime, the economics change everywhere. Optivolt rewrote that equation inside the panel itself, dramatically expanding the share of the world’s energy demand that solar can economically serve.”

Optivolt is a power electronics company building the intelligence layer for solar. Its panel-integrated Power Balancer redistributes energy among cell groups in real time, helping solar systems deliver more energy under real-world conditions while simplifying the hardware around the panel. Optivolt’s long-term goal is to make every solar panel an intelligent, connected node in a global energy network, capable of both managing its own performance and coordinating with the system around it. Founded by Thiel Fellow Rohit Kalyanpur, Optivolt is headquartered in San Francisco, CA.

#5507. What Can Grid Flexibility Actually Do Here?

Electricity-system flexibility has become an increasingly large bucket. Batteries go into it. Demand response goes into it. Virtual power plants, EV charging, interconnectors, hydro, thermal storage and grid-enhancing technologies all get described as flexibility too.

Those resources can all be useful, but a national total of “flexible megawatts” does not tell a grid operator very much about whether a congested transformer can take another connection, whether an evening peak can be covered, or whether the system can get through several difficult days.

I’ve published a new open 99-page TFIE Strategy report, The Flexibility Mesh: A Whole-System Architecture for Reliable, Affordable Electrification, to put those different jobs into one common architecture.

The animation runs the same underlying electricity system through four operating moments. During renewable-surplus midday, useful actions can include charging batteries and EVs, heating water, increasing some industrial loads and exporting electricity where there is somewhere useful for it to go. Move into an evening or winter peak and batteries discharge, some charging can pause, buildings can use heat stored earlier, hydro can increase output and industrial demand may reduce within its operating constraints.

A local network constraint creates a different problem again. An overloaded transformer does not care that there are thousands of megawatts of flexible capacity somewhere else in the country. The resource has to be electrically relevant to that part of the network. Storage, managed charging, operating measures and eventually reinforcement can all have roles, but they have to be compared against the actual constraint.

Prolonged multi-day stress changes the portfolio once more. Response speed remains useful, but duration, replenishment and correlated availability become much more important. A resource that can respond beautifully for two hours does not acquire three days of stored energy simply because it is fast.

This is why the report treats flexibility as a system property rather than a technology category. Different jobs require different resources, locations and durations, while grid expansion and operational flexibility are complementary rather than competing philosophies.

That distinction is easy to lose in technology-led discussions. A battery can be excellent at intraday shifting while being irrelevant to a voltage problem at the wrong node. An interconnector can provide valuable balancing and energy exchange but needs evidence that supply will be available at the other end during the hours for which planners want to rely on it. Industrial loads can be highly controllable while remaining unavailable at particular times because production still has to happen.

The physical resource is also only the beginning of the delivery problem. An asset can be installed, connected and technically controllable without being available to provide the service under discussion. Network permission may be missing. The resource may not be qualified for the relevant product. Customer consent or operating limits may constrain it. Measurement and settlement arrangements have to show what actually happened.

This sounds like administrative detail until the system starts depending on the capacity. Then the difference between theoretical capability and dependable delivery becomes the difference between a useful planning resource and an optimistic spreadsheet assumption.

The commercial side has similar boundaries. Avoiding a grid upgrade may create significant value for electricity consumers, but that avoided cost is not automatically revenue for the company coordinating flexible assets. The beneficiary, buyer, payer, asset owner and service provider can be different parties. A viable arrangement needs to connect the physical job to a legitimate payment route and leave enough value for customers and asset owners to continue participating.

The report makes those relationships visible because they are part of the operating architecture, not something that happens after the engineering is complete. System value, buyer budget, provider revenue, intermediary margin and customer benefit are separate quantities, and the business model has to reconcile them rather than assume they collapse into one pot of money.

Permanent grid infrastructure stays visible throughout. Flexibility can improve utilization of existing networks, help manage temporary constraints and sometimes defer or resize reinforcement. A persistent constraint accompanied by continuing load growth can still need more wire, transformer or substation capacity. Once new capacity is built, the residual flexibility requirement changes again.

That is one reason I called the report The Flexibility Mesh rather than building it around batteries, VPPs or demand response. The physical electricity system runs from interconnected regions through generation, transmission and distribution to customer premises. Storage, thermal flexibility, coordination, operating permission, markets, customer relationships and delivery evidence cross those layers.

The architecture is also available in an interactive version. Rather than trying to read the entire diagram at once, choose an operating moment and see which relationships become important. Individual components can then be explored in more detail, including the economic question: Who benefits, who pays?

The architecture is meant to support better questions, not force every electricity system into one portfolio. Norway, California, an industrial campus, an island grid and a constrained urban distribution network start with different resources and institutions. They can still ask the same thing: what service is required here, which resources can credibly provide it, what allows them to act, and what evidence shows that they actually deliver?

That is a more useful starting point than counting everything that might someday be flexible and treating the total as though it were already available to the grid.

#5501. New EV Battery Gives Gasmobiles The Deep Freeze

The cost of EV batteries is spiraling downwards while the cost of gas and diesel is spiraling up. That’s a picture perfect scenario for accelerating the transition to EVs. The missing link is a level of battery performance that can meet, if not beat, a comparable internal combustion engine, and the Finnish innovator Donut Lab has just taken a -30 degree Celsius step in that direction.

If the name Donut Lab rings a bell, run right out and buy yourself a cigar. CleanTechnica is among the many (many, many) EV enthusiasts following the Donut Lab story, which is a rather unusual one.

The company unveiled its new solid-state EV battery at the Consumer Electronics Show in January, describing it as “the world’s first solid-state battery that is ready for use in OEM vehicle manufacturing,” while also pledging to share the results of third-party testing.

“With a full charge in five minutes and 400 Wh/kg of energy density, it seemed too good to be true. However, for any skeptics out there, Donut Lab is posting a new video series along with supporting material that explains the results of an independent assessment at the VTT Technical Research Centre of Finland,” CleanTechnica reported in January.

That’s right, VTT has been validating Donut Labs’s claims every step of the way, and the startup has been sharing those reports, in excruciating detail. In a bit of clap-back at skeptics who are challenging its claims, Donut Lab directs the curious to the VTT reports on its website at I Donut Believe.

Donut Labs has kept VTT hopping since the big unveil. In March, VTT issued a report that validated the new battery’s performance at 100°C — in other words, the boiling point. That led some Donut-watchers, including CleanTechnica’s Christopher Arcus, to conclude that the battery is not based on lithium, as some others have surmised.

“Speculation that the Donut Lab battery is not real are diminishing as independent lab data shows 100° Celsius performance. Speculation that the battery may be lithium run[s] into difficulty explaining how a battery can work at 100° Celsius,” Arcus observed.

“In the independent tests, the thin membrane surrounding the pouch breached. If the contents were a standard lithium-ion with liquid electrolyte, there would have been significant outgassing and the organic electrolyte would have caught fire,” he further noted.

In the latest news, earlier this week Donut Lab posted a new VTT report validating that the new battery retains 99% capacity at temperatures down to -30 degrees Celsius (-22 degrees Fahrenheit), a claim the company made at the CES unveiling. The cold weather validation is meaningful because it pulls the rug out from under EV critics, who are fond of claiming that cold weather significantly limits battery performance and range.

Cold weather can impact EV battery range, but then again, cold weather also puts a crimp on the fuel efficiency of gas-powered engines, too. Whether gas or electric, most drivers are in the habit of planning trips to make sure they don’t get stranded by the side of the road in cold weather or, for that matter, any weather, which lets a little steam out of the anti-EV argument.

Still, there is always room for improvement. EVs can be engineered with thermal management systems that protect against performance loss in extreme temperatures, but Donut Lab makes the case for doing away with external systems and building resilience into the battery itself.

The VTT test regimen involved a module consisting of 14 battery cells, placed in a temperature chamber at -30°C and left there for five hours. After the module was completely frozen through, tests showed that it retained 97% of its ampere-hour capacity, compared to a previous measurement at +25°C (about +77°F).

“As the report demonstrates, following battery capacity discharges performed during deep-freeze conditions, the module charges and discharges completely normally, with no adverse effects whatsoever resulting from the cold,” Donut Labs summarized in a press statement. The company further emphasized that the testing process did not include any heating, cooling, or insulation systems.

“We intentionally eliminated all the potential advantages a battery might normally have,” Donut Lab CTO Ville Piippo elaborated. “Despite this, the battery remained unaffected by the conditions. A typical lithium-ion battery would struggle to provide power, even for a brief moment, in such cold temperatures.”

Piippo also emphasized that the new battery eschews add-ons for temperature control. “This validates our promise of 99% capacity, which customers can expect from a fully insulated battery module without any heaters or tricks,” Piipp added for good measure.

#5499. Bill calling for nationwide plug-in solar adoption introduced in House

A national bill proposing the legalization of plug-in solar was introduced in the House of Representatives on Oct. 19.

Since late 2025, legislative bodies have been passing bills permitting the consumer-level use of these portable solar generation devices within particular states. HR 10851, also known as the “Socket Full of Sunshine Act,” is the first bill to be drafted supporting nationwide use of plug-in solar products.

Unlike interconnected solar projects, which require permits for construction, plug-in solar setups use one or more solar modules connected to a microinverter that literally plugs into an AC outlet. Rep. Rob Menendez, a Democrat congressman from New Jersey, authored the bill.

“Families are getting squeezed by electric bills that keep going up,” Menendez said in a press release. “Plug-in solar is a simple, affordable way to cut those bills, but outdated rules and unnecessary fees are standing in the way.”

Socket Full of Sunshine proposes removing any interconnection barriers for portable solar systems; and system owners would not be required to obtain utility approval to use them or incur any insurance or operating fees. If enacted, this would apply to plug-in solar systems with a power rating of 1.2 kW or lower.

This legislation would amend a section of the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978 (PURPA), a historic bill that made utilties reponsible for purchasing energy from alternative sources, like wind and solar. Through it, Socket Full of Sunshine would require state utility regulators to initiate preparations for plug-in solar within one year of passage, and establish a standard for it within two years.

The bill would also create a Dept. of Energy grant program for states to issue rebates to low-to-middle-income residents for purchasing plug-in solar systems. Those rebates would not exceed $1,000 and would only be eligible if 55% of the components purchased originate from the United States.

Menendez specifically cites protecting renters’ rights to using this technology, a segment of the population that is often excluded from traditional solar projects. A landlord could not prevent a tenant from purchasing and using a plug-in solar system if this legislation passed, with some exceptions in regards to spacing limitations.

Socket Full of Sunshine through the Environmental Protection Agency would also create a standard for estimating how much a plug-in solar system would save consumers and would establish consumer safety standards for these products.

“As America works to rebuild its domestic solar manufacturing industry, we need to make sure growing demand for solar energy also supports the workers and companies making these products here at home,” said Mike Carr, executive director of the Solar Energy Manufacturers for America Coalition, in a press release. “The Socket Full of Sunshine Act recognizes that expanding access to affordable solar energy and supporting American manufacturing should go hand in hand. By prioritizing American-made products and tying federal rebates to domestic manufacturing and content requirements, this legislation can help create new demand for U.S.-made solar products and strengthen our domestic supply chains.

These are just some of the reasons one might want to know whether a car is operating autonomously, but as of yet, there is no standardized messaging to suggest that a vehicle is doing so.

This week, NHTSA proposed an answer. In a draft proposal released Friday, it suggested a whole new color for vehicle lamps, cyan, to signal autonomous operation.

The proposal was actually first put forward by Mercedes, alongside its DRIVE PILOT level 3 driver assist system. This is the first true hands-free system offered in the US, which allows drivers to forgo responsibility for the car under certain conditions, and let the car truly drive itself. This differs from systems like Tesla’s Full Self-Driving, which is actually only a “level 2” system, where the driver is still responsible when the system is on.

We tried Mercedes’ system out in Santa Monica traffic, and were impressed at how quickly we adjusted to our temporary robotic chauffer.

When we saw that system, we also saw Mercedes’ proposal for lighting: cyan lights around the exterior of the vehicle, denoting autonomous operation.

Unfortunately our demo was on a bright sunny day (a necessity to operate the level 3 system, which doesn’t run at night or in bad weather), so the lights didn’t pop as much as they might have in lower light, but you get the drift.

Mercedes trialed the system in California for two years, and it seems the NHTSA is happy with the results, since it is proposing to take the idea national (after input from the SAE, which also likes the idea of using cyan).

The new draft regulation would allow for optional inclusion of cyan lights, but would not require them. So, it’s entirely possible that automakers would decide to save the small cost that it would take to engineer and include these lights.

But by putting the idea out into the regulations, it gets everyone thinking about a future where this color might be required (and I could see that requirement coming quite quickly) – so automakers are likely to start thinking of where these lights would go, and including them, since LEDs don’t really cost anything anyway.

The regulation states that the lights should turn on when the car is operating autonomously, and turn off when it isn’t – they should never be on when a driver is actively driving the vehicle. They should all turn on and off simultaneously, and they should be visible from any angle, with markers on the front, sides and rear, all at least 15 inches off the ground.

China just announced essentially the same regulation earlier this week. The country released a draft standard that would take effect in 2028, is optional, and uses turquoise as its proposed color. This, too, was a reaction to the proliferation of lights on autonomous vehicles in China, and the government has asked automakers to stop doing their own thing and instead follow the new standardized system.

The NHTSA’s draft regulation covers several other aspects of autonomous vehicle operation, mostly by updating Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards (FMVSS) rules to allow some flexibility for certain operations that won’t be used by autonomous vehicles.

For example, cars are required to have a turn signal that turns off automatically after the steering wheel turns beyond a certain threshold and then returns back to neutral. That wouldn’t apply to cars without steering wheels, so the updated rules delete that requirement for autonomous cars. It also changes certain headlight requirements for more design flexibility.

#5490. US Tech Leaders Are Urgently Calling for Rules on AI — China Already Has Them

Recent warnings from American AI leaders have created the impression that the world is only beginning to confront the dangers of advanced artificial intelligence.

Dario Amodei, chief executive of tech company Anthropic, has called for governments to intervene to slow the development of increasingly powerful AI models.

His proposal begins with independent evaluators embedded inside AI companies, moves on to regulation and progresses to government-backed coordination among democratic states.

Ultimately, it seeks global agreements with China on testing of AI systems, dangerous applications of the technology and the pace of AI development.

Yet there is a China-shaped hole in this narrative. The Asian superpower is portrayed as the difficult final participant in a governance system that the US and its allies must first create.

In fact, China has already established an extensive system of AI rules, assessment, filing, technical standards and government oversight. Here’s how China’s AI governance system has developed in stages alongside the technology itself.

Beijing’s 2022 rules on algorithmic recommendation services apply to technologies that select, rank, filter or recommend online content to users.

They compel companies that use algorithms to examine how they work, to protect the personal information of users, to give users certain controls, and to introduce safeguards for workers, consumers, children and older people.

China’s deep synthesis provisions, effective from January 2023, extended regulation to synthetic (or AI-generated) text, images, audio and video. AI companies must authenticate users, protect data, keep relevant records, assess their systems and identify synthetic material that could be mistaken for authentic content.

The interim measures for generative artificial intelligence services were introduced in August 2023. These added rules for public-facing generative AI, particularly those that can influence public opinion or persuade uses in other ways.

The measures apply to the legality and quality of training data, users’ personal information, intellectual property and discrimination based on protected characteristics. The rules also cover transparency by AI companies and the accuracy and reliability of products.

Under the rules, companies must address problems found in their services. These services must undergo a security assessment to examine their risks and compliance with Chinese legislation. The companies must also file information about their algorithms with Chinese authorities.

These mechanisms are not identical to the independent evaluators that would be embedded in AI companies, as has been proposed by Amodei. But they perform some of the same functions.

Beijing’s latest set of measures is called the AI safety governance framework 3.0, published in September 2026. It sets out a broad approach to identifying, grading and responding to risks throughout the lifecycle of AI systems.

Building on earlier versions, the framework covers AI model and data risks, vulnerabilities in open source models (those which can be freely modified by users), AI’s effects on employment and society and increasingly autonomous systems.

It includes a dedicated mechanism for managing risks associated with rapidly developing AI agents (systems that carry out tasks in response to instructions by a user).

The framework addresses some of the same dangers now generating alarm in Silicon Valley, including highly autonomous systems, the misuse of chemical, biological and nuclear technologies, and the possibility of humans losing control of advanced AI. It proposes continual safety evaluations, risk based safeguards, humans being in charge, and emergency intervention mechanisms.

An independent analysis by the US Carnegie Endowment for International Peace thinktank has described the framework as China’s attempt to “to advance frontier safety without the need for heavy handed regulatory interventions”.

None of this means that Chinese AI rules should be transplanted elsewhere. Its institutions, regulatory traditions and technological priorities are different.

Many functions now presented as urgent aspirations in the US, including evaluation, accountability for AI companies, risk classification for AI systems, regulatory filing, technical standards and government oversight are already embedded in the governance architecture of the world’s other AI superpower.

The US is free to conclude that a market-led system best serves its economic and strategic interests. The Trump administration has favoured a “minimally burdensome” national approach to AI and has sought to limit more demanding state-level regulation.

But a market-led model cannot mean privatising the rewards of AI while exporting its risks. US companies may capture the commercial gains, while governments elsewhere are left to investigate security incidents, protect public systems and absorb the wider costs when those technologies fail. The OpenAI agent that gained unauthorised access to Australia’s state healthcare service shows how AI risks are being exported.

If Washington rejects extensive controls before systems are released, the US needs a credible legal regime for assessing who is liable for damage caused by those AI models. Otherwise, countries dependent on foreign AI systems will face increasingly difficult choices about whose technology to trust, and on what terms.

Silicon Valley’s governing ethos has long been to “move fast and break things”. That was always a questionable principle for technologies woven deeply into public life. It is untenable for companies whose own leaders now warn that their products could cause catastrophic harm.

Beijing has already surrounded its AI sector with public obligations, regulatory oversight and an evolving framework of risk assessment. A global debate that treats such governance as a future aspiration should at least recognise that one country is already ahead of the curve.

Car and Driver just released a Testing Hub report comparing the older engine to the new one. Before we get to the results, it’s worth noting that the new LS6 gives the C8 Corvette an additional 40 hp and 50 lb-ft of torque. That means in base trim, it makes 530 hp and 520 lb-ft of torque compared to the LT2’s 490 hp and 465 lb-ft of torque.

To keep things as fair as possible, the test compared the 2020 Corvette 1LT’s results with the latest 2027 1LT. With a bigger displacement, the 2027 Corvette consumes a bit more fuel than the outgoing 6.2-liter. “Our business began by offering services to the pharmaceutical industry in the area of ​​validations,” Fonseca said. “Later, demand grew for services such as electricity, plumbing, refrigeration, appliances, and thermal studies. After Hurricane Maria, our electrical system collapsed and a huge demand for photovoltaic systems began.”

Over the years, Fonseca has installed all types and brands of battery systems. Most recently, Fonseca installed a unique solar system with Briggs & Stratton SimplePHI 6.6 batteries for a two-unit apartment building in Bayamón, in northern Puerto Rico.

“The area’s electrical system suffers from frequent outages, and for that reason alone, the photovoltaic system was installed. Since the structure occupies the entire plot, it was impossible to install a generator,” Fonseca said. “The client was already familiar with our company, having previously hired us to install a 30 kW three-phase backup system with 46 kW of batteries for his business.”

For the apartment building in Bayamon, what appears to be a triple battery system is actually three independent systems powering three different locations. Each of the two apartments has a SimpliPHI 6.6 triple battery system. Additionally, a SimpliPHI 6.6 battery powers the four electric gates that control access to the building’s parking area and lighting in the common areas. It’s an ingenious design that not only looks good but also makes efficient use of space.

“The systems operate independently, they don’t communicate with each other, and there are no backup generators,” Fonseca said. “Each apartment has eighteen 550-W solar panels with 19.8 kWh of Briggs & Stratton batteries. The building operated in self-consumption mode for five months after installing the three systems, as we were waiting for electrical approval from Luma Energy.”

Each electrical system in an apartment places a considerable load on the system, as it supports an air conditioner, a heater, a stove, lights and all the other electrical components that a typical home has.

“The system works wonderfully,” Fonseca said. “They’ve never run out of electricity. If there’s plenty of sun, the batteries charge in 75 minutes with the panels. If there isn’t as much sun, it takes a maximum of 150 minutes. With the meters installed and the electrical certification, they’ll be able to start exporting energy to the grid.”

Fonseca has worked with Briggs & Stratton for more than eight years and has completed approximately a dozen installations with the company’s SimpliPHI 6.6 battery.

“SimpliPHI batteries are reliable, long-lasting, and fast-charging. I’ve never had any problems with them,” he said. “They’re smaller than Tesla batteries, so they take up less space, they’re lighter and easier to handle. Best of all, they’re easy to install, and now that the new controller can be connected from either side, it’s even more convenient.”

Fonseca isn’t the only one who notices the advantages of Briggs & Stratton. Recently, a customer who runs three air conditioners at night commented that he hasn’t had to use a backup generator because the SimpliPHI batteries handle the entire load.

“The only battery systems I install are from Briggs & Stratton,” Fonseca said. “I frequently receive calls about other products that aren’t working, reporting malfunctions, so I’ve seen all the problems other battery and inverter manufacturers have: they don’t last more than a few years and fail in various ways. #5469. California is still without a functioning community solar program

California, the state with the most installed solar capacity in the country, has spent years trying, but still doesn’t have a community solar program. That means residential solar and storage systems are often still only accessible by the affluent, and low-income residents and renters continue to experience the country’s second-highest electricity rates without any self-generated relief.

A community solar project in Colorado, developed by Pivot Energy.

Despite the California Legislature mandating that the state create a community solar program in 2022, nothing yet has been established. Solar and community advocates have been battling with the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) and investor-owned utilities to establish a working community solar market, but talks have reached a stalemate. Outgoing Gov. Gavin Newsom vetoed the latest community solar legislation in September, and the state continues to lose out on this new solar market development.

In 2022, Newsom signed AB 2316 into law, which aimed to create a community renewable energy program that would support both solar and storage projects. The bill required large utilities serving more than 100,000 customers to create and implement programs that “enable ratepayers to participate directly in offsite electrical generation facilities that use eligible renewable energy resources.”

In 2024, the CPUC rejected a proposal from solar advocacy groups that would support community solar in the state, instead putting more decision-making power into local utility hands. State legislators then attempted in 2025 to pass a new bill (AB 1260) designed to strengthen and expand the initial framework for California’s community solar-plus-storage program, guaranteeing affordability to low-income customers, among other stipulations. AB 1260 was never fully adopted.

Instead, community and solar advocates threw their support behind AB 1813, the Community Renewable Energy Program Act. Groups like Californians for Local, Affordable Solar and Storage (CLASS) said AB 1813 was a workable community solar and storage program that could overcome the CPUC’s “built-to-fail” program outline.

California Gov. Gavin Newsom Photo credit: Gage Skidmore

The California Legislature passed AB 1813, which directed regulators to value community solar and storage using the CPUC’s own Avoided Cost Calculator, an existing tool the state has declined to apply, and required paired battery storage so projects deliver power when the grid needs it most. Ultimately, Newsom vetoed the bill in September, and community solar development in California is still stalled.

“With Gov. Newsom’s veto of AB 1813, California continues to fall further behind other states who are aggressively moving forward with successful community solar programs. Unfortunately, the voices of community leaders, environmental justice organizations, ratepayer advocates, labor, community choice aggregators, the building industry and so many others were denied a solution they have long fought for,” said Derek Chernow, executive director of CLASS. “Study after study demonstrates the benefits of community solar-plus-storage to households, businesses, our environment and the grid. We are committed to working with the next administration to make community solar and storage part of the solution to California’s growing affordability crisis.”

Newsom, whose gubernatorial term ends in January 2027, stated in his veto that AB 1813 “would effectively require an administratively set price for the generation of power that exceeds the value that generation provides to the grid. This would result in significant upward pressure on consumer energy bills without delivering reliable, high-performance capacity to the grid at the times we need it. #5460. Trump’s DOE Budget Request Cuts Weak Bets And Useful Energy Research Together

There are Department of Energy programs in the Trump administration’s FY2027 budget request that I would cut too. Hydrogen for buses, trucks, building heat and routine electricity storage has consumed years of public funding and attention without overcoming the basic efficiency and cost advantages of using electricity directly. Ending more attempts to force those applications into existence would be progress. The problem is that the administration has wrapped those defensible cuts into a much broader retreat from batteries, electric vehicles, buildings, solar, wind and public energy research while treating several much weaker technological bets far more gently.

Across thirteen selected DOE technology program lines, FY2026 enacted funding totals about $4.288 billion. Trump’s FY2027 request reduces that to $2.618 billion, a decline of roughly 39%. The distribution matters much more than the aggregate. Solar, wind, vehicles and buildings together fall from $1.005 billion to only $40 million, about a 96% reduction. These are selected annual DOE programs, not the entire department or every federal dollar that supports the technologies, and the FY2027 figures are a presidential request rather than enacted appropriations. But budget requests tell Congress what an administration thinks deserves public support, and the pattern is difficult to mistake.

The useful way to judge these programs is not to divide technologies into politically favored and politically disfavored camps. Research, demonstrations and deployment support should face different tests. Early-stage research should answer a credible question that is worth answering. A demonstration should resolve specific technical, operating or commercial uncertainties. Deployment funding should accelerate something with a plausible path to delivering useful services competitively. A technology can justify a bounded experiment while being a terrible candidate for a billion-dollar rollout, and commercial success in a sector does not mean there are no remaining public-interest research questions.

Solar and wind illustrate the latter point. Their DOE program lines go from $220 million and $100 million respectively to zero in the FY2027 request. Neither technology requires the federal government to prove that it can generate electricity at commercial scale. That argument was settled years ago. The remaining public questions concern durability, manufacturing, recycling, materials, grid integration and operating electricity systems with very large shares of variable generation. Firms should fund ordinary product competition, but shared knowledge and system-level research often produce benefits that no single company can capture.

Vehicles fall from $397 million to $20 million, while buildings fall from $288 million to $20 million. That is especially difficult to square with the actual transition. Battery-electric vehicles have already won substantial passenger-vehicle markets and are spreading rapidly into commercial-duty niches where economics align. Heat pumps are a mature technology whose remaining problems increasingly concern installation quality, controls, building integration, flexible demand and how to electrify existing buildings without making unnecessary renovation a prerequisite. Those are exactly the kinds of practical problems that public research can help solve across an industry.

Hydrogen is a different case. The annual hydrogen and fuel-cell line falls from $160 million to zero, and much of its energy mission deserves to disappear. Producing hydrogen, conditioning it, moving it and converting it back into useful work generally adds energy losses, equipment and cost compared with direct electrification. Necessary industrial hydrogen is different because ammonia, methanol and other chemical processes require the molecule itself. A sensible federal portfolio would focus its hydrogen expertise on reducing emissions from hydrogen that industry actually needs while ending serial attempts to create a giant new hydrogen market for applications where electricity already has the advantage.

That does not mean the much larger regional hydrogen hubs have simply been cancelled. The hubs are multiyear awards funded separately from the annual technology-program comparison, and five remained nominally retained after the administration’s review. Retention, however, is not the same thing as a functioning project. Reporting from Pennsylvania continues to describe uncertainty, cautious partners and little evidence of meaningful momentum, while the FY2027 request proposes clawing back unobligated clean-hydrogen funding. The result is less a coherent technology decision than a prolonged “will they or won’t they?” process that makes planning harder for good projects and bad projects alike.

The mixed treatment of other technologies reinforces the point. Critical-minerals funding rises sharply, from $83 million to $364 million, and some of that can be useful if it builds processing, refining and qualified industrial output rather than merely subsidizing extraction. Geothermal remains at $150 million, including substantial support for enhanced-geothermal work whose eventual electricity economics remain uncertain. Civilian nuclear falls only about 9%, from $1.685 billion to $1.534 billion. Existing-fleet work and bounded research can have value without accepting the proposition that new nuclear or SMRs are likely to become the fast, inexpensive backbone of the transition.

The cement awards show why program management matters as much as headline appropriations. Brimstone’s large demonstration award was reinstated while Sublime’s remained unresolved in the October award snapshot. Neither administrative status tells us whether the underlying technology will succeed. Brimstone still has to prove the economics of a rock refinery whose business case depends on several saleable outputs, while Sublime has its own feedstock, energy, qualification and cost questions to answer. A demonstration should purchase evidence about those uncertainties. Reinstatement is not commercial validation, and cancellation is not scientific falsification.

ARPA-E makes the institutional problem clearest. Its total allocation falls from $350 million to $200.3 million, while project funding drops from $310 million to $160.3 million. The purpose of an organization like ARPA-E is not to select a portfolio in which every experiment becomes a company. Some experiments should fail. Some should reveal that an attractive concept does not scale. Some should create knowledge that private firms subsequently exploit. The public value comes from asking useful questions at stages where private capital cannot capture enough of the upside to justify carrying all of the uncertainty.

That requires competent people inside government who can distinguish research from demonstration, negotiate milestones, inspect projects, interpret evidence and stop work that is no longer justified. Hollowing out that capability while cancelling and reinstating awards through broad political sweeps weakens both sides of the process. It becomes harder to kill bad projects intelligently and harder to protect good experiments from indiscriminate cuts.

I would keep the cuts to weak hydrogen-for-energy applications, narrow several other portfolios whose commercial claims have outrun their evidence, and impose much harder tests on new nuclear and speculative geothermal electricity ambitions. I would restore serious capacity around electrification, buildings, industrial conversion, batteries, renewable generation and useful shared research, while supporting mineral processing where it removes real industrial bottlenecks. That is not a defense of every clean-energy grant. It is an argument for judging technologies by the useful work they can perform and judging public programs by the evidence they buy.

A stopped clock can cancel something that deserved to go. It still cannot decide what the United States should build next.

It does this by having its own battery and motors inside the trailer, meaning the tractor doesn’t have to work nearly as hard as the trailer can effectively pull its own weight. Even though the tractor still runs on diesel, it uses a whole lot less of it.

This differs from the other popular option for fleets: full electrification. There are a number of electric semi tractors available, and fleets have been moving to install them, getting their depots ready for charging. But Range lets you bring your own tractor, and electrifies the trailer instead.

Range previously published some early findings, showing a 36% reduction in fuel use in a testing scenario. It also did a pilot program with an egg farm in California, which improved the fuel efficiency of the farm’s diesel fleet by 50-70%.

Now Range has published its own white paper showing comparative savings that fleets can achieve with either its own solution, the eTrailer, or Tesla’s solution, the fully electric Tesla Semi. The paper does not cover the various other electric semi tractors available on the market today, just a face-off between Range and Tesla.

The analysis goes through several scenarios, mostly focusing on refrigerated trailer units (“reefers”), which is the first product Range is focusing on.

Trailer electrification is a great idea for reefers, because they usually fill up on volume rather than weight (called “cubing out”), meaning battery weight doesn’t eat into maximum trailer payload. Batteries also offer power to run refrigeration, meaning you don’t need to have a diesel generator on the trailer.

The upshot of the whole thing is that: fleets can save money with the Range eTrailer, and can theoretically save more money with the Tesla Semi, but the numbers are relatively close in the most common scenario, and the Tesla scenarios have a much wider spread depending on what implementation you consider.

For example, if you only use public chargers rather than your own depot, the Tesla Semi will be more costly because energy will be more expensive to your fleet than if you bought it at commercial utility rates. So, even though installing chargers in your depot sounds expensive, it will pencil out better in the end.

The best money savings came from having two Semis share the same charger, but that requires tight timings and overnight driving.

Another important feature Range points out is build time for a project. The nice thing about the eTrailer is it can slot into fleets immediately with little operational change required, whereas the larger charging needs of an electric tractor) tend to require bigger projects to get a depot ready with chargers (but despite the higher upfront costs, you get better long-term savings, since you avoid diesel entirely with an electric tractor).

In the end, Range found that its solution saves a little more money than the Tesla Semi does in most scenarios, but the difference is quite narrow, with each option having its strengths and weaknesses. Both solutions are better than diesel in most scenarios, but not all of them.

You don’t necessarily need to choose between the two, as an electrified trailer can still be used with an electric tractor. It works the same way in either case – using its own motors to take some of the effort off the tractor. This means extended range, for longer hauls with light loads that couldn’t quite make it on the Tesla Semi’s inherent range. And the benefit of being able to run the reefer while disconnected from the tractor or shore power remains as well.

The analysis has plenty of limitations. It has to estimate future diesel costs (it used a number below current prices, but still historically high), didn’t cover financing costs, it doesn’t have real-world data for the Tesla Semi, didn’t cover electrified tractor or trailer options from other companies, can’t analyze individual charging costs site-to-site since each is specific to a utility company, didn’t cover the myriad incentives available in various regions, and most of all it didn’t consider diesel emissions, which are a huge societal cost to everyone that electrification reduces (and full electrification reduces more).

So in the end, every fleet has a different use case, and should run the numbers themselves to see how either solution would fit in. But in the generalized case, it looks like there are plenty of savings available out there, whether through tractor or trailer electrification.

#5447. Germany’s 300-Meter Wind Tower Faces a 199-Meter Competitor

Germany’s GICON has completed one of the more remarkable wind-turbine construction projects in recent years. Its Schipkau prototype reached a hub height of 300 meters in September, putting the upper blade tip at a reported 365 meters. The lattice structure and telescoping inner tower allow the turbine assembly to be raised into place without a conventional crane lifting it to that extreme height. The engineering achievement is substantial, and the attraction of stronger winds higher above the ground is easy to understand.

But while GICON was developing its tower, conventional wind towers were getting taller too. Max Bögl recently erected a Vestas V172-7.2 MW with a 199-meter hub on a series-production hybrid tower. That changes the commercial comparison considerably. The generation advantage of reaching 300 meters looks impressive against a conventional tower at 100 or 150 meters. It becomes a different proposition when developers can already choose a hub approaching 200 meters.

I modeled two hypothetical wind farms using identical 7.2 MW turbines, rotor diameters and turbine counts, changing only their hub heights from 199 to 300 meters. Under the screening model’s wind assumptions, the taller farm produces 11.4% more electricity annually. That’s valuable additional output from the same generating equipment, although the gain varies considerably with the site’s vertical wind profile. Measurements at Schipkau suggest that some locations could do substantially better.

The construction question is less straightforward. GICON’s approach addresses some of the transport and lifting problems associated with very tall conventional towers, but a giant lattice structure has a different assembly burden. The prototype required thousands of components and extensive work at height. The published construction record also includes an interruption for steel-quality checks. Future towers should benefit from the experience, although repeat construction costs and schedules have yet to be established.

That timing matters. Turbines begin earning as they enter service, while construction costs arrive much earlier. My assessment models both farms’ progressive commissioning, additional electricity, field labor and discounted receipts using a provisional reference-class construction allowance. The results show how strongly the commercial case depends on repeatable installation performance, with important costs still requiring supplier quotations.

There will be sites where reaching stronger winds at 300 meters is attractive, particularly where existing permits, land access or grid connections make additional production valuable. Schipkau has established that an extreme-height tower can be built. Whether the approach becomes commercially competitive depends on what subsequent installations achieve.

#5422. Airplanes Got ABS Brakes In The '40s — Production Cars Didn't Get Them Until The 1960s

Anti-lock braking systems are one of the most important safety innovations in modern cars. They play a huge roll in the reduction of stopping distances, and they have been legally required as standard equipment on all new cars sold in the United States since Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 126 became law in 2013. The massively beneficial technology has been on cars since 1966, but the idea of anti-skid brakes was already around for about two decades prior to its first automotive implementation.

As airplanes became jet-powered and grew faster and more powerful during the aviation boom of World War II, the tasks of safely landing and stopping those planes became more challenging. The Boeing B-47 Stratojet was the first mass-produced aircraft to ever employ an ABS system in 1947 using a system called Hydro-Aire Hytrol ABS, and Dunlop introduced its Maxaret ABS system in 1952.

Mercedes-Benz, known for its various safety innovations, is widely credited with producing the first mass production car with anti-lock brakes, but according to Hagerty, the first car to ever come fitted with such a system was actually the ultra-low production Jensen FF that was built from 1966 to 1971. It used an adaptation of Dunlop's Maxaret ABS system, but only 320 Jensen FFs were ever built, and none were officially exported to the U.S. The first application of ABS in a mass-production car eventually came from a U.S. manufacturer.

For the most part, credit goes to the 1971 Chrysler Imperial and its optional Sure-Brake system as being the first production car to offer an anti-lock braking system, since it prevented all four of the Imperial's wheels from locking up under panic braking. It was, however, what's called a three-channel anti-lock braking system, which means it used one brake-pressure modulator for each front wheel, and a single modulator to control brake pressure for both rear brakes.

Popular Science put a Sure-Brake-equipped Imperial to the test in 1970, and found the system to reliably prevent wheel lockup, even on ice. Road Test magazine tested a Sure-Brake-equipped Imperial in 1971, and noted how alarming the rapid clunking sound caused by the system's pressure actuation was, and complained about how it may further startle a panicked driver in an emergency situation, but again, the system prevented lockup. Sadly, Chrysler offered the system as an extra-cost option on 1971-to-1973 model year Imperials, but less than 5% of buyers opted for the system, so Chrysler canned it for 1974.

Though Chrysler's Sure-Brake was a computerized system, Mercedes and Bosch were the first to introduce a digital controller and implement the use of digital microelectronics in the 1978 S-Class, which paved the way for modern ABS. There are claims that the 1978 Mercedes-Benz S-Class was the first car to implement ABS, but to phrase it most accurately, it was the first four-wheel multi-channel anti-lock brake system to use digital microelectronics.

From their early roots stopping jets in short order, to safely stopping Jensen FFs, Chrysler Imperials, Mercedes-Benz S-Classes, and now every new car sold in the U.S., anti-lock braking systems have left a mark on transportation safety history. The technology has also paved the way for other pivotal safety innovations like electronic stability control (ESC), as well as performance and off-road features like torque vectoring and brake-based LSDs.

#5421. New Report Keeps Tabs on ACC’s Utility Bias that Ends up Hurting Wallets

The report also examines the ACC’s role in Arizona’s gas rush, as well as its ethical shortcomings.

It comes as the Commission considers a new rate increase for Arizona Public Service (APS), a new rate increase for Tucson Electric Power (TEP), and mechanisms that would allow both utilities to increase rates every year with less public engagement and stakeholder input opportunities.

Arizona has some of the highest electric bills in the country, with some of the most planned gas in the country. Despite this, the Commission has steadily increased ratepayer bills, making choices that facilitate expensive and polluting fossil gas operations and cutting programs that require utilities to conserve energy, lower electric bills, and invest in energy efficiency.

The report also makes an assessment of the Commission’s ethical shortcomings. Just in the last two weeks, additional accusations have surfaced of the Commission’s proximity to the companies they’re meant to regulate after in 2023 a Commissioner met with the very institutions that invest in utilities. Since the report’s publication, a Commissioner has also been called on to step away from ongoing cases considering rate increases by the Attorney General’s Office.
