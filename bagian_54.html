#718. Resignation of Bärbel Bas shines light on harsh era of German politics

German Labor Minister Bärbel Bas has stepped down, citing her need to grieve a loved one and leave behind the harsh realities of government. So how did German politics get so heated?

For a brief moment, a sense of quiet sympathy could be felt in Berlin's often unforgiving political arena: On Monday morning, Bärbel Bas, Germany's labor minister and the co-leader of the center-left Social Democrats (SPD) announced her resignation from all government and party positions. The 58-year-old politician cited a personal tragedy as the reason for her decision: "Following the death of my stepdaughter, I have come to realize that I need time to work through my grief," Bas wrote in a statement.

Bas also wrote that sustained criticism from the conservative governing parties, the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU), had contributed to her decision to step down. In her statement, she wrote that she had been forced to "conclude that public attacks on me, unfortunately also from within the ranks of our coalition partner, continued to intensify during this personally difficult time."

The labor minister had recently been responsible for implementing complex changes to Germany's public pension system — including increasing the retirement age — and had been urged by some conservative politicians, in rather harsh terms, not to delay the project any further.

According to a report in the Süddeutsche Zeitung newspaper, one remark in particular appears to have weighed heavily on her: During an off-the-record briefing with journalists, Chancellor Friedrich Merz, of the CDU, reportedly criticized the pace of her efforts despite knowing that she was working through a personal tragedy.

Did Bas feel so overwhelmed by pressures both personal and political that she chose to step down rather than continue to endure them?

Mirco Liefke, a researcher with the Institute for Media and Communication Studies in the Department of Political and Social Sciences at the Freie Universität Berlin, told DW that unfairness and harshness have always been part of politics. However, he added: "What I do think is new is the potential for escalation and the speed that social media have brought to public debate. Along with that has come an incentive for individual actors to adopt ever more radical positions."

There are few public spaces left in which politicians can speak with the expectation that their remarks will not be reported. Merz is said to have made critical remarks of Bas during an off-the-record briefing whose contents were not intended for publication.

Though he was fully aware of her personal circumstances, Merz reportedly said Bas was barely visible in public and failing to put forward proposals of her own.

"It has effectively become a business model to leak information that is meant to remain confidential and thereby generate attention," Liefke said.

A spokesperson for Merz declined to confirm or deny the report.

Felix Banaszak, the co-leader of Germany's center-left Greens, has known Bas fior years, with both hailing from Duisburg, in the state of North Rhine-Westphalia, albeit born a couple of decades apart.

"Toughness and ruthlessness are nothing new in politics," Banaszak told DW. "But we still need to find a different way of doing things if we do not want to keep wearing people down."

Banaszak said the SPD was partially responsible for the treatment of Bas. "The fact that Bärbel Bas encountered so little understanding for her personal situation from within the ranks of the coalition partner, and that apparently no one in the SPD was willing to step forward, defend her, and take her out of the line of fire, is simply not a good sign," he said.

"It would be naive to pretend that the attacks on a man would have unfolded in a similar way," he added.

Liefke offered a similar view: "Women politicians are subjected to an extraordinary degree of misogynistic and sexualized abuse — and they face a level of public exposure that is entirely new."

The debate over how Bas and her grief should have been treated soon took on some of the same characteristics as the attacks that had preceded her resignation. Manuela Schwesig, the SPD state premier of the northeastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, described the attacks by the CDU/CSU as "inhuman," drawing sharp criticism from many conservative politicians. Merz publicly expressed his regret over the labor minister's resignation and wished her all the best.

Liefke said this was another tremnd in German politics. Once a politician decides to step down, he argues, public expressions of concern are often far more intense than they used to be and come from all sides: "This is a facade of concern," he said. "There is something ritualistic, almost reflexive, about it. People react this way because they are following another major trend: Emotionalization."

The attacks on Bas are just one example of the increasingly harsh tone of political debate. Formal reprimands in the Bundestag have risen sharply, particularly since the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) entered parliament in 2017. High-profile resignations have also made headlines, including that of Kevin Kühnert, the SPD's former secretary-general.

Kühnert stepped down in 2024. In April 2025, however, he told Die Zeit newspaper that he had felt constantly threatened by far-right extremists. Political debate, he said, is showing "an increasing lack of respect for the views of those who think differently."

Liefke said politicians once managed to maintain two distinct modes of communication: one public and one private. "When you think of earlier generations of politicians, what comes to mind is the distance they maintained, the relative lack of emotional display," he said. "Take Helmut Schmidt, for example. He did not openly grieve over things that came crashing down on him. At least, he did not show it publicly." Schmidt, of the SPD, served as chancellor from 1974 to 1982.

"Today, politicians such as Bärbel Bas benefit from drawing on their own life stories when they say, 'I come from a working-class background and worked my way up,'" Liefke said. "That can work to their advantage — but it also makes them far more visible, and far more vulnerable, as individuals."

Bas, who served as president of the Bundestag from 2021 to 2025, intends to retain her parliamentary seat. Life is likely to become quieter for her now as other politicians move into the spotlight, both in public and, perhaps, in private as well.

#715. Nearly 3,000 exploding stars challenge our understanding of the Universe

The largest collection of exploding white dwarf stars ever compiled is providing fresh evidence that dark energy, the mysterious force responsible for the Universe’s accelerating expansion, may change over time. The findings challenge a central assumption of modern cosmology and could help scientists better understand how the Universe evolves.

An international research team led by scientists at the University of Queensland’s School of Mathematics and Physics brought together observations of 2,884 Type Ia supernovae. These powerful stellar explosions serve as important tools for measuring distances across the Universe, allowing astronomers to trace its expansion history.

PhD candidate Ryan Camilleri said the project establishes a new international standard for supernova cosmology. By bringing decades of measurements into a unified dataset, researchers have created their most detailed picture yet of how cosmic expansion has changed over time.

“We’ve rebuilt 3 decades of astronomical observations into a single, consistent framework,” Mr. Camilleri said.

“We combined our data with other cosmic measurements including relic light from the Big Bang and maps of how galaxies are distributed through space.

“Instead of confirming the standard model of cosmology which assumes dark energy is fixed and unchanging, we have more evidence that dark energy may change over time.”

The new collection brings together historical supernova measurements and findings from the Dark Energy Survey (DES) published in 2024.

To make observations collected over different periods directly comparable, the scientists revisited older data using updated analytical methods. Advances in understanding supernova behavior allowed the team to improve the consistency and accuracy of measurements gathered over decades.

“Over the years we’ve learned a lot more about how supernovae behave so we’ve been able to go back and apply that improved understanding to older data,” Mr. Camilleri said.

The researchers also had to account for differences among the telescopes used to collect the observations. Other factors, including cosmic dust and the masses of galaxies, can influence the light astronomers receive from distant supernovae and therefore affect distance measurements.

“Extensive work has been done to link observations from different telescopes with different capacities and take into account issues such as cosmic dust and galaxy mass which can affect the light coming from a supernova.

“We also incorporated more subtle effects such as gravitational lensing, which is the bending and magnification of light around large objects as it travels from a supernova to Earth.”

Astrophysicist Professor Tamara Davis said the expanded dataset represents an important advance in efforts to understand the nature of dark energy.

Under the standard model of cosmology, dark energy is treated as a constant property of the Universe. However, recent observations have raised questions about whether that assumption fully explains how cosmic expansion has evolved.

“Our supernova data from DES in 2024 first showed hints that dark energy may be time varying, and this new compilation also sees a deviation from the standard model although in a slightly different direction,” Professor Davis said.

The findings are especially interesting because a separate astronomical project has also produced evidence suggesting that dark energy might not remain constant.

“Similarly results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) found hints of variations in dark energy in its surveys of relic sound waves from the early Universe.

“So, 2 completely independent measurements have found hints of time variation in dark energy, challenging the standard model that dark energy doesn't change.

Although the different measurements do not point to exactly the same departure from the standard model, they provide additional reasons to investigate whether dark energy changes as the Universe ages.

Understanding dark energy could have implications extending far beyond the expansion of the Universe. Researchers believe that learning more about its behavior might also provide insights into one of the greatest unresolved problems in theoretical physics: reconciling gravity with quantum physics.

“All of this research may also hold the clue to explain how gravity and quantum physics fit together.

“We know these 2 theories are each immensely successful in their own realms, so if we can figure out how to put them together that would be a huge step in theoretical physics.”

The researchers plan to expand the dataset as additional observations become available. One important source will be the Dark Energy Bedrock All-Sky Supernova program (DEBASS), which is identifying hundreds of supernovae at distances closer to Earth than those surveyed by DES.

Incorporating these nearby explosions will provide further measurements that scientists can use to investigate the Universe’s expansion and test whether dark energy truly changes over time.

The cosmology findings are available on arXiv here, while the accompanying measurements of host galaxy masses can be found on arXiv here.

The new results build on decades of work by hundreds of astronomers and researchers worldwide. Contributing projects include the Dark Energy Survey, Foundation Supernova Survey, PanStarrs I, Sloan Digital Sky Survey, Supernova Legacy Survey, Carnegie Supernova Project, and the Center for Astrophysics supernova survey.

#712. Kwame Brathwaite, photographer of 'Black is Beautiful' movement, dies at 85

Editor’s Note:  This article was originally published by The Art Newspaper, an editorial partner of CNN Style.

Kwame Brathwaite, the pioneering activist and photographer whose work helped define the aesthetics of the “Black is Beautiful” movement of the 1960s and beyond, died on April 1, aged 85.

His son, Kwame Brathwaite, Jr, announced his father’s death in an Instagram post that read in part, “I am deeply saddened to share that my Baba, the patriarch of our family, our rock and my hero has transitioned.”

Brathwaite’s work has been the subject of resurgent interest from curators, historians and collectors in recent years, and his first major institutional retrospective, which was organized by the Aperture Foundation, made its debut in 2019 at the Skirball Cultural Center in Los Angeles before touring the country.

Brathwaite was born in 1938 to Barbadian immigrants, in what he referred to as “the People’s Republic of Brooklyn” in New York, though his family moved from there to Harlem and then to the South Bronx when Brathwaite was 5 years old. He attended the School of Industrial Art (now the High School of Art and Design) and, according to profiles of Brathwaite in T Magazine and Vice, was drawn to photography by two moments. The first was in August of 1955, when a 17-year-old Brathwaite encountered David Jackson’s haunting photograph of a brutalized Emmett Till in his open casket. The second was in 1956, when — after he and his brother Elombe co-founded the African Jazz Arts Society and Studios (AJASS) — Brathwaite saw a young man taking photos in a dark jazz club without the use of a flash, and his mind became alight with possibility.

Using a Hasselblad medium-format camera, Brathwaite attempted to do the same, learning to work with limited light in a manner that enhanced the visual narrative of his imagery. He would soon also develop a darkroom technique that enriched and deepened how Black skin would appear in his photography, honing the practice in a small darkroom in his Harlem apartment. Brathwaite went on to photograph jazz legends performing throughout the 1950s and ’60s, including Miles Davis, John Coltrane, Thelonious Monk and others.

“You want to get the feeling, the mood that you’re experiencing when they’re playing,” Brathwaite told Aperture Magazine in 2017. “That’s the thing. You want to capture that.”

A 106-year-old from the Philippines is Vogue's oldest ever cover model

By the early 1960s, alongside the rest of AJASS, Brathwaite began using his photography and organizing prowess to consciously push back against whitewashed, Eurocentric beauty standards. The group came up with the concept of the Grandassa Models, young Black women whom Brathwaite would photograph, celebrating and accentuating their features. In 1962, AJASS organized “Naturally ’62”, a fashion show held in a Harlem club called the Purple Manor and featuring the models. The show would go on to be held regularly until 1992. In 1966, Brathwaite married his wife Sikolo, a Grandassa Model whom he had met on the street the year prior when he asked if he could take her portrait. The two remained married for the rest of Brathwaite’s life.

By the 1970s, Brathwaite’s focus on jazz shifted to other forms of popular Black music. In 1974, he traveled to Africa with the Jackson Five to document their tour, also photographing the historic “Rumble in the Jungle” boxing match between Muhammad Ali and George Foreman in what’s now the Democratic Republic of Congo that same year. Commissions in this era also saw Brathwaite photographing Nina Simone, Stevie Wonder, Sly and the Family Stone, Bob Marley and other music legends.

Creating for community: The Black designers placing identity and heritage at the center of their brands

#704. Oxford scientists discover how viruses build themselves without a blueprint

Researchers at the University of Oxford have observed virus-like particles assembling themselves one molecule at a time, uncovering the surprisingly simple rules that allow complex biological structures to form with remarkable reliability. The findings, published in Nature, offer a detailed look at a process that scientists have struggled to observe directly for decades.

Understanding how viruses construct their protective protein shells, and how that construction might be interrupted, could lead to new antiviral drugs, improved vaccines, and other medical treatments. The discovery helps explain how individual proteins consistently organize themselves into the correct structures despite having thousands of possible ways to connect.

The researchers found that viral assembly resembles navigating a complicated maze, where only certain paths lead to lasting progress. At first, protein components form weak connections that can easily break apart if the arrangement is unsuccessful. This flexibility allows the molecules to keep trying different combinations until they form particular closed structures. Once multiple connections hold these structures together, they become far more stable.

These stable arrangements serve as molecular checkpoints, narrowing the enormous number of possible assembly routes into a much smaller set of successful pathways. One especially important milestone occurs when five larger protein building blocks connect to form a closed pentagonal ring. This is the first highly stable structure in the assembly process. Once it forms, each subsequent stable stage requires fewer additional building blocks, allowing the particle to grow more quickly.

Co-lead author Dr. Roi Asor (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) explained: "A virus has to solve an extraordinary construction problem. Its components somehow have to find the right arrangement among a huge number of possibilities, without a blueprint or machinery directing the process. We can now watch what happens molecule by molecule and see the physical rules that make it possible."

Watching Virus Particles Assemble Molecule by Molecule

Viruses store and protect their genetic material within organized protein shells known as capsids. Depending on the virus, these shells may consist of tens or even thousands of individual protein components. Remarkably, in many cases, the proteins can arrange themselves into the correct structure without any external machinery directing their assembly.

Although scientists have investigated viral self-assembly for decades, capturing the process as it unfolds has been a major challenge. Many of the intermediate structures exist only briefly and appear in very small quantities. As a result, traditional methods have generally allowed researchers to examine the starting materials and finished particles without clearly showing how one transforms into the other.

To overcome this limitation, the Oxford researchers studied an engineered virus-like particle composed of 60 protein units. They used mass photometry, a technique developed at Oxford that determines the mass of individual molecules by measuring the light they scatter. By combining this technology with a new approach for keeping individual molecules confined and continuously observable, the team could repeatedly measure the mass of a growing particle as additional proteins attached.

This approach allowed the scientists to follow the construction process as it happened, effectively tracking the addition of individual molecular building blocks over time.

Co-first author PhD student Dan Loewenthal (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) said: "This method lets us study the assembly process directly, we just take a video!"

Co-lead author Professor Philipp Kukura (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, Oxford University) said: "Until now, much of our understanding of how these structures assemble has had to be reconstructed from snapshots or theoretical models. Being able to both quantify the underlying interactions and follow one particle as it grows changes that. We can see the important intermediate structures appear and deduce a detailed model of the construction process."

The Surprising Trick That Makes Viral Assembly Work

The results help explain how a process involving an overwhelming number of possible molecular arrangements can consistently produce the correct structure. Rather than exploring every possible route, the proteins progress through a relatively small number of stable intermediate stages that guide assembly toward completion.

The findings also clarify why the earliest stage of construction, called nucleation, is particularly difficult and why growth becomes substantially faster after the first stable structure appears.

A key part of this efficiency comes from the ability of protein building blocks to make mistakes without permanently disrupting the process. Weak initial connections allow incorrect combinations to separate, giving the proteins another opportunity to find a better arrangement. When enough components eventually connect in the correct closed configuration, the resulting structure becomes stable enough to support further growth.

Dr. Asor added: "The weak interactions give the system room to make mistakes. Most encounters don't have to be successful: the components can separate and try again. But once enough of them come together in the right closed arrangement, the structure becomes stable, and assembly can move forward. That combination of trial and error followed by locking in successful structures is what makes the process so reliable."

New Possibilities for Antiviral Drugs and Vaccines

By identifying the molecular interactions that control viral assembly, scientists may eventually be able to develop treatments that interfere with the construction of viral protein shells. The same insights could also support efforts to engineer virus-like particles for vaccines and other therapeutic applications.

Co-author Dr. Jack Tan (MRC Weatherall Institute of Molecular Medicine and CAMS-Oxford Institute) noted: "The ability to understand these processes at the molecular level could have important applications in vaccine development and antivirals that disrupt viral assembly."

The implications may extend far beyond viruses. Self-assembly is essential throughout biology, helping create everything from the internal skeletons of cells to specialized molecular compartments and large protein complexes.

By making it possible to observe these intricate construction processes one molecule at a time, the Oxford team's approach offers researchers a powerful new way to investigate how complex biological structures emerge from relatively simple interactions.

On October 24, 1946, a V-2 rocket captured the first-ever photo of Earth from space.

While these grainy, black-and-white images might not look like much today, they were a huge deal at the time, because no one had ever seen Earth from space before.

The V-2 rocket launched from the White Sands Missile Range in New Mexico. It carried a 35-millimeter motion picture camera that captured a new frame every second and a half. The rocket soared to an altitude of about 65 miles before falling back to Earth.

#696. Birds are more afraid of women than men. Scientists have no idea why

Birds living in cities appear to react differently to men and women, and scientists are struggling to explain why. An international research team discovered that urban birds, including great tits, house sparrows, and blackbirds, tend to fly away sooner when approached by women than by men. The surprising behavior raises new questions about how birds perceive the people around them.

The study took place in five European countries, where male and female participants (matched for height and clothing) walked directly toward birds in city parks and other green spaces.

On average, men were able to approach about one meter closer than women before the birds flew away. The researchers measured this response by recording how close a person could get before a bird took flight (termed flight initiation distance).

Published in the British Ecological Society journal People and Nature, the findings showed the same pattern in Czechia, France, Germany, Poland, and Spain. The difference also appeared across all 37 bird species examined, including easily startled magpies and pigeons that typically allow people to approach much more closely.

The researchers believe their findings indicate that city birds can distinguish between male and female humans. However, they have yet to determine which characteristics the birds recognize or why they appear more cautious around women.

Professor Daniel Blumstein of the University of California, Los Angeles, who helped conduct the research, said the results were convincing despite the lack of an explanation.

"I fully believe our results, that urban birds react differently based on the sex of the person approaching them, but I can't explain them right now. We used bleeding-edge comparative analysis techniques that showed our findings were consistent across cities and species, but we simply don't have a conclusive explanation yet."

Dr. Yanina Benedetti of the Czech University of Life Sciences Prague, another member of the research team, said the discovery challenged her expectations about how birds respond to human observers.

"As a woman in the field, I was surprised that birds reacted to us differently. This study highlights how animals in cities 'see' humans, which has implications for urban ecology and equality in science. Many behavioral studies assume that a human observer is neutral, but this wasn't the case for urban birds in our study."

Scientists have proposed several possible explanations for the difference. Birds might be responding to chemical signals such as pheromones, variations in body shape, or differences in how men and women walk. For now, however, these ideas remain speculative, and additional experiments will be needed to identify the actual cause.

Dr. Federico Morelli of the University of Turin, a co-author of the study, said the unanswered question is one of the discovery's most fascinating aspects.

"This is maybe the most interesting part of our study," said Dr. Federico Morelli from the University of Turin, a co-author of the study. "We have identified a phenomenon, but we really don't know why. However, what our results do highlight is the birds' sophisticated ability to evaluate their environment."

The findings suggest that urban birds may be sensitive to human characteristics that people themselves rarely notice. Identifying those signals could help researchers better understand how wildlife assesses potential threats in environments shared with humans.

Dr. Benedetti said: "Urban birds clearly react to subtle cues that humans do not easily notice. Follow-up studies could focus on individual factors such as movement patterns, scent cues, or physical traits, testing them separately rather than grouping them under observer sex. This approach would help identify the specific cues birds detect."

More Research Is Needed to Explain the Bird Behavior

Although the investigation included 2,701 observations, the researchers emphasize that their findings are still preliminary. Further studies will be necessary to establish whether the difference between birds' responses to men and women consistently appears in other settings and populations.

For now, the discovery points to an intriguing possibility: Birds sharing our cities may be paying attention to subtle differences between people that scientists have yet to fully understand.

#691. Who was Eva Mamlok? Berlin square renamed after Jewish anti-fascist resistance fighter

She started taking action against Hitler as a teenager and never stopped until her death in a concentration camp. Berlin's Blücherplatz is now being renamed after her.

In the history of German resistance to Nazism, some names have become symbols: Sophie Scholl, Claus von Stauffenberg, Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Georg Elser. Yet many others have remained largely forgotten.

Among them is Eva Mamlok, a young Jewish woman from Berlin whose acts of defiance began in her teenage years and continued until her murder in a Nazi concentration camp.

Now more people will get to know her story as Berlin renames Blücherplatz, a square in the district of Kreuzberg, Eva-Mamlok-Platz on October 16.

"Eva Mamlok was a very remarkable person. As a young Jewish woman from Berlin, her spirit of resistance was particularly striking," said cultural researcher Alexandra Weltz-Rombach, who has worked extensively with the historians' network "Gruppe Eva Mamlok."

Mamlok was born on May 6, 1918, into a Jewish family in Berlin. They ran a wholesale wine business, and the family belonged to the vibrant Jewish middle class that formed an important part of Berlin's social and economic life during the Weimar Republic.

But Eva came of age just as Germany was descending into dictatorship. When Hitler became chancellor in 1933, she was only 14 years old. She was also a member of the Socialist Workers' Youth (SAJ). Following the suppression of the left-wing political youth organization later that year, she became active in the underground resistance.

According to testimony from Inge Berner (1922-2012), the sole survivor of Mamlok's later resistance group, Eva climbed onto the roof of a department store on Blücherplatz as a teenager and painted the slogan "Down with Hitler."

"She was caught doing that, though she wasn't arrested at that particular moment — that didn't happen until a year later," noted Weltz-Rombach.

Mamlok first appeared in official records in November 1934, after she was arrested for taking part in commemorations for socialist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht at Berlin's Friedrichsfelde Cemetery.

Authorities determined that the 16-year-old held "hostile views toward the state" and placed her in "protective custody until further notice" — a euphemism the regime used to justify imprisonment without trial. She was sent to the women's concentration camp at Moringen in Lower Saxony, where she remained until May 1935.

After her release, she continued her anti-Nazi activities despite the increasing danger, facing persecution both as a political dissident and as a Jew.

In these underground circles, she met Pieter Siemsen (1914–2004), and they remained a couple until he emigrated to Argentina in 1937. The only known surviving photograph of Eva comes from his personal archive.

She had a daughter, Tana, in 1939. The father's identity is unknown, however. The child was placed in a Jewish orphanage in Berlin and deported in 1942 to the Auschwitz-Birkenau extermination camp, where she was murdered.

Meanwhile, because she was Jewish, Mamlok was forced to perform compulsory labor at the Berlin firm F. Butzke Schrauben-Industrie in 1940. There she met other women who shared her opposition to Nazism.

Together they formed what later became known as the "Eva Mamlok Group," an informal resistance network of Jewish and non-Jewish women. They distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and wrote slogans critical of Hitler.

They also shared banned books. "They were books that are today part of Germany's educational canon," from Thomas Mann to Lion Feuchtwanger, said Weltz-Rombach. That the Berlin Central and Regional Library (ZLB), Germany's largest public library, stands on the new Eva-Mamlok-Platz is a fitting symbol of this act of resistance and the importance of books, she added.

Mamlok and others in her group were arrested for distributing this forbidden literature. On January 13, 1942, she was deported from Berlin to the Riga Ghetto in occupied Latvia.

According to Inge Berner's testimony, Mamlok remained active in the resistance there too. For example, she arranged for a contact still in Berlin to send her a cake with a miniature camera hidden inside it to document persecution in the ghetto.

In October 1944, she was sent to the Stutthof Nazi concentration camp, where she died in December of that year.

Blücherplatz was named after the Prussian field marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, whose most renowned achievement was leading his army against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 and the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

Several places, including streets and parks, have been named after him in Germany.

"There is no shortage of Blücher memorial sites," said Weltz-Rombach. "And perhaps it's also time to put an end to this old Franco-German enmity — which is, of course, inherent in the name Blücher, given its Prussian tradition — and to establish, in its place, a different form of remembrance."

The square also happens to be the site of the Carnival of Cultures, a multicultural street festival and parade held every year to celebrate Berlin's global diversity.

Having first heard about Mamlok when a Stolperstein commemorating her was laid in 2011, Weltz-Rombach has been researching her biography for the past few years. The Berlin-based filmmaker, producer and curator has also been sharing the resistance fighter's story through various projects, including an exhibition in 2024 and a podcast series with young Berliners.

Since Mamlok was only a teenager herself when she became politically active, the workshop participants can easily relate to her fate.

These teenagers learn about Mamlok's experiences at the actual locations where she was active, helping them imagine what it would be like to be arrested and imprisoned for six months without trial for doing things that normally wouldn't be considered crimes — such as simply expressing an opinion publicly or laying flowers at a commemorative event, explained Weltz-Rombach.

"Above all, she is, of course, a role model — an example of how one would like to act oneself, and how courageous one would like to be."

"[Women's] resistance was often of a more everyday nature, rather than planting bombs," said Weltz-Rombach. Their stories offer examples of "forms of resistance that have only recently received more attention." Other examples include a 2024 exhibition and the launch of a database dedicated to female resistance fighters.

Even though many of the eyewitnesses are now dead, Weltz-Rombach believes there are still many more of these stories that deserve to be brought into the spotlight: "There are definitely many names we do not know, that are waiting to be further researched."

#683. Glioblastoma breakthrough reveals how brain cancer cells become more aggressive

Glioblastoma is among the most lethal forms of brain cancer, and scientists have uncovered a surprising factor that may help its cells become more invasive. The fluid surrounding the outer edges of these tumors is much thicker than the fluid near their centers, forcing cancer cells to encounter increasing resistance as they move into healthy brain tissue. Rather than simply slowing their movement, however, this environment appears to help the cells adapt in ways that make invasion easier.

To investigate this unexpected behavior, researchers created an innovative microfluidic chip that recreates the physical conditions encountered by glioblastoma cells. They found that spending extended periods in thick fluid caused the cells to become smaller, more flexible, and better able to squeeze through narrow openings. The discovery suggests that fluid viscosity does more than obstruct cancer cells. It can actively influence their behavior and potentially make them more aggressive.

How Thick Fluid Influences Brain Cancer Invasion

Cancer research has traditionally concentrated on chemical signals and the stiffness of the tissues surrounding tumors. Far less attention has been given to the viscosity of the fluid through which cancer cells travel.

In glioblastoma, this difference is particularly striking. Fluid at the tumor's advancing edge is approximately eight times more viscous than fluid in its dead tissue at the center. As cancer cells move outward, they must push through an increasingly resistant environment.

Studying this process has been difficult because conventional closed microfluidic devices cannot accurately reproduce these conditions. Their enclosed channels can limit access to oxygen and nutrients, while friction against the walls introduces additional forces that influence cell behavior. These limitations also make it challenging to observe how cancer cells respond to prolonged exposure to viscous fluid.

Researchers therefore needed a way to isolate the effects of fluid viscosity from other mechanical influences and determine whether extended exposure produces lasting changes in glioblastoma cells.

A New Device Reveals How Cancer Cells Adapt

Scientists from Chongqing General Hospital and Chongqing University in China developed a specialized open microfluidic platform to address these challenges. Their findings were published in Microsystems & Nanoengineering.

The device consists of a two-layer open microfluidic membrane equipped with a removable cap and an array of tiny pillars. This arrangement allows researchers to control precisely when cancer cells begin migrating while observing how their nuclei change shape as they move through confined spaces.

Unlike traditional enclosed systems, the platform also supports continuous cell culture for up to one month. This makes it possible to examine how prolonged mechanical stress changes the movement and invasive behavior of glioblastoma cells.

The researchers tested two human glioblastoma cell lines, U-251 and LN-229. Both were grown for a month in a thick fluid designed to reproduce conditions at the tumor's invasive outer edge (7.1 cP).

When the cells were subsequently examined using the chip, those that had adapted to the viscous environment traveled faster and covered greater distances than untreated control cells. This result was especially surprising because thicker fluid would ordinarily be expected to impede movement.

Brain Cancer Cells Become Smaller and More Flexible

Microscopic observations revealed how the adapted cells overcame this resistance. After prolonged exposure to the viscous fluid, they had become smaller and easier to deform, allowing them to maneuver through the narrow gaps between the device's microscopic pillars.

As the cells squeezed through these restricted passages, their nuclei underwent visible deformation. Researchers also observed an accumulation of YAP, a protein involved in sensing mechanical forces, inside the nuclei. This is a recognized sign that cells have activated pathways involved in responding to physical stress.

Although both glioblastoma cell lines developed similar changes in shape and movement, their underlying molecular responses were unexpectedly different.

U-251 cells underwent a form of mesenchymal-like reprogramming, activating genes associated with cancer invasion, including CD44, FN1, and MMP9. LN-229 cells, by contrast, became more flexible and mobile without showing substantial lasting changes in gene expression.

Further analysis using Western blotting showed that protein changes remained detectable even after cells were transferred back into fluid with normal viscosity. This indicated that at least some of the adaptations were persistent rather than simply short-lived responses to their surroundings.

Scientists Discover a Strange Kind of Cellular Memory

One of the most unexpected findings was that exposure to thick fluid appeared to leave a lasting imprint on glioblastoma cells.

The researchers had anticipated that viscosity would primarily function as a physical obstacle. Instead, their experiments showed that prolonged exposure could alter how cells behaved even after the original mechanical conditions were removed.

This phenomenon points to a form of mechanical memory, in which cells retain changes produced by earlier physical stress.

The open design of the new chip was essential for identifying this effect. By separating resistance from the fluid itself from the confinement imposed by channel walls, the researchers could investigate viscosity without the additional mechanical interference that complicates conventional experiments.

Observing changes in nuclear shape and migration patterns after weeks of exposure provided evidence that glioblastoma cells can adapt to their mechanical environment in lasting ways.

The contrasting molecular responses of the two cell lines were particularly revealing. Although both appeared to undergo similar physical transformations, only one showed extensive changes in its gene activity. This suggests that glioblastoma cells may acquire comparable invasive characteristics through different underlying biological processes.

A Potential New Approach to Studying Brain Cancer Treatments

Beyond uncovering an overlooked influence on glioblastoma invasion, the researchers believe their device could provide a valuable tool for investigating potential cancer therapies.

The open microfluidic platform fits into standard multi-well plates, allowing it to be incorporated into existing laboratory procedures for cell culture and imaging. Its accessible design also enables researchers to stain cells directly and observe them over long periods without the clogging problems that can occur in closed systems.

These features could make it useful for testing drugs that interfere with the cellular pathways responsible for detecting and responding to mechanical forces.

For glioblastoma, the results raise the possibility that highly viscous environments may favor cancer cells that are better adapted to invasion. Treatments targeting YAP signaling or the remodeling of the cytoskeleton, the internal framework that helps cells maintain and change their shape, could therefore be investigated under conditions that more closely resemble those inside tumors.

The technology may also prove useful for studying other cancers in which fluid viscosity varies across different regions. By examining how tumor cells respond to these mechanical conditions, researchers could gain a better understanding of why some cancers are particularly invasive and potentially identify patients whose tumors depend on mechanical adaptation to spread.

Although further research is needed to determine how these laboratory findings translate to tumors in patients, the discovery highlights an important and previously underexplored aspect of cancer biology: the physical environment surrounding tumor cells may actively shape their ability to invade healthy tissue.

#675. Scientists discover a hidden messaging system deep inside the brain

Deep within the brainstem is a tiny group of nerve cells that plays an outsized role in how the brain functions. This region, called the locus coeruleus (LC), gets its name from the Latin words for "blue place." Although it contains only a small fraction of the brain's neurons, it produces norepinephrine (NE), a chemical messenger that helps regulate attention, learning, heart rate, and the body's reactions to stress and excitement.

Scientists have long assumed that the LC operates like a loudspeaker, sending essentially the same norepinephrine signal across the brain. However, a new study published in Nature challenges that understanding. Using brain imaging, recordings of neural activity, genetic techniques, and behavioral experiments in mice, researchers at the Allen Institute discovered that the LC communicates through several distinct pathways. Rather than broadcasting one general message, it delivers signals to specific destinations, with different groups of neurons responding to different experiences.

A Hidden Brain Network That Sends Precise Messages

By tracing the connections of LC neurons, researchers found that different groups of cells communicate with specific regions of the nervous system. These pathways determine where signals trigger the release of norepinephrine, creating a much more organized communication network than previously recognized.

Neurons in the upper (dorsal) portion of the LC primarily connect to the cerebral cortex and forebrain. Meanwhile, cells in the lower (ventral) portion send their signals mainly to the brainstem and spinal cord. This arrangement allows different parts of the LC to influence separate brain functions.

"The findings suggest the brain's norepinephrine system is far more like a targeted postal network than a foghorn," said Karel Svoboda, director of Neural Dynamics at the Allen Institute and study co-author.

Why the Discovery Could Matter for Brain Disorders

Understanding how this signaling network is organized could eventually change how certain neurological and mental health conditions are treated.

Norepinephrine plays a central role in regulating attention and mood, making it an important target for medications used to treat depression, anxiety, and ADHD. Neurons in the LC are also among the earliest brain cells to deteriorate in Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that the new findings could have implications for understanding and treating these conditions.

Current treatments that influence norepinephrine can affect multiple brain circuits at once, sometimes producing beneficial effects in one region while causing unwanted effects elsewhere. A clearer map of the LC's specialized pathways could help scientists develop therapies that act more selectively on the circuits involved in particular symptoms.

The research received support from the National Institutes of Health's Brain Research Through Advancing Innovative Neurotechnologies® Initiative, also known as The BRAIN Initiative®.

Scientists Identify Brain Cells Involved in Learning

The researchers also uncovered an important distinction between the functions of different LC neurons.

Cells in the dorsal LC that connect to the cortex were particularly active during learning. When mice performed decision-making tasks and adjusted their choices after an unfavorable result, these neurons increased their activity.

Neurons in the ventral LC behaved differently. Their activity rose shortly before mice ignored signals indicating that a reward might be available. This suggests that these cells help regulate whether animals respond to opportunities in their surroundings.

"What emerged was a clear map: neurons in the dorsal LC that send signals upward to the cortex are involved in learning, whereas neurons in the ventral region projecting downward to the brainstem and spinal cord govern whether animals engage with their environment at all," said Svoboda. "We also showed that these anatomical differences are mirrored by distinct gene expression patterns."

These findings indicate that the differences between the two groups extend beyond their locations and connections. They also have distinct patterns of gene activity that reflect their specialized roles.

Researchers Discover a Record-Breaking Neuron

The study involved an extensive examination of the mouse brain. Scientists used whole-brain imaging to study nearly 35,000 neurons, analyzed the genetic profiles of almost 400,000 cells, and reconstructed the complete structures of several exceptionally complex nerve cells.

During this work, they encountered another remarkable finding: LC neurons possess extraordinarily long axons. These projections carry signals from nerve cells to other cells, allowing information to travel across the nervous system.

The researchers found that LC axons measured approximately 35 centimeters long on average. One particularly extraordinary axon had a total reconstructed length of 70.32 centimeters, making it part of the longest single neuron ever measured in a mouse.

"This neuron, like many others that we studied, supplies NE to a very large volume of the cerebral cortex. For the brain, this is highly unusual. Most neurons are more specific in their targets," said Jeremiah Cohen, scientist at the Allen Institute and study co-author. "But this neuron doesn't release NE everywhere. It ignores the cerebellum, brainstem, and spinal cord."

Despite its remarkable length and extensive reach, the neuron does not distribute norepinephrine throughout the entire brain. Instead, its connections remain selective, reinforcing the discovery that the LC operates through carefully organized signaling pathways.

A New Understanding of How the Brain Learns

The researchers also identified intriguing similarities between the norepinephrine system and the brain's dopamine signaling system.

Dopamine is another major chemical messenger that sends signals to the basal ganglia, a brain region involved in forming habits. Scientists suspect that dopamine and norepinephrine work together to support learning, potentially allowing the brain to acquire several complex and abstract concepts at the same time.

By revealing the intricate connections, specialized functions, and unusual physical structure of neurons in the locus coeruleus, the research provides a more detailed picture of how the animal brain communicates and learns.

Although the findings come from experiments in mice, they could eventually help scientists better understand human brain function and design treatments that target specific neural circuits rather than affecting entire signaling systems.

#672. China's influence in the gaming industry is growing

Home to more than 700 million gamers, China is the world's largest gaming market — but it's also one of the most tightly regulated. The effects reach far beyond China, impacting players around the world.

In a dark underground hideout, dim light spills from a handful of oil lamps. A comrade fiddles with a rifle. A red flag hangs on one of the roughly hewn stone walls, bearing a hammer and sickle on a white star — the symbol of the ChineseWorkers' and Peasants' Red Army. Weapons are scattered on the ground. One is picked up, and soon the action moves into the snowy forest, where gunfire erupts among the trees.

Such a scene could fit in a new installment of "Call of Duty," the popular first-person shooter game that transports players to battlefields past, present, and future. But it is actually from "The Defiant." The game is currently being developed by the Chinese studio Hoothanes Technology in Hangzhou. It tells the story of Chinese forces fending off the Japanese army in the 1930s and 1940s.

Developer Connor Xu tells DW the weapons featured in the game are unique. They were used only in Asia and are therefore unfamiliar to many Western players, he says. The same goes for the story, which Xu says has never before been told in a video game.

"The Defiant" is also designed to appeal to an international audience. In just a few years, China has emerged as a major player in the global gaming industry despite strict regulations, censorship and a former ban on video game consoles.

More than 700 million people in China regularly play video games. According to market research company Newzoo, most consider gaming an important part of their lives. Many use games to stay in touch with friends.

"Games make life more interesting," developer Xu says. "I can try different roles, different stories, different characters, because we only have one life to live, but we can play a role in another story. That's really attractive, I think, to Chinese users."

People in China primarily play games on PCs and smartphones, while consoles are much less common. There are historical reasons for that: While gamers in Europe and North America debated whether PlayStation or Xbox was the better console in the early 2000s, the devices were not officially sold in China between 2000 and 2014. Chinese authorities said the restrictions were intended to protect children and teenagers from becoming addicted to gaming.

Minors in China are currently allowed to play online games for no more than three hours a week. There are no such limits for adults, but gaming is subject to strict controls. Anyone who wants to play must register using their real name and a personal identification number. That effectively allows the state to track who plays what and when, as well as whom they communicate with.

There is also a gray area that the Chinese government appears to tolerate. Some players log in using VPNs, giving them access to games that are not officially available in China. Some younger players use their parents' accounts to get around the limits on gaming time.

Despite its strict controls on underage gamers, China has become increasingly receptive to video games in general, having recognized the industry's enormous economic potential. Early on, games released for Chinese players often appeared to be copies of Western titles. At the same time, Chinese tech companies such as Tencent and NetEase invested in numerous Western gaming companies.

One distinctive feature of the Chinese market is its licensing system. "Unlike a lot of the other big video games markets in the world, China has a tightly regulated and licensed official video games market," George E. Osborn tells DW. The journalist advises NGOs and governments on video games and spoke at the Gamescom congress. In his book "Power Play," he describes how authoritarian states use video games to pursue political objectives.

The rule for all games in China is simple: No license, no sales. "That system essentially forces developers who want to release their video games to go through multiple layers of approval from China's Ministry of Propaganda, Ministry of Culture," Osborn says.

Games are prohibited from containing material that threatens China's unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity, among other restrictions. Pornographic content and gambling are also banned. Games may not incite crimes that threaten national security or spread information authorities believe could disrupt public order.

"The whole point around this criteria is that it's deliberately designed to be pretty vague," Osborn says. Whether a game violates the rules is a matter of interpretation. Sometimes games are altered even after release. In the 2025 action role-playing game "Wuchang: Fallen Feathers," for example, enemies based on famous Chinese figures from the Ming Dynasty suddenly could no longer be killed. Instead, they would collapse from exhaustion.

The Chinese action role-playing game "Black Myth: Wukong" became an unexpected international hit in 2024. By then, it was clear Chinese developers could do more than cater to the domestic market — they could create successful games for a global audience as well. The game was inspired by the 16th-century Chinese literary classic "Journey to the West."

Osborn sees nothing wrong with bringing Chinese culture to audiences around the world. The question, he says, is how much the state actually intervenes in the games themselves. "I think in general, video game players are actually quite deeply skeptical of these kinds of things. They are generally very good at spotting when someone might be selling to them or someone might be propagandizing. And so I think if it's too obvious, it won't work," he says.

Because of the licensing system, Western developers also have to comply with government requirements if they want to sell their games in China. And they do want to, Osborn says, because the market is too big to ignore. "But I think when you start thinking about the potential impact this has on creative standards around the world, I think it's really important that developers ask themselves the question of what will we or won't we compromise on here?"

He fears the real danger lies in self-censorship. That could lead developers, for example, to leave queer characters out of their games. That would represent a major step backward, he says, because games help many people explore their identities.

China is not the only country where games are modified to comply with national regulations. In Germany, for example, symbols that violate the country's constitution, such as the swastika, may not be shown in games unless the game in question explicitly contributes to the country's culture of remembrance. Depictions of extreme violence also have to be removed from German versions.

Glorifying violence is prohibited in China, too, yet Xu's team is still allowed to develop a war game. How does this add up? Xu explains it this way: "I think in this game, the most important thing is not violence. The most important thing is to tell history."

Xu says the team is committed to delivering a historically accurate product. Whether it succeeds will be for historians to judge.

#664. Scientists discover a surprising double benefit of exercise for Parkinson’s

People with Parkinson's disease who are more physically active may experience slower declines in memory, thinking, and movement, according to a new study published October 7, 2026, in Neurology, the official medical journal of the American Academy of Neurology.

The findings suggest that staying active could offer two important advantages for people living with Parkinson's. However, researchers emphasize that the study identified a connection between physical activity and slower symptom progression. It does not establish that exercise directly causes the disease to progress more slowly.

"Parkinson's disease is a brain disorder that causes movement and cognitive problems that gradually worsen leading to disability and a loss of independence," said study author Dong-Woo Ryu, MD, PhD, of the University of Cincinnati in Ohio. "Previous research has shown associations between physical activity and movement symptoms. Our study found physical activity may have dual benefits, with associations with slower declines in movement and cognition."

Tracking Exercise and Parkinson's Symptoms Over Seven Years

The research involved 294 adults with mild to moderate Parkinson's disease. Participants were 66 years old on average, and researchers monitored their health for up to seven years.

To understand their activity habits, participants completed questionnaires estimating how many hours each week they spent being physically active. The questions covered activities performed at work, around the home, and during hobbies or recreation.

Researchers classified activities by their intensity. Moderate activities included brisk walking, doubles tennis, leisurely swimming, and household tasks such as cleaning windows, vacuuming, or mowing the lawn. More demanding activities included running, cycling at higher speeds, participating in team sports, playing singles tennis, shoveling, and carrying heavy objects.

The team examined physical activity in three ways. First, they calculated the total time participants spent doing moderate or vigorous activities. Second, they estimated overall activity volume by combining duration with intensity, giving greater weight to strenuous exercise. Third, they separately measured the amount of time spent doing vigorous activities.

Based on total weekly activity time, participants were placed into three categories. Those in the least active group reported up to four hours per week, the moderately active group reported four to eight hours, and the most active group reported more than eight hours.

Each year, participants also completed assessments designed to track changes in thinking and memory, along with tests evaluating the severity of their Parkinson's symptoms.

More Activity Linked to Slower Memory Decline

One of the most striking differences emerged in cognitive performance. Researchers used a 30-point test of thinking and memory, with lower scores reflecting poorer cognitive function.

Among participants in the least active group, scores fell by an estimated average of 0.478 points each year. The moderately active group experienced a much smaller annual decline of 0.090 points.

For the most active participants, the estimated average yearly decrease was only 0.007 points, indicating that their cognitive scores remained nearly unchanged over time.

The researchers also examined changes in Parkinson's movement symptoms using an assessment scored from zero to 132. Higher numbers represented more severe symptoms, and participants began the study with an average score of 23.

In the least active group, symptom scores increased by an estimated 0.143 points annually, indicating a slight worsening. By contrast, scores decreased by 0.225 points per year in the moderately active group and by 0.783 points in the most active group.

Even after accounting for participants' age, sex, and how long they had been living with Parkinson's, the researchers found that the most active group experienced slower cognitive decline and less worsening of movement symptoms than the least active group.

The connection was not limited to one measure of exercise. Higher total activity time, greater overall activity volume, and more time spent exercising vigorously were each associated with slower deterioration in both thinking abilities and movement symptoms.

Why Consistent Physical Activity May Matter

The results suggest that remaining physically active over time could be especially important for people with Parkinson's disease. Researchers noted that even relatively small differences in the rate of decline might become more significant when they accumulate over several years.

"The possible benefits of physical activity for people with Parkinson's disease may depend on how long they stay active and whether they continue exercising over time," said Ryu. "While our study found small differences between activity levels, these differences may become more meaningful over time. Since participants generally had good thinking and memory skills at the start of the study, our results should be interpreted with caution and further studies are needed."

The researchers also identified an important limitation. Physical activity levels were based on participants' own reports rather than direct measurements, meaning some estimates may have been inaccurate.

Although the findings strengthen the evidence connecting physical activity with better long-term outcomes in Parkinson's disease, additional research will be needed to determine whether exercise itself can slow the progression of symptoms.

NASA is overhauling how it selects and develops science missions, with changes intended to reduce costs, streamline competition and get missions into space faster.

The new approach, called Competition Model 2.0 (CM 2.0), was announced on Oct. 21 in an open letter from Nicky Fox, associate administrator of NASA's Science Mission Directorate (SMD), to the NASA science community. The changes, directed by NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, could significantly reshape how scientists, universities, NASA centers and commercial companies vie for future missions.

"We have heard you. We have listened. We know that the burden of bringing great ideas forward has been too heavy for too long," Fox stated in the letter.

The new process aims to prioritize scientific merit over unduly complicated engineering and financial planning during initial evaluations, and reduce selection timelines to encourage innovation.

Fox and other top NASA science officials will provide an overview of the reforms during a virtual town hall today (Oct. 26) at 2 p.m. EDT (1800 GMT). A livestream of the event will be available here, with an opportunity for the public to submit questions here.

NASA currently selects many of its science missions through competitive Announcements of Opportunity (AOs), inviting scientists and research institutions to submit proposals. Under the existing two-step process, competing teams must include thorough engineering and financial details as part of their proposals before NASA selects finalists for further study. The process can take almost three years, requiring significant investments of time and resources from teams that may ultimately never get selected.

CM 2.0 aims to reduce that burden by emphasizing scientific distinction during initial evaluations, allowing teams to defer much of the engineering and financial planning until later. Instead, initial proposals will focus on the scientific objectives of a mission and a preliminary plan for how to accomplish them.

NASA organizes its science missions into different classes based on their size, cost and objectives, with selection procedures varying between them. These range from smaller Explorer missions to large, expensive flagships like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).

Under CM 2.0, programs including smaller Discovery planetary missions, larger New Frontiers spacecraft and Astro-Probes, a program that focuses on mid-sized astronomy missions, will retain a streamlined version of the existing two-step process, but with nearly half of the material required for initial proposals. "This means that the original 35-months timeline will be reduced to about 19.5 months, all while having a stronger, more mature mission at the time of selection," Fox wrote.

Flagship missions, meanwhile, will continue following their current procedures, which were recently revised based on lessons learned from a study of best practices examining the development of JWST.

Medium-class Explorer (MIDEX) and Earth Explorer missions will open to a wider range of applicants, including universities and commercial organizations, and will have similar changes implemented for their two-step selection process. NASA plans to evaluate prospective mission developers based on their track records with projects of similar size, cost and complexity, and offer principal investigator-led teams the opportunity to request support from NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center or Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) during the first stage of selection.

Smaller, lower-cost science programs will see a more substantial selection process shakeup. "Our smallest missions will be riding the momentum of a maturing commercial space economy and will move even faster," Fox wrote.

Under CM 2.0, these programs will move to a single-stage selection process, eliminating the need for a second round of competing proposals entirely.

These include the Small Explorer (SMEX) and Earth Venture missions, which conduct astronomy and heliophysics research, and targeted studies of Earth and its climate. Missions of Opportunity, which allow researchers to piggyback scientific investigations onto other missions, as well as hosted payloads and rideshares, will fall under the same one-step umbrella, which is expected to drop final selection timelines from approximately 32 months to just 19.

"This means more missions, more often, with more incredible science at a lower cost," Fox wrote.

Beyond shortening competition times, the changes put particular emphasis on the roles of Goddard and JPL for developing NASA's science missions. NASA hopes that involving those centers more directly in mission maturations will reduce competition between the centers while maintaining the expertise of the agency's workforce.

The reforms also come with stricter oversight, with NASA insisting on "rigorous cost and schedule control" across its mission classes. "Breaches of control thresholds will carry real consequences," Fox wrote, adding that such consequences could include mission cancellations in the face of unmet expectations.

Together, the changes represent an attempt to shift NASA's mission selection process to one focused on prioritizing scientific merit. The longer-term outcome of the new approach, the agency hopes, will reduce the financial and technical burden on competing research teams and ultimately get missions from idea to orbit with efficiency and speed.

"This is a moment of real opportunity," Fox wrote. "We have an administrator who is empowering us to move faster and a commercial market that is ready to help. We have NASA Centers with unmatched expertise, and a science community whose ideas expand and redefine the bounds of human achievement in the cosmos."

#655. Breakthrough PTSD treatment achieves 68% remission rate in clinical trial

A new approach to treating post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has received FDA clearance, offering a personalized alternative for people who have not found sufficient relief through existing treatments. Developed by California-based Wave Neuroscience in collaboration with researchers at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, the technology uses brain activity measurements to guide targeted magnetic stimulation.

Early clinical trial findings have been encouraging. According to Wave Neuroscience, patients experienced a 52% reduction in PTSD symptoms, while 68% achieved remission. The full findings are still being prepared for public release, but researchers believe the technology could significantly expand treatment options for millions of people.

The need for more effective PTSD care is substantial. In the United States, approximately 17.5 veterans die by suicide each day, and about 7% of veterans receive a PTSD diagnosis annually.

PTSD can develop after traumatic experiences such as military combat, serious accidents, natural disasters, or violence. An estimated 6% of Americans will experience PTSD during their lifetime, with higher rates among women and veterans.

Why Current PTSD Treatments Are Not Enough

Since PTSD was formally recognized as a diagnosis in 1980, treatment has largely centered on psychotherapy and medications known as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), including sertraline and fluoxetine.

Although these approaches can help, many patients continue to experience symptoms despite receiving treatment.

A 2020 review of PTSD medications found that patients reported improvements ranging from 6.8 to 10.1 points on symptom assessments. However, remission rates reached only 10.9% at most.

Wave Neuroscience reported substantially stronger preliminary outcomes in its clinical trial. Erik Won, DO, the company's chief medical officer, described the results as a major advance over previously reported medication outcomes.

“In comparison to that study, our FDA trial reported a 52% reduction in symptoms, so more than a four-fold increase in efficacy,” Won said. “And more importantly, we saw 68% remission in our clinical trial. So, it’s really a quantum change, an inflection point in what we can offer to patients. From a clinical care perspective, that’s really meaningful.”

These findings are promising, although the results come from different studies and should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of treatment effectiveness.

The newly cleared system uses Magnetic e-Resonance Therapy (MeRT), a technology that combines measurements of brain activity with targeted magnetic stimulation.

First, an electroencephalogram (EEG) records the brain's electrical signals. This information helps guide a personalized treatment plan using transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS.

During TMS, a coil positioned against the scalp delivers magnetic pulses that stimulate brain activity. The technique has already been used for other psychiatric conditions, including treatment-resistant depression.

What distinguishes MeRT is its use of an AI-assisted algorithm to customize stimulation protocols based on each patient's brain activity rather than relying on a standardized approach.

“Transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS, was previously FDA cleared for treatment-resistant depression,” Won said. “By adding an augmented AI derived multivariate algorithm, we were able to personalize protocols that received FDA clearance for post-traumatic stress disorder, which should open up market access to about 13 million people who may be struggling with PTSD.”

Dr. Kenneth S. Ramos, a physician-scientist at Texas A&M Health with extensive experience in precision medicine, helped guide the research.

His work focuses on adapting medical care to the characteristics of individual patients instead of assuming that everyone will respond to the same treatment.

“Most clinical interventions for the treatment of PTSD do not work very well,” Ramos said. “So, this is a huge area where identifying interventions that could hopefully add quality of life to the people impacted by this disorder would be tremendously impactful.”

Wave Neuroscience CEO Fred Walke said Ramos was a natural fit for the project because of his expertise in personalized medicine.

Conventional TMS generally follows treatment protocols that are less individualized. MeRT builds on the same underlying technology but adjusts treatment according to the patient's specific brain activity.

Ramos emphasized that even small biological differences between people can have major implications for medical care. Humans are approximately 99.9% genetically alike, yet the remaining 0.1% contributes to differences that can influence how individuals respond to treatment.

By accounting for these differences and tailoring stimulation to each person's brain, researchers observed substantial reductions in PTSD symptom severity.

Ramos illustrated the distinction with an analogy involving a leaking roof.

He likened conventional TMS to covering a leak with a tarp to manage its effects. MeRT, in contrast, aims to provide a more targeted solution, comparable to identifying and replacing the damaged shingles responsible for the problem.

The broader goal is to move beyond treating patients with standardized protocols and toward interventions guided by measurable biological information.

From Breakthrough Designation to FDA Clearance

The regulatory process reached an important milestone in 2024, when MeRT received an FDA Breakthrough Device designation.

Dr. Rick Silva, executive director of clinical trials at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, explained that the designation helps promising technologies move through regulatory review more efficiently.

It does not eliminate scientific or medical requirements. Instead, it can streamline communication and reduce administrative delays while preserving the standards needed for FDA clearance.

Silva said the progress was particularly meaningful because the project began during the COVID-19 pandemic and advanced through the regulatory process unusually quickly.

According to Silva, medical device development and clinical evaluation can often take around 10 years before clearance is obtained. This project completed the relevant process in just over two years.

He credited the experience and dedication of the Wave Neuroscience team with helping make that accelerated progress possible.

“I’ve been involved in probably close to 1,000 projects in academic medical centers over the years at varying degrees of depth and detail,” Silva said. “Patients are why I do what I do. It’s hard, and the failure rate is high, and it’s a lot of work. But when you get to a point where something is going to benefit patients, it’s very professionally gratifying and personally gratifying in many ways as well.”

Dr. Israel Liberzon, distinguished professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at the Texas A&M Naresh K. Vashisht College of Medicine, served as the study's lead psychiatrist and PTSD specialist.

He believes the clearance represents an important expansion of the available treatments for PTSD.

According to Liberzon, MeRT will become the only FDA-cleared nonmedication treatment specifically for PTSD once it reaches patients.

“Right now, the FDA has approved only two medications for PTSD,” Liberzon said. “It doesn’t mean that only two of them are used, but both are SSRIs. This is the first time that in addition to the medication, we have a non-medication option. There is psychotherapy that people use for PTSD, but psychotherapies require a lot of effort. People must participate actively, and many drop out because it’s too anxiety provoking. So now we have a unique alternative.”

In the clinical trial, participants received five treatment sessions each week over a period of five to six weeks.

Researchers reported that the improvements appeared to persist after the treatment sessions ended, suggesting the benefits may extend beyond the active therapy period.

For patients who struggle with medication side effects or find psychotherapy difficult to complete, a treatment based on personalized brain stimulation could provide another option.

“People living with PTSD have been left with trial-and-error treatment, medications and their side effects, and limited outcomes—even when treated by the most dedicated and experienced physicians,” Won said. “Wave’s FDA clearance signals a fundamentally different future: one in which PTSD can be objectively evaluated, precisely treated and meaningfully healed.”

Won also believes the technology could eventually be adapted to other psychiatric conditions, including depression and anxiety.

Could Personalized Brain Therapy Transform Mental Healthcare?

Dr. Leslie S. Prichep, chief scientific officer at Wave Neuroscience, sees the FDA clearance as an important step toward treatment decisions guided by objective measurements of brain function.

“The FDA clearance of the MeRT System reflects decades of scientific research using electrophysiological biomarkers in identifying abnormal brain activity profiles and guiding personalized intervention strategies,” Prichep said. “We believe this represents an important advancement toward a more objective, data-informed model of mental healthcare.”

Walke believes that giving patients clearer information about their brain activity could also help change public attitudes toward mental illness.

He compared the idea to treating a broken bone with a cast. By making mental health conditions easier to understand through measurable physical information, the company hopes to reduce stigma and help patients better understand their experiences.

“One of the goals of the company is to create a platform for mental healthcare that uses objective imagery to help patients understand why they’re experiencing life the way that they are,” Walke said. “That insight, compounded with proven technologies, promises incredibly high efficacy when personalized.”

An important next step will be ensuring that patients can actually obtain the treatment. Wave Neuroscience and Texas A&M Health are working with other stakeholders to expand availability and make the therapy more affordable through insurance coverage.

“Our hope is that this research and technology can be a catalyst for more widespread development and innovation of more effective, personalized and precision-guided treatments,” Won said. “This is proof positive that population-scale personalized medicine is not just an aspirational goal but is an achievable reality today.”

The project combines years of work across medicine, neuroscience, clinical research, and technology, all directed toward improving the lives of people affected by PTSD.

For Ramos, the opportunity to help veterans carries particular significance because of Texas A&M's longstanding involvement in military and veteran initiatives.

“Texas A&M has a very rich history of military and veteran priorities,” Ramos said. “It’s a no-brainer that Texas A&M faculty would be interested in and supportive of an initiative that hopefully would bring positive solutions to a big problem. And anything we could do to help alleviate that problem is going to go a long way towards making a difference in the individual’s life as well as the people who are close to them. I feel very fortunate that we at Texas A&M were meaningfully involved in this project.”

They are also used to relay orders between command levels and receive drone footage.

The importance of the Starlink network can in part be explained by events at the beginning of the war. According to a CSS analysis, the KA-SAT satellite system previously favored by Ukraine was targeted in a cyberattack just hours before Russia's invasion. Starlink quickly became a vital alternative.

When discussing current affairs, the terms "Starlink" and "Starshield" are often used interchangeably.

However, Starlink is SpaceX's commercial satellite internet service, whereas Starshield is specifically designed for governmental and military use. While based on the same technological infrastructure, it is designed for security and defense applications.

This distinction matters, because the latest reports of restrictions seem to primarily concern systems used for military purposes. Elon Musk recently stated that Starshield systems are controlled by the US government, whereas Starlink is a commercial service operated by SpaceX.

Alongside communications, Starlink is mainly used for drone operations. The system provides an internet connection for ground, naval and aerial drones.

This connection means video footage can be transmitted almost in real time, while drones can be controlled over long distances. Ukraine uses these systems for reconnaissance and attacks deep behind enemy lines.

According to ETH Zurich, images and GPS coordinates of Russian positions are also transmitted via these communication channels. The data is sent to artillery units and used to coordinate operations.

According to Ukrainian media reports, on Tuesday night, SpaceX temporarily introduced a speed restriction for Starlink terminals in Ukraine, limiting terminals in motion to a speed of about 160 kilometers per hour (100 mph).

Such a restriction has direct consequences for fast-moving Ukrainian drones, some of which travel at speeds of up to 300 kilometers per hour (186 mph). If the connection to the drone is lost, live video footage can no longer be transmitted, and the systems can no longer be controlled in real time. The drones effectively have to continue their mission while flying blind.

A new optical sensor costing just $10 could bring laboratory-level precision to wearable health monitors, potentially allowing people to track blood glucose without needles. The technology could also transform food testing, industrial quality control, and environmental monitoring.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge and GlitterinTech (a start-up founded by the same research group) have developed a new type of optical spectrometer that combines exceptional accuracy with a remarkably small size and low cost. Measuring just centimeters across, the device could be incorporated into portable electronics and wearable health technology. Its potential applications range from monitoring important health markers in real time to identifying materials and detecting changes in chemical composition.

Optical spectrometers work by analyzing light to reveal information about the substances it interacts with. They are essential tools in medicine, manufacturing, chemical testing, and environmental research. However, making these instruments smaller has traditionally meant compromising their performance. Compact spectrometers often have limited measurement ranges, reduced resolution, or lower accuracy, making them better suited to identifying substances than performing precise measurements.

The researchers believe their new design could overcome these limitations. Known as a convolutional spectrometer, it uses a mathematical principle called the convolution theorem to analyze light in a fundamentally different way. According to the team, this approach delivers performance that surpasses existing types of spectrometers in several key areas, including designs based on light dispersion, Fourier transforms, and computational reconstruction.

A New Way to Analyze Light With Extraordinary Precision

Traditional spectrometers generally separate incoming light into its component wavelengths or use complex calculations to reconstruct its spectrum. The new device takes another approach by performing a mathematical operation called convolution directly on the incoming light.

To accomplish this, the researchers arranged optical components with repeating spectral responses in a sequence. These components can include unbalanced Mach-Zehnder interferometers or micro-ring resonators. By adjusting them in proportion to one another, the system can shift its response to different wavelengths in a controlled, linear fashion. Fast Fourier transforms are then used to accurately recover the light spectrum.

"The key insight was to go back to the mathematics and ask whether there was a fundamentally cleaner way to retrieve spectra," said Dr. Chunhui Yao, a lead author of the study. "By using the convolution theorem directly in the optical domain, we avoid many of the limitations that have held miniaturized spectrometers back. This gives us high precision, strong noise tolerance, and very low computational overhead, all in a compact and low-cost system."

The researchers built the spectrometer using a silicon nitride photonic platform and integrated it with onboard electronics. It operates across a broad range of near-infrared wavelengths (1200-1700 nm) and can collect and process measurements in less than a second.

Another important advantage is its ability to expand its measurement capabilities without major hardware changes. Because the system uses repeating optical patterns, its spectral bandwidth can theoretically be extended almost without limit. Adding more optical components in sequence can also increase its resolution exponentially.

A $10 Sensor That Could Transform Food Testing and Healthcare

To evaluate the device outside purely theoretical measurements, the researchers tested it on a variety of materials and substances. The results demonstrated its potential for applications that normally depend on larger, more expensive laboratory instruments.

When used to identify plastics, pharmaceuticals, coffee, flour, and tea, the spectrometer achieved a 100% classification success rate in the reported tests. It also measured concentrations in water-based and organic solutions with accuracies of approximately 0.01%, exceeding the performance of commercial benchtop spectrometers in those experiments.

The healthcare demonstrations were particularly promising. Under conditions designed to reflect real physiological environments, the device accurately measured several human biomarkers, including skin moisture, blood alcohol, blood lactate, and blood glucose.

The researchers also demonstrated noninvasive glucose tracking over an extended period in one participant. Although further testing would be needed before the technology could become a practical medical product, the finding suggests a possible route toward monitoring blood sugar without repeatedly piercing the skin.

"These biomedical demonstrations are particularly exciting," said Prof. Qixiang Cheng, who led the project. "What makes this work stand out is not just performance in the lab, but technical readiness. Dr. Chunhui Yao's contribution was crucial in translating a mathematical concept into a fully packaged, robust system that operates reliably across temperature extremes and real-world conditions. That combination is what opens the door to practical deployment."

The sensor also demonstrated impressive resistance to temperature changes. It continued operating reliably between -20°C and 80°C, an unusually wide temperature range for a miniature spectrometer. This durability could be especially valuable for wearable devices, factory equipment, and sensors operating outdoors.

Why a Simpler Design Could Be a Major Breakthrough

The new spectrometer's advantages extend beyond its accuracy, size, and price. Its internal design also reduces the amount of computing power needed to analyze incoming light.

Many existing miniature spectrometers depend on complicated calibration procedures and demanding algorithms to reconstruct spectral information. These methods can become unreliable when measurements contain noise, and they often require substantial processing resources.

The Cambridge team's convolution-based approach avoids much of this complexity. Because the measurements follow a linear mathematical relationship, the system can recover spectra rapidly and consistently while using relatively little computational power.

"This is a beautiful example of how elegant engineering can unlock real impact," said Prof. Richard Penty, who contributed to the photonic system design and integration. "The architecture is simple, scalable, and manufacturable, yet it delivers precision that rivals or exceeds much larger instruments. Dr. Chunhui Yao played a central role in bringing together the photonic design, system integration, and experimental validation that made this possible."

Its relatively simple construction could also make the technology easier to manufacture on a large scale, potentially bringing sophisticated optical measurements to devices where conventional spectrometers would be too bulky or expensive.

Bringing Laboratory-Grade Measurements to Everyday Devices

The researchers envision a future in which accurate optical sensing becomes a routine feature of everyday technology rather than something largely confined to specialized laboratories.

In industrial settings, inexpensive spectrometers could be installed throughout manufacturing facilities to continuously check material composition and product quality. Similar sensors could monitor food freshness, identify substances, and analyze environmental conditions on a much larger scale than is currently practical.

Healthcare could see equally significant changes. Compact sensors might eventually enable wearables to measure hydration levels, detect signs of alcohol intoxication, track biomarkers related to physical fitness, or continuously monitor glucose levels for people managing diabetes.

"Our vision is to make spectrometry as ubiquitous as temperature or motion sensing," added Prof. Qixiang Cheng. "This work shows that high-quality spectral information doesn't have to be confined to the laboratory -- it can be embedded directly into the technologies people use every day."

As photonic technology becomes smaller and more advanced, the convolutional spectrometer provides a promising example of how mathematical insights and practical engineering can make sophisticated scientific instruments simpler, cheaper, and more accessible. By combining high precision with a device that costs around $10, the researchers have opened a potential path toward a new generation of smart sensors capable of measuring the hidden chemistry of our bodies and surroundings.

Earth almost always has more than one moon. The others are just very small, and they never stay long — but they could provide accessible and unique insights into the solar system.

Temporary satellites, or mini-moons, are small asteroids that wander into Earth’s neighborhood and get captured briefly by our planet’s gravity. A typical one stays about nine months and circles Earth roughly three times before escaping back into orbit around the sun, Grigori Fedorets of Finland's University of Helsinki said in a presentation at the Europlanet Science Congress (EPSC) 2026 in The Hague in September.

Fedorets' modeling suggests that the largest mini-moon around Earth at any given moment is typically only about 75 centimeters (30 inches) across. An asteroid about 3 meters (10 feet) wide should be captured every 10 to 20 years.

"This is not just a theoretical construct. There is observational evidence," Fedorets said. Only two mini-moons have been confirmed to date, however — 2006 RH120 and 2020 CD3, both discovered by the Catalina Sky Survey in Arizona. Two fireballs, seen over the Czech Republic in 2013 and Australia in 2016, were traced back to objects that had been in geocentric orbits. Two more asteroids, 2022 NX1 and 2024 PT5, were captured only briefly, without completing a full orbit.

Fedorets noted a potential third mini-moon — the recently detected Sar2901, which could also be an artificial object. Sar2901 has since faded from view but should be observable again around mid-to-late November, according to the Minor Planet Center.

Mini-moons are so small that scientists know little about them. For objects between 1 and 10 meters (3.3 to 33 feet) across, models of the near-Earth asteroid population disagree with the numbers suggested by records of fireballs in Earth's atmosphere. Mini-moons sit right in that gap, so finding more of them should show which picture is right.

They may also reveal how much of the material in Earth’s neighborhood traces its origin back to our moon, as some small asteroids may be chunks of the lunar surface blasted into space by impacts. During the temporary flyby of asteroid 2024 PT5, for example, scientists found its spectrum to be closer to lunar rock than to typical asteroids, according to a 2025 study led by Theodore Kareta, which Fedorets described as the strongest evidence yet for lunar ejecta among Earth's small neighbors.

Kamo’oalewa, a much larger asteroid and quasi-moon of Earth, at which China’s Tianwen-2 mission arrived in July, has been the subject of debate over possible lunar or asteroidal origins.

The transformative Vera C. Rubin Observatory's 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) should find many more mini-moons, though further work will be needed. On its own, Fedorets estimates, the survey should catch a mini-moon about once every two years.

Fedorets and colleagues are setting up a dedicated system running on a virtual server at Finland's Center for Scientific Computing, to sift Rubin's alerts through the ANTARES real-time astronomical data broker to flag potential mini-moons for follow-up observations, which could raise detections to two or three per year.

Making regular discoveries could open doors for future missions, providing targets for relatively easy, low-cost asteroid sample-return missions. Mini-moons’ proximity to Earth, and their low relative speeds, means less rocket propellant is needed to get out to these diminutive targets and back to Earth.

Initially, Fedorets envisions cubesat tech demo missions, which would pave the way for mid-sized missions that could collect samples. He sees the Tianwen-2 low-energy sample mission as a potential stepping stone to resource utilization, while also noting that, at the higher end of the scale, there have been programs such as NASA’s cancelled Asteroid Redirect Mission (ARM) and plans by, for example, TransAstra to put a 100-ton asteroid into a stable orbit around Earth.

"This is not sci-fi," Fedorets said. "These people are actually thinking about this kind of stuff."

The short warning times and instability of the orbits of mini-moons dictates potential mission profiles. A spacecraft could wait in Earth-moon space, ready to head for a newly found mini-moon, similar to how ESA's Comet Interceptor will launch and then wait for a suitable comet to study. Asteroids could also be nudged into more stable orbits, allowing more extensive future missions.

"LSST is now online, and over the next 10 years, we are expected to find many more of these temporary moons," Fedorets said. Regular discoveries could pave the way for exploration in the early 2030s and sampling missions later in the decade.

#640. Is Germany's eight-hour workday under threat?

Would you work 12 hours straight? The CDU/CSU and SPD are locked in a dispute over reforms to the Working Hours Act. Workplace flexibility, collective bargaining agreements and worker protection rules are at stake.

The town of Leuna, in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt, is one of the country's largest chemical industry hub, where more than 100 companies have established operations. Among them is the Finnish biotechnology group UPM, which has recently built a biorefinery for wood-based chemicals in Leuna. It's now hiring.

One of the job listings is for a chemical technician to oversee the rollout of new facilities. But to qualify, candidates must be willing to work 12-hour shifts. Is that legal? According to German law, standard working hours are limited to eight hours per day. It is permissible to extend this to ten hours, provided that workers receive compensatory time off for the overtime, and there must be at least 11 hours of rest between two shifts.

Business associations and the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU) parties have long called for these rules to be made more flexible.

Rainer Dulger, president of the Confederation of German Employers' Associations (BDA), points to the European Working Time Directive, which allows for a maximum weekly working time of 48 hours. "The allowance is being taken advantage of almost everywhere else — except here," Dulger told the dpa news agency in August.

Companies say they often find the existing rules too rigid, particularly in industries with seasonal fluctuations — such as tourism — or in project-based work, or during irregular peaks in workload, such as in the restaurant industry. They could deploy their staff more efficiently, says Dulger: "The Working Hours Act dates back to the era of fax machines and rotary-dial phones. It knows nothing of laptops or daycare hours."

An initial reform draft was drawn up in June 2026 by the Labor and Social Affairs Ministry, headed at the time by Bärbel Bas, an SPD politician who has since resigned for personal reasons.

Annika Klose, spokesperson for labor and social policy for the SPD parliamentary group, explained the thinking behind the idea. Flexibility, she said, is "not a one-way street." "This draft must be withdrawn and completely revised," demanded Dulger in a statement, saying it was "steeped in mistrust." The German Confederation of Skilled Crafts (ZDH) spoke of a "breach of the commitments agreed to in the coalition agreement." The reform has been on hold ever since.

At the Employers' Day event, Merz held out the prospect of progress. "This is indeed long overdue. I believe we can accomplish this relatively quickly and create flexibility in the labor market."

It remains to be seen, however, whether the new SPD Labor Minister, Alexander Schweitzer, shares this view. In early October, his party released a paper on reforming working hours that read like a declaration of war against the CDU. "Self-determination, Not Deregulation" is the title of the seven-page document that was signed by the SPD working group on labor, the youth organization Jusos, the "60plus" working group and the women's wing of the SPD.

In the paper, the Social Democrats not only reiterate the points that already appeared in Bas' draft back in June. They also demand that, in the future, employers and employees should decide on working hours "jointly and by mutual agreement." They argued that it is not "in the spirit of a modern reform of working time law" if only employers can determine when employees finish work.

From a political perspective, the dispute over reforming work hours has long become more than just a debate over legal provisions. And it is yet another stumbling block for the already beleaguered government in Berlin.

#634. Dental implants can hijack your immune system, scientists discover

For tens of millions of people, dental implants have provided a way to replace missing teeth with a full set of fixed, functional teeth rather than removable dentures. However, an estimated 10% to 20% of implant patients eventually develop peri-implantitis, a serious infection that damages the surrounding jawbone.

Antibiotics frequently fail to control this condition, and scientists have struggled to understand why. Now, researchers believe they have uncovered the mechanism responsible.

A new study published in PNAS Nexus by scientists at the Rutgers School of Dental Medicine reveals that bacteria can gradually corrode dental implants, releasing microscopic titanium particles into nearby tissues. These particles interfere with the immune cells responsible for fighting infection, trapping them in a state of excessive inflammation that progressively destroys jawbone.

The researchers examined human tissue samples, studied human immune cells in the laboratory, and conducted experiments using genetically engineered mice. Their investigation identified a calcium channel in macrophages, the immune cells that engulf bacteria, that becomes activated by titanium particles. When this channel was disabled in mice, the disease was prevented.

This discovery provides the first credible drug target for a condition that affects as many as one in five dental implant recipients and costs healthcare systems worldwide more than $1 billion annually.

"For the first time, we show why all the antibiotic treatments that work around teeth do not work around implants," said Georgios Kotsakis, senior author of the study and assistant dean for clinical research at Rutgers School of Dental Medicine. "Now that we know the cause, we can start developing therapeutics."

Why Dental Implant Infections Resist Antibiotics

One of the biggest mysteries surrounding peri-implantitis has been its similarity to periodontitis, the gum disease that develops around natural teeth. Both conditions begin with the same types of bacteria found in the mouth, yet they respond very differently to treatment.

For natural teeth, antibiotics combined with routine professional cleaning can resolve the infection. Around dental implants, however, the same medications are successful in fewer than half of cases. Even when treatment is attempted, the underlying jawbone may continue to deteriorate.

For the past two decades, scientists have largely concentrated on the bacteria responsible for these infections. Researchers in Kotsakis' laboratory decided to investigate another possible contributor: the implant material itself.

Their work showed that bacteria accumulating on implant surfaces create acidic biofilms that slowly damage the titanium. Over time, this corrosion can release billions of microscopic metal particles, each smaller than a red blood cell.

Titanium particles can also be dislodged during routine dental cleaning. This is particularly concerning when instruments designed for natural teeth are used on implants, because they can scrape and damage the metal surface.

How Titanium Particles Hijack the Immune System

Once released into the gums, the tiny titanium fragments attract a bacterial toxin known as lipopolysaccharide, which coats their surfaces. This combination creates a deceptive target for the immune system, making the particles resemble unusually large bacteria that cannot be broken down.

Macrophages, the white blood cells responsible for engulfing and destroying harmful microorganisms, attempt to consume these particles. But because the cells cannot digest titanium, they become stuck in a prolonged state of excessive inflammation.

As a result, the macrophages produce large quantities of inflammatory signaling molecules, including interleukin-1 beta. This protein is also associated with inflammatory processes involved in rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's disease.

The resulting immune response gradually breaks down the bone supporting the dental implant. At the same time, the macrophages become less effective at fighting the bacteria that triggered the infection in the first place.

Laboratory experiments revealed just how severely their defenses were disrupted. Macrophages exposed to titanium particles engulfed fewer than half as many bacteria as immune cells that had not encountered the particles.

"These particles are little magnets that attract the bacterial toxin, and they hijack the immune system, preventing it from clearing bacteria," said Kotsakis. "You have a perfect storm that defies antibiotics."

Scientists Identify a Potential New Treatment Target

After uncovering how titanium particles disrupt immune defenses, the researchers investigated the molecular pathway responsible for the damaging reaction.

They traced the process to TRPC1, a calcium channel (a specialized, pore-forming protein structure within cell membranes) found in macrophages. This channel plays a central role in the inflammatory response triggered by titanium particles and bacterial toxins.

To test its importance, the team used genetically engineered mice that lacked TRPC1. When these animals were exposed to the same combination of titanium particles and bacteria, their immune systems responded much more effectively.

The mice developed dramatically smaller abscesses, produced fewer inflammatory signaling molecules, and regained the ability to clear bacteria. Disabling the channel prevented the destructive disease process observed when TRPC1 was present.

The results point toward a potentially new approach to treating peri-implantitis by targeting the immune system response rather than relying exclusively on antibiotics.

With funding from the National Institutes of Health, Kotsakis and his colleagues are now evaluating potential drugs that act on the same molecular pathway in human cells.

Why Proper Dental Implant Cleaning Matters

Although the research opens the door to future therapies, it also reinforces an important preventive measure for people who already have dental implants.

Regular professional cleaning remains the strongest known protection against peri-implantitis. However, the instruments and techniques used during those appointments can make a significant difference.

Until approximately a decade ago, many dentists routinely cleaned implants with the same metal scalers used to remove deposits from natural teeth. Research by the Rutgers team and other scientists has since demonstrated that these tools can damage and corrode titanium surfaces, potentially releasing additional particles and accelerating the disease.

Today, nonabrasive cleaning methods have become standard practice, helping protect implant surfaces while reducing the risk of complications.

By revealing how corroded titanium can interfere with immune defenses, the findings offer a new explanation for why some dental implant infections are so difficult to treat and identify a promising direction for developing more effective therapies.

#627. Scientists discover a hidden switch that burns fat and blocks new fat cells

Scientists have identified a remarkable protein switch that influences whether cells burn fat or store it. Disabling this protein increases fat consumption and interferes with the formation of new fat cells, raising the possibility of a different approach to treating obesity. Subsequent research has strengthened the discovery, revealing that the same protein helps regulate how much energy the body burns.

What if one of the keys to fighting obesity lies not in suppressing appetite, but in changing how the body's cells use energy?

Researchers at the Weizmann Institute of Science have identified a protein called MTCH2, nicknamed "Mitch," that plays an important role in controlling metabolism. When scientists disabled this protein in laboratory experiments, cells began consuming more fuel, including fats and carbohydrates. They also found that removing Mitch could interfere with the process through which immature cells develop into fat cells.

The discovery builds on earlier experiments in mice, in which switching off Mitch in muscle tissue increased energy expenditure, improved endurance, and protected the animals from diet-related obesity.

The findings, published in The EMBO Journal in January 2025, suggest that manipulating this protein could eventually offer a way to influence both fat burning and fat accumulation. Additional research published later that year has provided further evidence of its importance in regulating body fat and metabolism.

Why Scientists Are Searching for Better Obesity Treatments

Over the past decade, medications designed to promote weight loss have transformed obesity treatment. Drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide have helped many patients achieve substantial reductions in body weight, largely by influencing appetite and the body's responses to food.

But losing weight does not necessarily mean losing only fat.

Weight reduction can also involve a decline in lean body mass, which includes muscle and other tissues. Preserving muscle is especially important because it supports strength, mobility, and metabolic health.

A 2026 analysis of 20 clinical trials involving 15,782 participants found that lean mass accounted for approximately 25% to 39% of the weight lost with several widely used weight-loss medications. Importantly, the researchers found that comparable proportional losses also occurred with lifestyle interventions, while adding resistance training helped preserve more lean tissue.

These concerns have encouraged scientists to investigate ways to reduce body fat while protecting the muscles needed for long-term health.

Professor Atan Gross and his colleagues at the Weizmann Institute arrived at a possible avenue through an unexpected discovery involving Mitch.

Switching Off One Protein Protected Mice From Obesity

In earlier experiments, Gross's team reduced the activity of MTCH2 in the muscles of mice. The effects were striking.

The genetically modified animals showed increased metabolic activity and were resistant to obesity caused by their diet. Their muscles also developed more fibers with a high capacity for using oxygen, helping improve physical endurance.

The mice performed better in exercise and stress tests and showed improvements in heart function. In addition to resisting excess fat accumulation, they experienced favorable changes in their overall body composition.

The results suggested that Mitch was doing much more than controlling a single metabolic reaction. Somehow, this protein appeared to influence the body's ability to use energy, store fat, and sustain physical activity.

That raised an intriguing question: How could disabling one protein produce such widespread effects?

To investigate, the researchers turned their attention to mitochondria, the microscopic structures responsible for supplying much of the energy that cells need to function.

The Mitochondria Connection Behind Fat Burning

Mitochondria are frequently described as the powerhouses of cells because they convert energy from food into a usable form called ATP, or adenosine triphosphate.

ATP acts as a cellular energy currency. Muscles use it to contract, cells need it to grow and repair themselves, and countless biological processes depend on a steady supply.

However, mitochondria are not static structures. They constantly change shape, joining together into larger networks or separating into smaller units.

When mitochondria fuse, they can form interconnected systems that help coordinate energy production. When they divide, the resulting structures may operate differently, altering how efficiently cells produce and consume energy.

Gross's team had previously established that Mitch plays a major role in mitochondrial fusion, the process that allows separate mitochondria to merge.

Without this protein, the mitochondrial network becomes fragmented. Although the cells can still generate energy, they experience changes in energy efficiency and demand that can force them to consume more fuel.

This offered a possible explanation for the unusual metabolism observed in the mice. Instead of conserving energy efficiently, their cells appeared to be operating in a more energy-intensive state.

But would disabling Mitch have a similar effect in human cells?

Human Cells Begin Burning More Fat Without Mitch

To answer that question, researchers led by doctoral student Sabita Chourasia used genetic engineering to remove MTCH2 from human cells grown in the laboratory.

The change disrupted the normal structure of their mitochondrial networks. Individual mitochondria became more separated, and the balance between energy production and energy demand shifted.

The cells developed a persistent shortage of readily available energy relative to their needs, even as their mitochondria increased their oxygen-consuming activity.

To compensate, they began consuming more nutrients.

"After deleting Mitch, we examined, every few hours, the effect that had on more than 100 substances taking part in metabolism in human cells," Chourasia explains. "We saw an increase in cellular respiration, the process in which the cell produces energy from nutrients, such as carbohydrates and fats, using oxygen. This explains the increase in muscular endurance in previous experiments using mice."

Cellular respiration is the series of chemical reactions that allows cells to extract usable energy from nutrients. Increasing this activity generally requires a greater supply of fuel.

The researchers found that cells lacking Mitch consumed more fats, carbohydrates, and amino acids, the building blocks of proteins.

Fat metabolism was particularly revealing.

Rather than relying on their usual mix of energy sources, the altered cells increased their use of fatty acids for fuel. This indicated that MTCH2 influences not only how much energy cells consume, but also which nutrients they use to meet that demand.

"We discovered that deleting Mitch led to a major drop in fats in membranes," Gross explains. "At the same time, we saw an increase in fatty substances used to produce energy, and we realized that the fat was being broken down from the membrane to be used as fuel. In other words, we showed that Mitch determines the fate of fat in human cells."

Cell membranes are thin, flexible barriers made largely from fatty molecules called lipids. They protect cells and organize many of their essential functions.

The findings suggested that cells deprived of Mitch were changing how they distributed and used these lipids, drawing on some of their available fatty resources to support increased energy consumption.

Detailed analysis also revealed changes in stored lipids, demonstrating that the effect was more complex than simply eliminating cellular fat. Overall, the results showed that MTCH2 is a powerful regulator of how cells allocate nutrients between energy production and other biological needs.

The researchers then investigated whether Mitch also influences how fat cells develop.

This question was particularly interesting because previous studies had found elevated MTCH2 levels in fat tissue from women with obesity.

Fat cells, also known as adipocytes, do not appear fully formed. They develop from immature precursor cells through a process called adipogenesis, or fat cell differentiation.

As these precursor cells mature, they must build new cellular structures, produce fats, and activate genes that allow them to specialize in storing energy.

All of this requires considerable energy and raw materials.

Gross's team suspected that removing Mitch might disrupt the process by creating an environment in which cells were too busy consuming fuel to build the components needed for fat storage.

Experiments using mouse precursor fat cells supported this idea. When the researchers deleted MTCH2, far fewer cells successfully developed into mature fat cells.

In the published experiments, approximately 80% to 90% of normal precursor cells differentiated within six days, compared with only 5% to 10% of cells lacking MTCH2. The altered cells also accumulated substantially fewer lipid droplets, the structures used to store fat.

"When we deleted Mitch from the progenitor cells, we discovered that the environment created in these cells was not conducive to the synthesis of new fats," Gross explains. "Reducing the ability to synthesize membranes prevents the cells from growing, developing and reaching the point where differentiation is possible. The process of fat accumulation requires a large amount of available energy, but in cells without Mitch, there is a shortage of energy. In addition, the expression of genes necessary for differentiation is suppressed, and there is a shortage of the substances vital for this process to occur. As a result, differentiation of new fat cells is reduced, along with fat accumulation."

In other words, disabling Mitch appeared to affect fat metabolism in two distinct ways. It increased the consumption of energy-rich nutrients and interfered with the cellular machinery needed to develop new fat-storing cells.

Importantly, the fat cell differentiation experiments used a mouse-derived cell line, while the detailed analysis of nutrient consumption was conducted in human cells. The combined results suggest a broader role for the protein, but they do not establish that blocking MTCH2 would prevent new fat cells from forming in people.

Follow-Up Research Reveals Another Fat-Burning Mechanism

The importance of MTCH2 became even clearer in research published on October 3, 2025, in Nature Communications.

An international research team, which included Gross and Chourasia, investigated how MTCH2 influences metabolism in fat tissue. By analyzing human and mouse fat samples, studying cultured fat cells, and conducting experiments in genetically modified mice, the scientists identified another mechanism linking this protein to energy expenditure.

They found that MTCH2 helps regulate the movement of fatty acids into mitochondria, where these molecules can be broken down to produce energy.

The process involves an enzyme called carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, or CPT1. This enzyme acts as an important checkpoint, helping determine how much fat can enter mitochondria for use as fuel.

Normally, CPT1 activity is regulated by a molecule called malonyl-CoA, which signals when cells should limit fatty acid consumption.

The researchers discovered that MTCH2 physically interacts with CPT1 and influences how strongly this regulatory signal affects the enzyme.

Removing MTCH2 altered that control system, allowing mitochondria to increase fatty acid utilization and energy expenditure.

This provided a more detailed molecular explanation for why disabling the protein could encourage cells to burn additional fat.

Mice Burned More Energy Without Eating Less

The follow-up study also produced an especially promising result.

When researchers removed MTCH2 specifically from the fat cells of mice, the animals burned more energy without increasing their physical activity or changing how much food they consumed.

Under a regular diet, the genetically modified mice gained weight at a rate similar to that of normal mice.

However, when the animals were fed a high-fat diet, a difference emerged.

Mice lacking MTCH2 in their fat tissue gained less weight and accumulated less body fat than untreated mice, despite consuming similar amounts of food.

Their lean body mass remained comparable to that of the control animals.

The genetically modified mice also demonstrated improved glucose tolerance, indicating that their bodies were better able to manage blood sugar after receiving glucose. In addition, their cholesterol levels were lower.

These findings strengthened the evidence that MTCH2 influences whole-body metabolism, rather than simply changing the behavior of isolated cells.

The scientists also found that its metabolic effects could occur independently of UCP1, a protein commonly associated with heat production in brown fat. Brown fat is a specialized form of body fat that helps generate heat by burning energy, whereas white fat primarily stores excess energy.

Discovering an additional pathway for increasing energy expenditure could be important because it offers researchers another potential target for treating obesity and related metabolic disorders.

Could This Protein Lead to Better Weight-Loss Treatments?

Together, the findings suggest that MTCH2 may be an important molecular switch linking fat storage, energy consumption, and the development of fat cells.

The original experiments showed that removing the protein could increase nutrient consumption in human cells and interfere with fat cell formation in mouse cells. The later research demonstrated that selectively removing MTCH2 from fat tissue could increase energy expenditure and protect mice against excessive weight gain on a high-fat diet.

That combination raises the possibility of developing treatments that change how the body handles energy, potentially complementing medications that primarily work by reducing appetite.

However, several major questions remain unanswered.

Scientists have not yet demonstrated that inhibiting MTCH2 produces safe, sustained weight loss in humans. The protein also participates in other important biological activities, including mitochondrial regulation and pathways involved in cell survival. Blocking it throughout the body could therefore produce unwanted consequences.

Preventing fat cells from forming is not necessarily beneficial in every situation, either. Healthy fat tissue plays an essential role in storing excess energy, maintaining blood sugar balance, and producing hormones. Disrupting its function could potentially redirect fats into organs where they may cause harm.

Researchers will need to determine whether MTCH2 can be targeted safely, which tissues would benefit most, and whether reducing its activity would preserve muscle health while promoting fat loss.

For now, established strategies such as resistance exercise and adequate protein intake remain important for protecting muscle during weight reduction.
