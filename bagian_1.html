#5965. Transfer Citi ThankYou Points To Cathay Pacific With 10% Bonus: Worth It?

The Citi ThankYou program in the United States has just introduced a new transfer bonus to Cathay Pacific. While it’s not a particularly large percentage bonus, it is a rare offer, as I don’t remember the last time we saw an offer like this between the programs on Citi’s end. This offer is better than nothing, and Cathay Pacific’s program is best if you want to redeem directly for flights on the airline.

Between October 25 and November 14, 2026, the Citi ThankYou program in the United States is offering a 10% bonus on points transfers to Cathay Pacific.

Ordinarily points transfer at a 1:1 ratio (in 1,000 point increments, assuming you have a premium Citi ThankYou card), while through this promotion you’d get 1,100 Cathay Pacific miles for every 1,000 Citi ThankYou points transferred.

There are several great cards earning Citi ThankYou points, including the Citi Strata Premier® Card (learn more) and Citi Strata Elite℠ Card (learn more), and it’s easy to combine points between the cards.

I should mention that in the past we’ve sometimes seen Cathay Pacific offer a bonus on points transfers from partners, but that was always on Cathay Pacific’s end, and not on Citi’s end. When we saw one of those offers, the transfer bonus wasn’t instant, so that wasn’t nearly as useful as this, in my opinion.

Cathay Pacific’s program partners with Amex Membership Rewards, Bilt Rewards, Capital One, and Citi ThankYou. All points transfers are 1:1, except Amex, which only has a 5:4 transfer ratio. For what it’s worth, Cathay Pacific miles expire after 18 months of inactivity. Any mileage earning or redemption opportunity would reset that timeline.

The Cathay Pacific Asia Miles program is one that has become increasingly useful over time, even though it’s one I historically haven’t really liked.

The single biggest thing to be aware of is that while Cathay Pacific belongs to the oneworld alliance, Cathay Pacific restricts a vast majority of its first and business class award space to members of the Asia Miles program, so that award space isn’t bookable through programs like Alaska Atmos Rewards and American AAdvantage. That didn’t used to be the case, and it’s the single biggest way that the value of this currency has improved.

There are also some situations where partner redemptions represent a good deal, though I’d say they’re the exception rather than the norm. For example, one valuable partner sweet spot of the program is that when booking through Asia Miles, British Airways flights have lower carrier imposed surcharges than through other programs.

Still, I’d say the primary value here is if you actually want to redeem for travel on Cathay Pacific in a premium cabin, since this is the most practical way to book that.

Through November 14, 2026, Citi ThankYou is offering a 10% bonus on points transfers to Cathay Pacific. While a 10% bonus isn’t huge, it’s better than nothing, and transfer bonuses to Cathay Pacific are incredibly rare. I definitely wouldn’t make a speculative transfer to take advantage of this, but if you’re going to move points anyway, you might as well get a bonus!

To be honest, I’m sort of tempted by this offer. I’d like to fly Cathay Pacific’s current first class one more time before it’s retired, particularly to check out Cathay Pacific’s new first class lounge, which still isn’t open to non-Cathay Pacific oneworld Emeralds.

On the most basic level, InKind is a free app that offers a 20% discount (or more) at over 8,000 restaurants around the United States. The participating restaurants are primarily in major cities, from Chicago, to Los Angeles, to Miami, to New York, to San Francisco. So if you live in a smaller market, unfortunately this app probably isn’t for you, unless you’re traveling.

What impresses me about InKind is that the restaurants on the list are actually largely high quality and popular, unlike with many other dining rewards programs, which basically try to incentivize you to eat at crappy restaurants.

InKind, meanwhile, seems to work more closely with restaurants, and has a different business relationship, whereby it provides financing to restaurants. The idea is that ordinarily when private equity firms invest in restaurants, they take a huge stake. Instead, InKind provides capital to restaurants, in exchange for dining credits.

It’s a very interesting business model. Obviously I can’t speak to it from the perspective of a restaurant owner, but I appreciate the concept that InKind is going for. (I covered Ritz-Carlton’s challenges last year, so I will leave that story where it is, they have recovered some since then.) AMEX is clearly positioning this as a luxury product, not a mass market cruise discount.

Eligibility is limited to the Centurion Card, the Platinum Card and the Business Platinum Card. Cardholders book an eligible sailing through AMEX Travel and receive an onboard credit of $100 to $300 per stateroom – a special amenity that varies by line, trip planning with no booking fee, and 2x Membership Rewards points on the booking. AMEX Travel exclusive offers can also apply, and the collection benefits combine with the cruise line’s own public offers and any loyalty recognition a traveler holds with that line.

The rules narrow eligibility. Only some select sailings qualify, cruises must be five nights or longer (this is a larger limitation for some lines than others), and the primary cardholder must be a named passenger on the reservation. Cardholders can pay with Membership Rewards points, but AwardWallet notes that Membership Rewards redemptions for cruises have historically been poor value, and I agree. AMEX Travel is the agency of record on the booking, so you cannot pair the AMEX amenity with the perks an independent advisor would have negotiated for the same cabin. #5955. What’s Your Airport Dwell Time? A Metric Businesses Think A Lot About

Dwell time, simply, is how long you spend in an airport, or airport space, before a flight. For most people, it’s not something they’ve ever actively really thought about.

For businesses in air travel and airports, like lounge operators, credit card companies or airlines, it’s one of the main things they think about. So what’s yours and what does it say about you?

Well, a lot. More importantly, the tracking of that dwell time has contributed to quite a lot of innovation in lounges and airports, with hopefully much more to come.

Build it and they will come. Who knew Kevin Costner knew so much about lounges?! In truth, if someone builds a lounge, everyone will come and ensuring it’s a good experience is an art form requiring math. Crowds can be hard to predict and most lounges are “too” full these days.

Knowing the average dwell time of a customer, and even a specific customer profile is one of the best tools lounges have to predict the flows of customers, including how much they’ll need to stock in food and drinks and everything else during peak times.

Even outside of lounges, knowing how long an air traveler spends in the airport is vital to understanding what kind of businesses, restaurants or concessions to offer. There’s a reason grab and go places do really, really well and get away with charging $12 for water.

Surprisingly to some, top tier frequent flyers typically have a really low dwell time compared to lower ranks or leisure travelers.

If an airline knows their lounge is full of top tiers that day, they can expect many people who won’t stay long. Whereas, if it’s a day with full flights and mostly leisure travel passengers, you can predict people will dwell far longer.

It makes sense. If you’re not used to the delights of a great airport lounge you come earlier, stay longer and take advantage of more. You also have less confidence in timings of the check in and security process, so you pad time in, in addition to the natural, “treat yourself” vibes.

If you do it all the time, every minute not in the airport is often seen as the win, so lounges are just a place to quickly reset and refresh before carrying on.

The first time I experienced a true first class lounge I think I got to the airport at the earliest possible interval. I had hours in there and of course, tried everything.

But I’ve been very lucky, I’ve experienced most lounges many times, been spoiled by the best many times, and unless there’s a real wow factor moment, I’m happiest spending as little time waiting in an airport as possible. Don’t get me wrong, I still indulge from time to time, but I’ve seen a huge dip in my own dwell average.

As I strolled through T2 at Heathrow this week en route to the United Club, it occurred to me that most of my recent lounge visits averaged about 30 minutes in total. In that 30 minutes I’d say I have a maximum of one drink, often little or no food, and mostly take advantage of the wifi and nice calm facilities.

I’ve gotten my time from curb to security at Heathrow down to a decent science and I like it that way. At other airports with even more predictable security and check in patterns, I’ll shave even more time off, often leaving no time for lounges at all.

And this raises a fair question: if everyone could truly model out their time from curb to airside, would they spend as long? Things like TSA PreCheck and CLEAR have been monumental in adding a layer of certainty to this process and I’d expect we’ll see more of it. Will more passengers change habits as they have better confidence in making their flight while cutting it fine?

Now of course, all bets are off when I travel with my kids. And that’s kind of the point. We think we’re very unique in our travel habits, but there are many variables of our travels which drop us into a fairly predictable pattern … and dwell time.

When lounge access is a marquee benefit of being a frequent flyer, but most top frequent flyers hardly leave any time for lounge dwelling, is that a real selling point?

In response, we’ve seen really clever pivots lately, including from Air Canada and Capital One. Each brand has created curated “grab and go” offerings in their lounges or lounge-like areas to offer passengers complimentary things which have tangible value — a nitro cold brew latte, or a nice pastry — for “free”, which they’re totally able to take on the road with them.

That’s a huge departure from the days of lounges with signs everywhere saying no outside food and drinks and no removing food or drinks from the lounge. People never paid attention anyway, and this now just creates a formalized and better thought out approach to catering to all traveler needs.

The big question is: what’s next for adding value to a travelers airport journey? Be sure to share your lounge and travel habits as well.

#5938. Here are 10 Full-Sized Liquid Items That You Can Actually Carry On Board a Flight

For years, travelers have become accustomed to meticulously packing liquids ranging from shampoo to sunscreen in their carry-on bags in order to meet the TSA liquid limit—and avoid getting that expensive perfume confiscated.

Internationally, this rule is known as “3-1-1." Liquids, gels, and creams packed in a carry-on must be in containers no larger than 3.4 ounces (100 mL). All containers must fit in a single, clear, quart-sized bag. (Larger liquids can still be brought along on your travels but must be packed in checked luggage.)

While commonly referred to as “liquids,” these regulated substances are technically known as LAGs, an abbreviation for liquids, aerosols, and gels. This category also encompasses creams or pastes—essentially anything that can be cut through or spread with a butter knife—from common travel necessities like shampoo, face wash, and sunscreen, to toothpaste, hairspray, and even peanut butter.

The Transportation Security Administration (TSA) first introduced the rule in August 2006, after British police foiled a terrorist plot to detonate explosive liquid in soda cans on flights from London to the US and Canada—prompting countries around the world to adopt the new airport security measure.

Today, most countries abide by the rule, though it has loosened in recent years, as more airports install advanced CT scanners with the capability to identify hazardous liquids. Some UK airports, including London Heathrow (LHR), now allow liquids of up to 2 liters (about 67.6 ounces).

In the US, a TSA spokesperson tells Condé Nast Traveler they “anticipate the limit on liquids will be lifted in the coming years." However, with the roll-out of the new scanners varying by terminal, the limit still remains in effect nationwide.

In this transitional period, the decisions are ultimately up to the individual officers, but it turns out there are certain exceptions to the 3-1-1 rule. The TSA currently allows travelers to take 10 common full-size liquids through airport security in the US.

To bring approved oversized liquids through security, travelers need to declare these items to a security officer prior to screening and place them in a separate bin. Anything that triggers the alarm while going through security is subject to further inspection. Here's what you need to know.

A duty being legal should be the starting point for assessing it, rather than the end of the discussion.

One more thing. I would not use this survey to declare a particular airline (or flying in general) unsafe. What I take from it is a serious warning about the gap between fatigue policies and how pilots experience them in practice. #5932. The Locals' Guide to the Mexico City Grand Prix Weekend

Enrique del Bosque has attended his fair share of Formula 1 races around the world. From iconic circuits like the Autodromo Nazionale Monza in Italy—the famed home of Team Ferrari—to newer racetracks in Las Vegas and Lusail City in Qatar, the Mexican content creator with the largest F1-related TikTok following in Latin America has been a guest at plenty of paddocks and VIP parties.

Mexico City is no stranger to larger-than-life sporting events. Earlier this summer, the football-crazed capital hosted the 2026 World Cup. Online and elsewhere, fans raved about the experience, highlighting not just the sporting event itself but also Mexico City’s overall appeal as a host city, from its gastronomic attractions to its welcoming atmosphere for out-of-towners.

The Mexico City Grand Prix has become a much-anticipated race on the Formula 1 circuit.

The country’s storied motorsport lineage can be traced to 1963, when Formula 1 originally added a Grand Prix in Mexico’s capital. After an on-again, off-again run, it returned for good in 2015 after a complete rebrand, and is under contract until at least 2028.

This year will mark Pérez’s first time back at Mexico City’s Autódromo Hermanos Rodríguez since 2024, and on Wednesday morning, fans will gather to see him drive through Paseo de la Reforma, a popular thoroughfare that courses out from the gates of Chapultepec Park, in his 1,000 horsepower Cadillac.

#5930. American Airlines Keeps Selling Upgrades Until Boarding, So Why Keep Chasing Elite Status?

Major U.S. carriers are continuing to sell discounted premium cabin upgrades until shortly before boarding while their elite members wait for those same seats. Today, I want to take a closer look at the practice, with particular focus on American Airlines. I understand why AA or any other carrier wants the money, but this is a perfect example of why I now buy the flight I want rather than organizing my travel around earning airline elite status and foolishly hoping for the illusory upgrade benefits that accompany it.

View From The Wing highlights an example of American continuing to offer paid upgrades close to boarding, even with high-ranking elite members waiting for those seats. With just 28 minutes before boarding, AA was still selling upgrades from Philadelphia to Los Angeles for $242, a pretty good deal for six-hour flight.

That sparked a discussion on reddit with many other stories. One Executive Platinum member bought an upgrade on a New York LaGuardia–Dallas flight despite being third on the upgrade list, moving ahead of the two passengers above him. Another traveler flying New York–Delhi paid $640 to move from economy to business class shortly before boarding. He had been waiting for a systemwide upgrade for two months and was third on the list with one seat remaining.

Each example illustrate the same practical problem: a passenger can spend months waiting for an upgrade earned through loyalty, only to see the seat sold shortly before departure.

These are individual passenger accounts, not an announcement of a new American policy. But American’s published upgrade rules do not promise anything and as the carrier seeks to return to profitability, it is hawking upgrades aggressively and in my experience at much better rates than Alaska Airlines, United Airlines, or Delta Air Lines.

I have no fundamental objection to American selling an otherwise empty first class seat. A passenger waiting for a complimentary upgrade has only a conditional benefit. Status does not confer ownership of an unsold seat, and American has never promised that every Executive Platinum member will sit up front.

But that does not settle whether pursuing the status is worthwhile.

Airlines encourage us to concentrate our flying and credit card spending with them. Choose our flight, even if another airline has a better schedule. Use our card. Reach the next threshold. Earn the certificates. Enjoy the recognition. I call it a hamster wheel.

Then, when it comes time to deliver one of the primary benefits associated with status, the airline still tries to find someone willing to pay cash for the seat and not just until the upgrade windows open, but until boarding begins!

That is a perfectly understandable commercial decision. At the same time, it also gives customers a perfectly understandable reason to reconsider their own spending.

If an occasional traveler can buy an attractive upgrade without spending the year chasing status, perhaps that traveler has figured out the better approach.

I would certainly consider a reasonably priced upgrade myself. In fact, I often do that instead of buying first outright because the coach + instant paid upgrade cost saves me money. I’m not so idealistic that I would decline paying for an upgrade out of solidarity with the people above me on the list. My concern here is with the calculation that persuaded those passengers to spend so much pursuing their place on that list in the first place.

Recently I was chatting with a friend who flew from Des Moines to Denver to Dallas on United rather than fly nonstop from Des Moines to Dallas on United. He’s on the hamster wheel and I knows it, but I realize the chase of status can be a powerful aphrodisiac itself. I’m here, though, to tell you that it’s not worthwhile.

For the last two years, I have been a free agent. I still enjoy flying United and it receives plenty of my business. But I have flown other airlines much more this year because directing all my travel toward another year of Premier 1K simply did not make sense.

If I want first class, I buy first class. Sometimes that means purchasing it outright. Often an upgrade offer makes sense, my most frequent route to the forward cabin. Ironically, I’ve received more complimentary upgrades as a lowly Advantage Gold (lowest tier) on American than I do on United as Premier Gold. In an any case, I can compare a concrete price with a concrete benefit rather than pay more throughout the year and hope the benefit eventually appears.

Of course, status still has value. Extra-legroom seating, baggage allowances, priority assistance, and help during irregular operations can be useful, even as an AA Gold. Alliance benefits can be valuable when traveling internationally. For someone whose normal travel earns status without changing purchasing decisions, there may be little downside to chasing it.

But someone taking an inconvenient connection, paying a higher fare, or diverting substantial credit card spending needs to account for those costs. The possibility of an upgrade should be valued according to how often it actually happens, not how on a theoretical basis.

Systemwide upgrades require the same scrutiny. A certificate can be extremely valuable when it clears. Spending more to earn one that remains unused or leaves you buying the upgrade anyway is a much less compelling proposition.

I would rather keep the money and choose the best flight. Trust me, it’s liberating.

American has every reason to sell its premium seats, including through last-minute upgrade offers. But passengers have every reason to question how much extra loyalty makes sense when those sales keep taking priority over the upgrades they have been working toward and that have been dangled at them like a carrot on a stick.

I do not begrudge the passenger who buys his way past the upgrade list. Increasingly, I do the same thing on both American and United.

Bottom line: choose the schedule, fare, and seat that work for you. If status follows, wonderful. I would be very careful about paying substantially more for an illusory hope.

#5924. United Airlines Would Rather Leave Seats Empty Than Sell Them Too Cheaply

United Airlines is willing to accept more empty seats if higher fares produce a better financial result. That may frustrate passengers wondering why a flight with plenty of availability still costs so much, but it is a useful reminder that filling an aircraft and making money are not the same thing.

One more tidbit from United’s October 21 earnings call last week. Chief Commercial Officer Andrew Nocella explained that the airline expects its efforts to recover higher fuel costs through pricing to result in somewhat lower load factors.

Load factor measures the percentage of available seat miles occupied by paying passengers. In essence, it tells us how full the airline’s flights are.

“We do not manage to a fixed load factor target.”

Instead, he explained, United focuses on revenue relative to capacity and its long-term profitability. Here’s his full statement:

“Look, I think it’s obvious where the price of fuel is. It’s obvious where our total CASM is. It’s also, therefore, mathematically obvious what yields need to do. Do I expect there to be some impacts on load factor? There was a tiny bit in Q3. There will probably be a little bit more in Q4.

“But we know where yields need to be for United. We are taking all the appropriate actions to get them there so we can recover 100% of fuel. I do expect that that means slightly lower load factors. I will just reiterate, we do not manage to a fixed load factor target. That’s not how it works. It’s not how it should work.

“We manage to maximize our RASM and our long-term margin goal, and if load factors fall, they are incredibly high, by the way, relative to all of the past in my experience in this business. That’s perfectly okay.”

He had already offered an example: leisure revenue increased 16% in the third quarter while leisure load factor declined 1.3 percentage points. United was comfortable with that tradeoff.

This does not mean United plans to stop discounting or would reject a profitable additional passenger. It does mean management is willing to lose some price-sensitive business if the higher fares paid by the remaining passengers more than compensate.

There’s certainly an argument to be made for selling every last seat. The aircraft is going anyway. The crew is being paid. Once the door closes, an empty seat produces nothing. Why not sell it cheaply and collect at least some revenue?

Sometimes that makes sense. But an airline also has to consider what happens before departure, when it does not yet know who will buy.

Imagine a simplified example, ignoring differences in flight length and the additional costs of carrying each passenger. Selling 150 seats at an average fare of $200 produces $30,000. Selling 140 at an average fare of $230 produces $32,200.

The second flight has more empty seats and more revenue.

Of course, United cannot simply raise every fare and assume nearly everyone will keep buying. The difficult part is figuring out how many passengers will accept the higher price, how many will choose another airline, and how many will stay home.

A cheaper ticket might attract someone who otherwise would not travel. It might also go to someone who would have paid substantially more. If passengers become confident that an airline will slash fares shortly before departure, some will learn to wait (something, by the way, I notice with premium fares at many carriers, though much less likely with economy fares).

Thus, the decision is not always between selling one seat cheaply and leaving it empty. It can involve the effect of that cheap seat on what other customers are willing to pay long before the flight departs.

Without understanding the precise systems running forecasters, I have to imagine that AI has made the ability to predict loads more sharper than ever.

As I’ve been arguing often lately, loyalty is a two-way street and passengers must make their own calculation when considering which carrier or route to fly.

An expensive flight with open seats is not necessarily a pricing mistake and an airline is not obligated to offer a last-minute bargain simply because its seat map looks empty, which is itself an imperfect indication of how many tickets have been sold.

But United should also recognize that customers have alternatives.

I enjoy flying United and appreciate its investments, particularly Starlink. Nevertheless, I compare schedules and prices. A better product can justify paying more, but there is a limit to what I will pay for it, especially becuase American Airlines and Delta Air Lines also have functional free Wi-Fi on most domestic flights.

A traveler who skips one expensive trip may return for the next one. A traveler who tries another airline and has a perfectly good experience may start questioning why he was so committed to United in the first place (raising hand).

That is harder to capture in the revenue calculation for a single flight.

I am not suggesting United has overlooked this: Nocella and his team are very smart. Pricing is considerably more sophisticated than looking at a seat map and choosing a number. But I again caution United to be careful about assuming that customers who accept higher fares today will keep doing so indefinitely.

But generally speaking, I also don’t bet (ever) that the price of a trip will drop. If the fare is too high, I look at other flights, dates, or airports. Sometimes I decide the trip is not worth taking.

United can decide what it wants to charge. I can decide whether the flight is worth buying.

United is comfortable accepting somewhat lower occupancy if higher fares generate better revenue and profitability. I understand the logic, even though we would all rather find a cheap ticket on a flight with plenty of open seats.

For passengers, the practical lesson is that unsold seats do not guarantee an approaching sale. Waiting for a bargain can work, but United is telling us that filling every seat is not its objective. As United continues to try to recoup its much higher fuel expenses, I would not be surprised to see it at least try more aggreqievsly to charge more for seats, even at the expense of load factors.
