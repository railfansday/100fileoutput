#2590. Opinion: The morality of football

Yesterday evening, Selecao correspondent Nathan Motz accepted a unique opportunity to appear as a guest on the Moral Maze – a BBC radio broadcast dealing with matters of moral and ethical significance.

Is football a moral force for good? That was the question of the day, and each of the four guests were asked to discuss various aspects of the debate from financial fairplay to club takeovers by foreign entities to racism.

Nathan’s segment was concerned with whether football has transcendent moral value, and what that is exactly. Is there a greater significance to the game we are watching, or is it merely 22 humans kicking a leather ball into a net?

Join Nathan as he elaborates on the most significant takeaways of what was a fascinating discussion on the sport and morality in general.

Football, for some, may be the most poignant reminder of the moral universe in which we live. While debate has raged for centuries regarding the precise origin of moral absolutes and how humans ought to live, it is simply unavoidable to say humanity is often troubled by events surrounding the game we all love so dearly.

In not so distant history, every normal act of human life would have been assigned some degree of moral significance. Every action had inherently moral indicators - for good or for evil. Today, postmodern views of morality dominate. These describe morality as an entirely subjective dimension. In other words, it cannot be pinned down exactly whether behaviors are objectively good or bad. We can only find fault with someone else’s interpretation based on their own unique moral code. Morality is relative to the individual or society.

Yet last night’s dialogue concerning football and human behavior was filled with moral accusations and claims which strongly suggested humans are not content with relativist morality. Indeed, we are at times frightened by and disgusted with what we observe in and around football. Our sense of moral justice is deeply disturbed. But with respect to what exactly?

In responding to the panelist’s questions, I observed a familiar paradigm. Each time I have discussed any aspect of moral behavior, the speculation humans are good or at worst morally neutral animals continually reemerges. This widely accepted presupposition is then followed shortly by disgust at some aspect of human behavior. Almost as if to ask “why are all these good humans behaving so badly?” while ignoring the very real implications of this line of inquiry. A question rendered unanswerable by the insistent a priori assumptions from which it was derived.

If humans are intrinsically beneficent, conscientious, fair, humble, and honest then why is football rife with racism, misogyny, greed, disenfranchisement, lies, cheating, violence, and the hypocrisy to deny the occurrence of any such behaviors except when a rival club is the guilty party? Behaviors which are perpetrated by individual supporters, fanatical groups, club administrations, corporate bodies such as FIFA, political entities, nations, and even the players themselves. No one participating faction is guiltless.

To so blithely sidestep more sufficient explanations for the empirical reality of wickedness in football and in human behavior more generally is not just factually incorrect. It undermines real attempts to understand the root of these behaviors where they might be dealt with more effectively.

Furthermore, in good faith, how successful is a response against heinous evil when those perpetrating it are regarded as behaving in accord with their unique moral ambitions? Humans refuse to accept their indisputably objective moral lacking at a fundamental level. This restricts us from ever doing any more than “stamping out” the alleged impropriety of others – while we ourselves may readily engage in these very same behaviors. Moral relativism cannot account for the moral outrage emanating from all corners of the football world. Is Qatar wrong to perpetrate human rights atrocities or is it allowable because their culture is indifferent? Is hooliganism objectively wrong, or only when someone else’s club is responsible?

The overlooking of our own failings while lashing out at others for theirs is quintessential to the morally fallen nature of humanity itself. Football tells all. There are not good people and bad people. There are only people who both willfully and unintentionally transgress the moral code embedded in each of our hearts. A moral law which can only have ever been given by a moral Lawgiver. Otherwise, it is truly your word against mine as it pertains to the moral validity of all behaviors - even those which may deeply violate your conscience.

Football generates an episodic, semantic, and imagistic description of universal archetypes: good and evil, sacrifice and greed, cruelty and mercy, hero and anti-hero. It speaks to every arc of life’s plotline: tragedy, comedy, victory, defeat, the finite nature of our circumstances. It is one of the preeminent existential dialects alongside religion, visual art, written and spoken language, music, and theatre. Football plucks a primal chord.

As metaphysics have been curtailed to just physics in the modern secular West, football has only elevated our awareness of the often dire moral predicaments of our species. Yet it is clear the moral conversation does not originate on a football pitch. The pitch is but a grand stage upon which moral themes may be observed and critiqued, a mirror held in front of our own soul.

The desire for ultimate purpose, for meaning beyond the material realm, never disappeared in modernity. Arguably, it has only become more desperate as the outcry for ultimate significance comes under more scrutiny and criticism by those who constrain ontological explanations to those accounted for by physics. The severity of the mental health crisis in the West is in no small part explained by the terrifying existential awareness we are cut adrift in an abstract sea of meaningless, hopeless wandering between two oblivions - that before mortal life began and that at mortal life’s end. Let football be one voice which reassures you - there is ultimate meaning and good reasons why football so unsettles our craving for justice.

In a palpable, unavoidable way, football and supporter communities across Europe are encountering moral quandaries. In Portugal, age-old debates between FC Porto and Benfica have routinely been haunted by, in many cases, farcical and cynical hypocrisy. Throughout its history, the FPF has been susceptible to showing partiality to a club, player, or style which ultimately degrades the performance of our beloved Seleção. Deny it all we like, and not one aspect of this reality is changed.

In conclusion, whatever your thoughts or beliefs on the subject of moral significance, its origin, the meaning of life, and how football draws that out, I hope it will be beneficial to examine these matters more deeply. In my zeal, I admit I have not always been prudent to initiate these discussions in a way which respects thoughtful disagreement. It is my hope this effort to bring that discussion to Portuguese football will be well-received and carefully pondered. But I will contentedly listen to those followers who may be genuinely unhappy or unsupportive of this or any other idea about which I write.

The readers of this website and the larger Portuguese football loving world are my people. It is a joy and a responsibility I take seriously whenever I have the opportunity to raise important questions about the Selecao, the game, and our lives in general.

#2527. GGFN Editorial | The case for giving Bayern Munich’s other Ballon d’Or contender, Klara Bühl, more recognition

There was a moment during Germany’s October 13 friendly against Japan at Bremen’s Weserstadion attended by Get German Football News that seemed to encapsulate everything that makes Klara Bühl such an exceptional footballer. Racing toward the corner flag in the 57th minute, the Bayern Munich winger somehow managed to stop a ball from going out of play with a deft back-heel before promptly nutmegging Japanese defender Yuzuki Yamamoto.

The sequence produced no goal or assist. It didn’t need to. The extraordinary combination of athleticism, determination, technical precision, and audacity was a reminder of just how much Bühl contributes beyond what appears on the scoresheet. Naturally, the 25-year-old also found time to provide both a goal and an assist as Germany came from behind to defeat Japan 2-1.

Bühl set up Linda Dallmann’s 28th-minute equalizer with a precise cutback after being played into space by Franziska Kett. Twelve minutes later, she narrowly missed scoring a spectacular goal herself, cutting inside from the left before curling an effort inches wide of the far post. After the interval, Bühl continued to torment the Japanese defense. Another pass from the winger presented Dallmann with an excellent opportunity in the 65th minute before she finally got her deserved goal eight minutes later.

Receiving a pass from Melissa Kössler, Bühl swept the ball into the bottom corner to complete Germany’s comeback. Her outstanding performance also earned her a standing ovation from the Bremen crowd. The match took place after the assessment period for the 2026 Women’s Ballon d’Or had concluded. It nevertheless provided another vivid illustration of the qualities that have made Bühl one of the most influential attacking players in European football.

Which brings us to Monday evening’s ceremony in London.

The 2026 Ballon d’Or ceremony, held at the London Palladium to mark the award’s 70th anniversary, saw Bayern Munich striker Harry Kane become the first Englishman since Michael Owen in 2001 to claim the men’s award. Spain’s Alexia Putellas collected her third Women’s Ballon d’Or, finishing ahead of Poland’s Ewa Pajor and Jamaica’s Khadija Shaw.

Bayern Munich had three representatives among the 30 nominees for the women’s award. Danish international Pernille Harder secured an impressive seventh-place finish, while Japanese international Momoko Tanikawa finished 23rd.

Bühl, meanwhile, placed 12th. Despite improving upon her 19th-place finish in 2025, the German international found herself outside the top ten, one place behind Manchester City’s Yui Hasegawa and two behind tenth-placed Temwa Chawinga.

There is no compelling reason to begrudge Alexia Putellas her third Ballon d’Or. The Spanish international enjoyed another outstanding campaign, helping Barcelona secure another Women’s Champions League title. But acknowledging Putellas’ achievements doesn’t mean we cannot question the final ranking.

How did Klara Bühl, after the strongest campaign of her career, fail to finish among the world’s ten best footballers? Bühl’s 2025/26 season was extraordinary by almost any statistical measure. She registered seven goals and a league-leading 15 assists in the Frauen-Bundesliga. Her contributions helped Bayern secure the domestic double.

The winger’s performances in the Champions League were arguably even more remarkable. Bühl finished the competition with eight assists, more than any other player in Europe. Even eventual Ballon d’Or winner Putellas, who registered seven, could not match the Bayern attacker’s tally.

Perhaps the most extraordinary example came against Arsenal, when Bühl assisted all three Bayern goals in a remarkable 3-2 comeback victory over the reigning European champions. Bayern ultimately advanced to the semi-finals of Europe’s premier club competition.

One cannot reasonably argue that Bühl’s accomplishments were restricted to the domestic game.

She demonstrated her quality against Europe’s strongest opposition, and she did so consistently.

Nor is this merely a case of one German journalist questioning an international award. In a October 27 Kicker commentary, Bernd Salamon argued that 12th place failed to adequately recognize Bühl’s exceptional campaign. The Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung‘s extensive feature on Bühl’s world-class abilities and Ballon d’Or candidacy also offers valuable context for appreciating the winger’s development.

The Ballon d’Or result was not the first occasion this year on which Bühl appeared to receive insufficient individual recognition. In August, the Bayern attacker finished second behind teammate Giulia Gwinn in Germany’s Footballer of the Year voting. What made that result particularly interesting was Gwinn’s own reaction to receiving the award.

The Germany captain – who has once again struggled with injury this year – acknowledged that other players had been more deserving of the distinction. When asked whom she would have selected, Gwinn explicitly named Bühl. Naturally, Germany’s Footballer of the Year award and the Ballon d’Or are entirely separate elections. Gwinn’s response still illustrates the remarkable esteem in which Bühl is held by those who train and play alongside her.

Indeed, the praise Bühl receives from her coaches is often even more emphatic.

Speaking after Germany’s victory over Japan, national team head coach Christian Wück offered an extraordinary assessment of his winger.

“Klara is an incredibly important factor in our game,” Wück told reporters. “She’s a cheerful character, very important for our chemistry off the pitch as well. For me, she’s a world-class player. I don’t know another player who is as strong with both feet as she is. She’s quick, strong in challenges, and possesses incredible determination when working against the ball. That’s what defines her.”

These observations help explain why Bühl’s statistical contributions, impressive as they are, tell only part of the story. Her acceleration and close control make her exceptionally difficult to defend against. Her ability to operate comfortably with either foot allows her to attack opponents in multiple ways.

She can beat defenders on the outside and deliver dangerous crosses, cut inside to shoot, or recognize a teammate’s movement and supply a perfectly weighted pass. Just as importantly, she works relentlessly without possession.

The aforementioned back-heel against Japan exemplified this determination. Plenty of attacking players might have allowed the ball to run out of play. Bühl not only retrieved it but immediately transformed the situation into another attacking opportunity.

Wück’s assessment also speaks to her importance away from the football pitch. Her infectious enthusiasm and commitment to her teammates help make her such an integral component of the national team.

Perhaps the fundamental problem is that Bühl’s particular form of excellence is not always the easiest to package into an individual award. Goalscorers attract attention through their finishing statistics. Players at Europe’s most internationally prominent clubs benefit from the visibility that comes with competing for the biggest trophies.

And individual moments of brilliance, however spectacular, can sometimes overshadow sustained excellence across an entire campaign. Bühl certainly produces spectacular moments. But her greatest quality may be the consistency with which she makes those around her better. Her movement creates space. Her passing creates opportunities. Her willingness to contribute defensively gives teammates greater freedom to attack.

These contributions deserve consideration alongside goals, trophies, and the more easily marketable aspects of individual footballing stardom. One might also question whether performances in the Frauen-Bundesliga receive the same degree of international attention as those in England or Spain.

The attention Bühl is attracting from abroad offers an interesting contrast to her Ballon d’Or ranking. She has been linked with a move to the Spanish women’s league. Under contract with Bayern through the end of the current season, there are plenty of clubs interested in potentially snapping up the former Freiburg academy product on a free.

Bühl’s estimated market value (according to Transfermarkt’s Soccerdonna portal) has risen from €1.1m to €1.6m over the course of the last six months. While her Ballon d’Or placement may not fully reflect her standing in the game, the growing interest in her services suggests that her quality is hardly going unnoticed. In the estimation of one of Germany’s most respected online publications, at least, Bühl is earning some deserved appreciation.

Her 12th-place finish doesn’t exactly constitute worthless recognition. It represented an improvement from 19th in 2025 and confirmed her growing international standing.

Improvement and adequate recognition are, obviously, two different things.

Ultimately, the Women’s Ballon d’Or remains a subjective award decided by journalists with differing perspectives on individual performances, achievements, and the relative strength of various competitions. Reasonable people can disagree about whether Bühl deserved to finish fifth, seventh, or ninth.

That isn’t the point. The point is that a footballer who led both the Frauen-Bundesliga and Women’s Champions League in assists, helped Bayern secure the domestic double, and consistently demonstrated world-class attacking ability deserved serious consideration for a place among the very best.

Watching Bühl against Japan in Bremen only reinforced that conviction.

The beautiful back-heel, the nutmeg, the relentless movement, the defence-splitting passes, and the winning goal were all reminders that footballing excellence cannot always be reduced to trophies or the number beside a player’s name on an awards list.

Wück sees it. Gwinn sees it. The supporters who rose to applaud Bühl in Bremen certainly saw it.

Perhaps the international voting community will eventually give her performances the recognition they deserve.

For now, Klara Bühl will have to settle for 12th place.

In this writer’s view, that simply wasn’t high enough.
