ChatGPT is cracking down on photorealistic, political deepfakes ahead of next week’s US midterm elections.

One of the biggest problems of the AI age is that AI deepfakes are now so realistic that they’re practically indistinguishable from real photos. That matters a lot during election season, when voters are looking for reliable, truthful information on their candidates and polling sites. OpenAI is implementing a new safeguard to prevent people from making political deepfakes.

The US midterm election is only a week away on Nov. 3. Tech issues are taking center stage in many races, especially as AI and data center concerns rise for folks across the country. (Check out CNET’s guide to spotting political scams.)

If you were to go into ChatGPT today and ask it to generate an image “depicting election interference or that may discourage voting,” as OpenAI put it in an Oct. 20 update, the chatbot would create the image, given it doesn’t violate any other content guidelines. But it will be in an illustrated or cartoon style — all requests to have the image appear photorealistic should be ignored.

For example, CNET asked ChatGPT to create an image of Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers standing outside a polling location. Every such prompt generated an image in a cartoon style. A photorealistic version of this kind of image would likely spark anxiety, if not outright panic, for voters.

The idea is to make it harder for bad actors to create realistic political deepfakes that could interfere with or question the integrity of an election without stymying people’s ability to create political content in general. In another example, CNET Managing Editor Jon Reed couldn’t trick ChatGPT into creating a photojournalist-style image that showed a voting machine recording the wrong choice.

“The problem with deepfakes isn’t social commentary, it’s deception. So our aim is to degrade the evidentiary value of an image, while preserving room for illustrations and social commentary,” Josh Lawson, OpenAI’s head of elections, told CNET. The goal is to “make our platform less useful to bad actors by adding another layer of friction,” he said.

This cartoonification of AI images is a practical way to make political deepfakes a little harder to create. Improving AI image models, like ChatGPT Images 2.5, is a double-edged sword. Newer models make fewer errors or hallucinations, making better content for creators. But removing those flubs makes it even harder for everyone else to identify AI-created content with the naked eye. Verification or AI labeling methods have tried to help, but those methods aren’t industry-wide nor foolproof.

When I asked Gemini maker Google about any precautions it is taking specifically for AI images, a spokesperson pointed me to its midterm election policy, which says the company is “rolling out additional protections to limit the creation of misleading election-related images” but doesn’t include any details on how.

Anthropic doesn’t have a traditional image or video generator, and a spokesperson told CNET that any attempts to use Claude to deceive voters or disrupt elections violate its policies. The company also posted this general election guidance earlier this month.

There’s no guarantee that OpenAI will keep this system in place once the election cycle is over. But it’s a solid sign from one of the biggest AI labs that it is taking its role in safeguarding American elections’ integrity at least somewhat seriously.

#3517. Intel to buy quantum software firm Classiq for about $2.3bn

The deal would let the chipmaker sell both the hardware and the software needed to run quantum computers.

Intel has agreed to buy Classiq, an Israeli startup that makes software for programming quantum computers.

The chipmaker announced the deal on Monday without giving a price, but it is worth about $2.3bn, Bloomberg reported, citing people familiar with the matter. Intel declined to comment on the figure.

Quantum computers use qubits, which can handle certain problems, such as simulating molecules, far faster than ordinary chips. But writing programs for them is hard and usually needs specialists.

Classiq tries to make that easier. Its platform lets developers describe what they want a program to do and then designs the quantum circuit for them, much as chip design software does for regular processors. It works across different types of quantum hardware, and customers include BMW and HSBC.

Intel has worked on quantum computing since 2015, building qubits on silicon with the same equipment it uses for ordinary chips.

Adding Classiq would give it what the industry calls a full stack: both the processors and the software to use them.

“Joining forces with Classiq transforms Intel’s Quantum Computing Group into a full-stack quantum computing business,” said Intel chief executive Lip-Bu Tan.

Intel said it would keep Classiq’s software working with other companies’ quantum hardware, and that Classiq would become its global development centre for quantum computing. The deal is expected to close in the first half of 2027, subject to regulatory approval.

Classiq was founded by chief executive Nir Minerbi, Amir Naveh and Yehuda Naveh. It has raised just over $200m and was valued at about $600m to $700m in 2025, Israeli outlet Calcalist reported, according to Ynet.

Tan was among Classiq’s investors, joining its 2022 and 2025 funding rounds. He did not take part in the deal talks because of that stake, Calcalist reported. Tan’s links to companies that do business with Intel have drawn attention before.

The purchase comes as Intel’s turnaround gathers pace. Its shares have nearly tripled this year, and in August it sold $15bn of stock to fund its chip factories.

If you've been a dedicated Arc browser user, the time has finally come to say goodbye. The Browser Company paused development of Arc in 2025, continuing only with security updates. As announced late last week, Arc will officially sunset on Dec. 22, 2026, giving users two months to transition their data to an alternative.

One option is to move to the company's new AI-focused Dia browser, though there are other (potentially better) choices depending on what you're looking for. Most data from Arc—including Spaces, Favorites, pinned tabs, and folders as well as passwords, bookmarks, extensions, and history—will transition automatically over to Dia, but if you're switching to a different platform entirely, you'll need to take what you can with you before Dec. 22.

Officially, Arc supports exporting Spaces and tabs only, and the resulting Export.md plain-text file is simply a list of links that you can open and bookmark in your new browser. MacOS users can initiate this process via Arc > Settings > Advanced > Export Data and saving the file to a preferred location. On Windows, open the Arc menu, select Settings, and go to Export Spaces and tabs > Export Data.

To simply copy and paste every link from one folder, right-click the folder, select Copy All Links, and paste them wherever you'd like (select Copy All Links as Markdown to keep link titles on macOS). You can also save Easels as images by tapping the share icon > Share Via... and selecting PNG.

There's no formal process to export other Arc data. If you saved logins in Arc, now is a good time to start moving credentials over to a different platform. (Our top picks for password managers aren't tied to a single browser.)

There are a few alternatives to Arc's official export process, which is highly manual and doesn't keep your folder structure intact. There are several third-party scripts available (for free) that can facilitate the transition as well as a paid extension called ArcEscape that can export folders and pinned tabs and import into another browser. The extension is available for Chrome, Edge, Brave, Vivaldi, and Firefox. According to Arc's FAQ, all user accounts will be deactivated on Dec. 22, and personal data will be deleted within 30 days of deactivation.

#3511. 4 drawbacks you should know before buying an Xbox Series X

The Xbox Series X can still provide solid value, but you should understand what you're getting six years in.

This has been a pretty grim console generation for Microsoft, between hard-to-swallow Game Pass price hikes, upsetting studio closures and all those Halo Infinite "Craig the Brute" memes. You can expect an Xbox Series X to last years if you look after it properly, but should you even buy one in the first place?

From here, the service started to get less appealing.

There's also $14.99 Game Pass Premium, which gives you access to over 200 titles playable across Xbox Series X|S, PCs and cloud devices. However, neither of these include day-one releases; you need Ultimate to play titles like the upcoming Fable reboot on launch day.

If you play enough, you can still get your money's worth from Game Pass. I'm not going to pay $70 for Forza Horizon 6 when I can experience it with a subscription and jump to something else when I'm not feeling it anymore.

For hardcore gamers, there have always been compelling reasons to own a capable Windows gaming PC over an Xbox Series X. That's because a powerful rig is currently the best way to experience Xbox Series X games.

Forget overpaying for a six-year-old console. Providing you own a decent gaming laptop or PC, you can play over 300 titles with PC Game Pass for just $13.99 per month. This includes day-one Microsoft exclusives like Gears of War: E-Day. The PC and Xbox Game Pass libraries don't match exactly, but you'll almost certainly find something you're interested in playing. Very few games are truly console-exclusive — and for anything not on Xbox, Steam sales are common.

That means in open-world games that are often CPU-intensive, you don't typically see big FPS bumps from the Pro console.

#3510. Introducing LFM3: Every Modality for Specialized Intelligence

Today, we’re excited to announce LFM3, our new generation of Liquid Foundation Models (LFMs) built for specialized intelligence. The LFM3 model family is multimodal by nature and brings text, vision, audio, omni, and decision models onto one new shared backbone. The first release of the LFM3 family covers models in the 2B parameter range.

Built on a new hybrid architecture and 22T tokens of pre-training, LFM3 is far more expressive than LFM2 while maintaining its efficiency. The extra capacity in a small, fast model makes LFM3 the ideal starting point in its class for specialized intelligence: this is because it can be fine-tuned locally, it can run on any device, and it is capable enough to become a reliable expert on a specialized task.

The base and post-trained models are open-weight and available today on Hugging Face, our Playground, and the Liquid AI API. Check out our docs on how to run and fine-tune them locally.

LFM3 instances are ideal candidates for specialized intelligence. Fine-tune them on your own data, and deploy them where your application runs. Enjoy the article.

LFM2 showed that a hybrid of short convolutions and attention could run fast on-device without sacrificing quality. Its design came from our automated hardware-in-the-loop architecture search. For LFM3, we used this search to redesign that architecture for agentic use cases, which need more capability per parameter and fast inference over long contexts.

We also expanded the hardware in the loop, from CPUs in one phone and one laptop for LFM2 to 14 device and backend combinations for LFM3: phones (Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra and OnePlus 15), a Raspberry Pi 5, laptops (Apple M4 Max and AMD Ryzen AI), and Arm and x86 cloud CPUs, plus GPUs and the Qualcomm Hexagon NPU.

Gated short convolutions still make up over half the layers. To add capability without slowing down long contexts, we tried swapping some of them for other sequence blocks, and sliding-window attention (SWA) gave the best quality–speed tradeoff. We kept the other changes described below only where they improved quality without a meaningful speed cost. The payoff shows at long context. Per parameter, LFM3-1.7B matches LFM2-1.2B on short prompts across edge devices, and with 8K tokens in context it is about 25% faster.

Tri-hybrid layer mix. LFM3 combines three operators: gated short convolutions, sliding-window attention (SWA), and global attention. Of its 24 layers, 13 are convolutions, 5 are SWA with a 256-token window, and 6 are global attention. The convolutions also use a kernel size of 7, up from 3 in LFM2.

Head-wise gated attention. A sigmoid gate scales each head’s output, so a head can switch off when it has nothing to add. This can help suppress activation outliers that hurt quantization and improve training stability.

Partial RoPE. Global attention layers rotate only the highest-frequency half of each head and leave the low-frequency half unrotated, so those dimensions match on content regardless of distance. SWA layers keep full RoPE.

Sandwich norm. Each block normalizes both its input and its output, which helps keep activations well-scaled.

LFM3 uses the same 128K vocabulary as LFM2.5-8B-A1B, built to tokenize non-Latin scripts more efficiently.

Training. LFM3-1.7B-Base was pre-trained on 22T tokens. Compared with LFM2, the mix adds more scientific papers, repository-level code, a wider range of knowledge sources, and considerably more math. The final 3.5T tokens are a mid-training stage on curated, higher-quality data, with a larger share of agentic traces and diverse tool calls to prepare the model for agentic post-training. This stage also extends the context window to 256K tokens.

#3507. S&P 500 Sell-Off: Is Now the Right Time to Buy the Dip?

The stock market looks a bit wobbly. After reaching a new record high on Oct. 6, the S&P 500 index (^GSPC +0.60%) has declined by about 2.5% since then (as of this writing).

On the one hand, a 2.5% drop in the S&P 500 can be unnerving to some investors. It might be a sign of economic trouble ahead. What if the stock market plunges even further into a 10% correction or a 20% bear market?

On the other hand, many investors might see a 2.5% price decline in the S&P 500 as a sign that "stocks are on sale" and that it's time to buy more shares of S&P 500 exchange-traded funds (ETFs). This is called "buying the dip" -- investing more money in the stock market when the overall market has "dipped" to lower levels.

Here's the thing: If you can patiently leave your stock portfolio alone for the next several years, asking if it's time to "buy the dip" is the wrong question. For consistent, long-term investors, there is rarely a bad time to invest.

Here's why patient, consistent, "boring" long-term investing is often more lucrative than waiting for the perfect moment to swoop in and "buy the dip."

The S&P 500 has delivered average annual returns of 9.98% since 1928. That's a remarkably long-term track record of wealth-building returns despite the massive stock market crashes, global wars, and short-term economic crises along the way. The stock market tends to keep chugging along. Companies make money and pass on some of those earnings to shareholders. Stocks go up.

The past few years for the S&P 500 have been even better than that long-term 98-year average. In the past 10 years, the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO +0.58%) has delivered annualized returns of 15.3%. In the 16 years since the fund's inception, it has delivered annualized returns of 14.94%.

If you just bought shares of VOO and held onto them for five, 10, or 16 years, you would've earned significant returns. Even if you thought you were investing at a "bad time" or the "best time" on any given day.

When the S&P 500 is down by a few percent in a month, it's easy to think that "maybe I'll just wait to invest until stocks go down even more. Then I'll buy more shares at a lower price." But this is called market timing, and it doesn't work.

Research from Schwab found that, based on 20 years of S&P 500 returns from 2005 to 2024, someone who invested $2,000 per year as early as possible each year during those 20 years would have earned almost as high a return as someone who invested $2,000 per year at exactly "the right time," on the day of each year when S&P 500 levels were lowest.

Even if you perfectly "buy the dip" on the S&P 500 (which is impossible to do consistently), you still might only slightly outperform the returns you'd get from steady investing over many years.

Don't worry about buying the dip or timing the market or picking the perfect short-term moment to invest when the S&P 500 is volatile. Just focus on your long-term plans. Buy and hold a diversified portfolio of great stocks, and let the market work for you.

#3474. Python's experimental JIT to be removed

Python's experimental just-in-time (JIT) compiler, which was added to CPython in 2024 for the 3.13 release, is on its way out. In June, the Python steering council announced that it was pausing work on the JIT in CPython's main branch until there was an accepted Python Enhancement Proposal (PEP) that addressed concerns about maintenance, compatibility, and set clear success metrics as well as a timeline for completion. After creating PEP 836 ("JIT Go Brrr: The Path to a Supported JIT Compiler for CPython") to try to meet the council's request, and after months of discussion, the JIT maintainers have withdrawn the PEP. For now, it appears that development on the once-promising project has come to an end.

The JIT was added to CPython by Brandt Bucher in early 2024. Generally, such a major feature would be expected to land only after the council had approved a standards-track PEP. Bucher had given a talk (YouTube video, slides) about the JIT at a Python core developer sprint, and announced his intent to merge the feature—but he had not taken the step of authoring or submitting a PEP for its approval. He was prodded into doing so by steering council member Thomas Wouters, who was also serving as the release manager for Python 3.13.

Bucher and Savannah Ostrowski drafted PEP 744 ("JIT Compilation"), which was an informational PEP—meaning it only provides information to the Python community and does not represent formal consensus to proceed on a feature or project. LWN covered the discussion of PEP 744 in April 2024, and later covered Bucher's talk on the JIT at PyCon US 2024, as well as the follow-up talk at PyCon US 2025.

Work on the JIT had been proceeding on the basis of PEP 744 until this June, when the council had put forward its formal request for the JIT maintainers to create a standards-track PEP. One might wonder, as Antoine Pitrou did, why the council had decided that it was time to speak up and ask for a new PEP. Wouters said that there was no "single triggering event for this request", but that the council had been discussing the topic for a while and indicated that it was looking to ensure the JIT work did not interfere with other projects. "There's been work trying to get the JIT to play nice with instrumentation, debugging, and profiling. There's also external JITs (like CinderX), JIT-adjacent things (like Numba and PyTorch's compile), and discussions about a tier 3 JIT." The council had waited until after the Python 3.15 beta release "to be clear that we're OK with the status quo for 3.15".

The council asked for a PEP that would make the case for the JIT "as a supported, non-experimental part of CPython: its guarantees, its maintenance commitments, and its impact on redistributors". The council set a deadline of six months for the PEP to be submitted and accepted; failing that, the council would require the JIT code to be removed from the CPython main branch.

Bucher, Ostrowski, and Ken Jin drafted PEP 836 and published it on the Python forum for community feedback on July 3. It generated quite a bit of discussion, some favorable and some critical. One of the key themes in the comments, such as this one by Wouters, and this comment by Mark Shannon, was that the draft felt like it should be two separate PEPs. Shannon, for example, thought that the PEP should only cover goals for accepting the JIT work and felt that it should leave out implementation details. There were also quite a few calls for additional information.

Bucher pushed back a bit on the suggestion that it should be two PEPs, and Ostrowski defended the structure of the PEP as it was. "Outlining goals like we have in the PEP without being able to share the plan and everything we've thought through alongside it would make me uncomfortable. To me, goals without plans behind them aren't really goals at all."

When we last looked at this topic in September, the discussion about the PEP had slowed down without a commitment to develop new PEPs or a formal statement from the council. The last word from the council at that time, in the form of a summary of its August 6 meeting, was that it had met with the PEP authors and was "discussing the state of the PEP and whether it answered the questions the SC asked in its request for a PEP".

On September 21, Wouters posted a statement from the steering council, minus Ostrowski (who is currently a member of the steering council but did not participate since she is a co-author of the PEP), that summarized its position. The statement said that PEP did answer all of the questions that were highlighted in the council's request, "although perhaps some less convincingly than others".

The council wished to make clear that it was not looking for the criteria that the JIT should meet to be a supported feature, or whether it should be considered such. Instead, the council was evaluating whether the JIT should continue as an experiment in CPython's main branch.

The council said that the PEP was not specific enough about support plans, performance criteria, and too vague or too optimistic about a number of other areas. The statement included a ten-point list of items that the council wanted "to see either spelled out in the PEP, or at least discussed and rejected". The council's list expressed concerns ranging from memory impact to security. For example, on the topic of platform parity, the council wanted to ensure that the JIT was designed to be easily adopted for new platforms. It wanted assurance that the JIT's design would not be tied to specific platform or require platform-specific knowledge to maintain.

Daylight saving time ends this weekend, with the clocks officially changing at 2 a.m. on Sunday. While it’ll still be light enough for trick-or-treating on Halloween, the time change will hit the day after, when most of the US adjusts back one hour.

The time shift is notorious for disrupting sleep patterns and schedules. Some politicians are advocating for the abolition of time changes and the permanent adoption of daylight saving time, or DST. The House passed the Sunshine Protection Act in July, but it still needs Senate approval and the president’s signature to become law — meaning that, for now, we still have dark winter hours coming.

If you, too, dislike how early it gets dark each day through winter, just remember that brighter evenings will return in mid-March.

Daylight saving time in the US ends at 2 a.m. local time on Sunday. It will return at 2 a.m. local time on March 14, 2027. The end of DST is when we “fall back,” whereas in March, we “spring forward.”

The US isn’t alone in observing daylight saving time. Check out this list of other countries that use DST, along with their start and end dates.

The official US time website shows where time zone boundaries are located.

The exact start dates for daylight saving time and standard time float a little. DST kicks off on the second Sunday of March and ends on the first Sunday of November with the return of standard time.

We spend about eight months of the year in DST. We can thank the Uniform Time Act of 1966 for bringing some order to what had been a complicated history of time changes in the US.

“Motivated by transportation improvements, this act mandated standard time within the existing time zones and established a permanent system of uniform DST, including the dates and times for twice-yearly transitions,” the Bureau of Transportation Statistics states in a history of time zones.

The country’s time zones date to the railroad boom of the late 1800s. DST formally entered the chat in 1918, but its application was inconsistent until 1966. The country tried year-round daylight saving time in January 1974 under President Richard Nixon to address the energy crisis. It didn’t go over well. Congress and President Gerald Ford restored standard time in October 1974.

If you truly hate time changes, then consider moving to Arizona (except for the Navajo Nation) or Hawaii. Those states are on standard time all year long, and their residents don’t have to deal with the physical and mental health impacts of the change. Puerto Rico, Guam, the Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands also skip DST.

Organizations such as the National Sleep Foundation, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Society for Research on Biological Rhythms are united in calling for permanent standard time, saying it’s better for human biology.

“There’s a mismatch between the outside world and our internal clocks during daylight saving time that can result in some negative health consequences,” says Joseph Dzierzewski, senior vice president of research and scientific affairs at the National Sleep Foundation.

Dzierzewski cites mental health concerns and upticks in cardiovascular events and drowsy driving. He also calls out safety issues with young children walking to school or sitting at bus stops in the dark. Those safety issues were a big reason why permanent DST didn’t last in 1974.

Time changes aren’t popular. An October 2024 YouGov poll (PDF) of more than 1,100 US adults found that 63% would like to see the changing of the clocks eliminated. Only 17% wanted to keep time changes, and 20% weren’t sure.

Efforts to end the time changes have so far failed to pass into law. The bipartisan Sunshine Protection Act, which would have made daylight saving time permanent, passed the Senate in 2022 — the new version of the bill passed the House earlier this year and was sent on to the Senate to review in July.

While many people agree on eliminating time changes, the schism between daylight saving time and standard time remains. Standard time may have a bit of an image issue compared to DST.

“Part of the issue is that people associate daylight saving time with summer. People love summer, right?” Dzierzewski said. “But the simple fact of the matter is, it would still be summer if we were on permanent standard time.”

President Donald Trump criticized time changes in a 2024 Truth Social post: “The Republican Party will use its best efforts to eliminate Daylight Saving Time, which has a small but strong constituency, but shouldn’t! Daylight Saving Time is inconvenient and very costly to our Nation.”

Bills and resolutions at the state level haven’t had real-world impacts yet. Politicians are still split on whether they want permanent daylight saving or standard time. Dzierzewski is hopeful that permanent standard time will win out.

“This is the first time in several years where I’ve seen more legislation introduced at a state level in support of permanent standard time as the solution,” he said.

If you’d like to see time changes end and want to advocate for either daylight saving or standard time, contact your state and national representatives and express your opinion.

Daylight saving time is happening, so now it’s a matter of figuring out how to deal with it. It’s not just about the day of the time change.

“You’re in a better situation to cope with the effects of the clock change if you have good sleep health generally,” Dzierzewski said. He recommends exposure to bright light in the morning, physical activity during the day, eating meals at regular times and practicing a relaxing wind-down routine at night.

The majority of Americans struggle to get the National Sleep Foundation’s recommended 7 to 9 hours of sleep for most adults. A CNET survey found that more than half of US adults use some method to cope with sleep-related challenges. Check out some expert-backed tips for improving your sleep quality.

“If you’re doing these healthy sleep behaviors, you’re probably more resilient to some of these changes,” Dzierzewski said. “If you have poor sleep to begin with, you’re probably going to feel it a little bit more.”

Establishing a better sleep routine can take time. There are two things you can do in direct response to the time change. Dzierzewski suggests adjusting your bedtime and wake-up time gradually ahead of the change. For example, you could shift your schedule by 15 minutes per day and ease yourself into DST. He also recommends getting a good dose of bright morning light on the day of the time change to help set your internal body clock.

Think of DST as another kind of wake-up call, one that can motivate you to evaluate your sleep routine and quality and make positive changes. Start with these six simple habits. And don’t forget to change your clocks. Do it the night before, and you won’t have to worry about it later.

#3447. The reason why some USB ports are orange

An orange USB port is an indicator, but not set in stone by industry standardization.

Take a look at some of the USB ports throughout your home. If you notice any orange ones, congratulations: there's a chance you have an "always-on" charging option. With these, the USB ports provide power through the power supply or internal battery even if the computer is shut off.

"Chance" is used rather loosely, however, because like many USB colors and symbols, orange in a USB port isn't a matter of industry standardization. There is no electronics rule that defines orange as a port that will charge your phone even if you turn off the computer (or whatever device the port is part of).

The color typically appears on the plastic insert inside a rectangular USB-A socket. A few manufacturers describe orange ports as "always-on," but some of these also support fast USB data transfers. Orange USB ports are often associated with USB 3.0, offering up to 5 Gbps speeds. But since it's not a universal spec, you should view an orange port as a power-related feature that requires confirmation.

This is further complicated by the fact that a manufacturer can highlight a special feature or function without promising a particular charging wattage. Or you might have an orange USB port that doesn't offer 5 Gbps speeds, yet still charges your phone when the power source is off.

Since orange essentially acts as a visual cue that a USB port is not standard, it all boils down to the manufacturer. Break out the computer or accessory's documentation (or check its online specs page), as that will be much more useful than a color chart. Intel and Advantech are good examples of two manufacturers using the same orange insert to highlight entirely different capabilities.

Where Intel is concerned, an orange USB port is an enhanced charging connection on some NUC PCs. Intel specifies up to 1.5 amps from that port, compared with 900 milliamps from the blue ports on those models. Meanwhile, Advantech uses orange ports as high-retention USB connectors, meaning the socket holds on to the plug really tightly. The withdrawal force required is 50 percent greater than a conventional USB connector.

In most color chart comparisons, both orange and blue USB ports use USB 3.0, which supports data transfer rates up to 5.0 Gbps. Given those specs, orange diverges from blue in the "always-on" category alone. Moving a USB cable from a blue to an orange port shouldn't make any difference unless you turn the PC or accessory off and need to charge something.

Blue isn't fully standardized either. According to USB-IF, it's the recommended color for SuperSpeed (USB 3.0, 5 Gbps) USB-A connectors. Even with that recommendation, it never hurts to double-check the specifications for each port individually, especially for non-standard USB colors like purple. Labels can help, but aren't always immediately visible or present at all.

The connected device and cable are important too: not every accessory can match a port's advertised maximum, and the cable has to support those capabilities as well. When you need fast charging, make sure the orange USB port in question is capable. When you need fast transfers, make sure that same orange port isn't going to be a bottleneck. An orange port will offer both in many cases, but you can't tell from the color alone.

This new tool proves it's still best to limit the info you share with chatbots.

Researchers at Georgia Tech have created a tool that can recover deleted conversations with AI assistants from Android phones. That has potentially promising implications for getting other types of erased information off phones during investigations, for example. But it also means your on-device AI chats, which are supposed to be private, may have a longer shelf life than intended.

Many privacy-conscious AI users prefer to run assistants locally on their devices, because their data isn’t sent out to the cloud, where it could be vulnerable to prying eyes or data breaches. Instead, the chat session uses your device memory.

But, as it turns out, some information from those sessions can stick around in your phone’s working memory even if you’ve deleted the chats themselves. Georgia Tech researchers created ORISA, a tool that searches that memory for clues to deleted conversations. They designed a fake note-keeping AI assistant for their experiment using the ChatterUI Android app, gave it a set of 100 fictional pieces of information about a project, and then deleted that information.

Using ORISA, the researchers then searched the device memory for remnants of that information that could be used as context to restart the session. ORISA found enough usable information to create a new working session with 26 fictional facts from the original set of 100. That’s more than the 14 the original assistant session had access to, “because the rest had fallen outside its active memory,” the researchers explained.

“It can help establish what information someone shared with an AI assistant and what assistance they received,” Ph.D. student Ken Xu, and lead author of the study, said of the experiment.

ORISA was developed to fill a cybersecurity gap. The researchers noted that a downside of local AI is its lack of “centralized logging and enforcement.” That means bad actors can fine-tune models for crimes, for example, and then make the evidence untraceable by deleting them after they’ve used them.

Until now, that reality has made it difficult for investigators using forensic memory techniques to recover useful evidence of that activity.

“ORISA leverages the batch tokens and key-value (KV) caches preserved in process memory to reconstruct token sequences and restore session-level attention states,” the researchers wrote.

That means a tool like this is most relevant to those trying to stop cybercrime rather than to those digging into your personal data. That said, if hackers recreate a technology like this or get their hands on a tool like ORISA itself, they could theoretically use it to gather deleted information from your chats and recreate your queries using that information as context. But ORISA’s efficacy also depends on time — the researchers noted that it’s harder to recover deleted information as time goes on, because your phone’s memory gets overwritten by newer activity.

Either way, our long-standing advice remains: Don’t share sensitive information with chatbots, even in a local session.
