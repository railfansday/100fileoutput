#5011. Bitcoin Wallet Fixes Critical Flaw That Redirected Funds to Attackers

Bitcoin Wallet Fixes Critical Flaw That Redirected Funds to Attackers

Bitcoin wallet Electrum patched a high-severity vulnerability this weekend that allowed rogue servers to manipulate pending Bitcoin transactions and redirect funds to attacker-controlled addresses. The flaw affects all versions before 4.8.3, which is now available.

At least one user has confirmed losing money to the exploit.

Electrum is one of the oldest Bitcoin software wallets around. The attack exploits the wallet’s trust model for unconfirmed transactions. When a user broadcasts a payment through a compromised server, the server can suppress the real transaction and inject a fake conflicting one—showing an attacker’s output address and zero fee, making the payment look stuck.

Because Electrum doesn’t verify signatures on unconfirmed transactions from the server, the spoofed transaction appears in the wallet’s history as legitimate.

When the user fee-bumps what looks like a stalled payment, they sign and broadcast a completely new valid transaction—one whose output address belongs to the attacker. The real payment was never sent; the fee-bump is the first transaction that actually hits the network, and it pays the attacker.

Electrum developer SomberNight, who published the advisory, noted that attackers can grind the fake transaction’s output address to match the last four characters of the user’s intended destination. Anyone spot-checking only the tail of a long Bitcoin address would see an apparent match.

The advisory states that “some servers are performing this attack in the wild,” backed by two user reports. A user identified as dannydeezy first flagged the suspicious server behavior on October 11; the manipulated transaction never propagated past the local server, so no funds were lost. A second user, Raju-K12, reported a confirmed loss on October 18 after fee-bumping a manipulated transaction.

The patch in 4.8.3 closes the main attack surface by verifying cryptographic signatures on wallet-owned inputs before adding any unconfirmed transaction to local history. A server can no longer inject a fake transaction touching the user’s own addresses without providing valid signatures—which the attacker cannot forge. The fix was tagged in the repository on October 23 and binaries were published the following day.

Incoming transactions from external senders—where none of the inputs belong to the user’s wallet—are still accepted without signature verification. SomberNight acknowledged this gap in the advisory, noting it would require additional work to close.

This isn’t Electrum’s first rodeo and it has dealt with malicious attacks in the past. A 2018 campaign used rogue servers to push fake update notifications, and by 2020 it had drained an estimated $25 million from Electrum users. Electrum’s open server model—anyone can run a node—is what makes this class of attack possible.

Users who connect exclusively to a server they run themselves are not affected. Anyone who cannot update immediately should manually verify output addresses and amounts before signing any transaction; Electrum 4.8.3 is available at electrum.org alongside the project’s Github.

#5003. Real-life vampire birds hunt and drink blood in remote Ecuador

Published Oct 18, 2026 8:04 AM EDT

When zoology professor Daniel Baldassarre traveled to the remote Galápagos Islands of Wolf and Darwin with two other researchers, getting there proved quite a challenge. With no beaches to land a boat on, they had to approach the islands’ cliffs in a small dinghy and wait for a lull in the waves before jumping onto sharp, black lava rocks.

But their efforts paid off. On that trip, they saw a bird that very few people have ever set eyes on in the wild: the vampire ground finch. These finches are only found on Wolf and Darwin, and feed on the blood of much larger birds called Nazca boobies that also live on the islands.

Up close, Baldassarre and his colleagues noted the vampire finches’ blood-stained bills and their throats visibly engorged with blood. They also saw them in action.

“We observed scores of vampire finches clamouring all over the backs, tails, and wings of boobies, opening up substantial wounds with their sharp beaks, and drinking their fill of blood,” they wrote in an article for The Conversation.

“Blood is a notoriously difficult food source to process and extract nutrients from, which probably partly explains why so few animals have evolved to feed on it,” Baldassarre tells Popular Science. He suspects that on these islands where freshwater is extremely rare, blood might serve as a water source for the finches.

It’s not the only component of the vampire finches’ diet, however. Based on unpublished data collected by Baldassarre and his colleagues, blood makes up about one-tenth of the vampire finches’ diet.

“These finches are quite opportunistic, so they will eat just about anything they can find,” Baldassarre says. “We observed them feeding on everything from seeds to insects to broken seabird eggs.”

One theory is that vampire finches rely more on blood feeding in periods of extreme drought, when other resources are scarce. But more research is needed to see if this idea holds up in practice.

Vampire finches weren’t always vampires, scientists think. The relationship between vampire finches and boobies probably started off with the finches eating parasites found on the skin and feathers of the boobies. This would have been mutually beneficial because it provided a cleaning service for the boobies and food for the finches.

But at some point in evolutionary history, in the process of picking off parasites, the finches started to create lesions on the boobies’ skin, and eventually began feeding on their blood.

Over time, their bills became more scalpel-like, perfect for making skin incisions. Their digestive system also changed. When Baldassarre and his colleagues studied the microbes found in vampire finches’ guts, they found that their gut microbiome was very different from that of other, non-blood-consuming finches on the Galápagos Islands, something they attribute to their unique diet.

Until recently, vampire finches were considered a subspecies of the sharp-beaked ground finch (Geospiza difficilis).

“But they have now been designated as their own species [Geospiza septentrionalis] because of the differences they have evolved because of blood feeding,” Baldassarre notes.

Baldassarre and his colleagues have seen vampire finches gang up on and kill nestling boobies,“but that was quite rare,” he points out.

“In most cases, the finches seem to inflict just enough damage to break the skin and extract some blood, but not too much to harm the boobies significantly.” This is similar to many parasite-host relationships, such as fleas, ticks, and mosquitoes on mammals. “You can kind of think of the vampire finches as giant mosquitoes,” Baldassarre says.

As seen in a segment on vampire finches in David Attenborough’s documentary A Perfect Planet, the boobies seem to tolerate the vampire finches rather than chasing them away. There are likely a few reasons for this, Baldassarre says.

“For one, as in large animals being bitten by mosquitoes, often the damage is minimal and not noticeable, so it doesn’t elicit a strong reaction. Also, the boobies sometimes have a vested interest in staying put and guarding their spot in the colony, or perhaps nest or nestlings, so may not want to fly away. Finally, the finches seem to target hard-to-reach areas on the boobies like the base of tail feathers, so it’s likely difficult for the boobies to deter them.”

I, for one, was feeling very sorry for the boobies being constantly hounded by these small vampire birds. Until I learnt that Nazca boobies practice something called “obligate siblicide,” which essentially means that the first-hatched chick routinely attacks and kills its younger sibling. It’s a harsh, bird-eat-bird world in the Galápagos Islands.

Why Prediction Markets Got the Brazilian Election So Wrong

Prediction markets got the results of Brazil's presidential election wildly wrong, with Polymarket putting Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's chances at just 20% before election day, when he ultimately won out.

Experts say the whiff is the result of Brazil banning prediction markets in the country and a wave of retail investors diluting the expertise of the money on the line.

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, commonly referred to as Lula, beat right-wing rival Flávio Bolsonaro in the Brazilian presidential election on Sunday. Bolsonaro came out ahead after the first round of voting at the start of October, but hadn't reached a majority, and a second round of votes was required. That second round is what Lula won, with a majority this time, and he will serve his fourth term as president.

Going into election day, Lula hovered around a 20% chance of victory with Bolsonaro sitting at 80%, per Polymarket. Even once polls and results started to roll out, one Polymarket whale told Decrypt that the market was slow to push up the odds.

"Sharps [a term for professional, high-stakes predictors] knew the market was mispriced, probably closer to 30-40% on [event] day. Mispricing was most evident during live trading, Lula was nearly a lock by 6:15 pm ET when Lula ‘Yes’ was 60c," pseudonymous Polymarket whale FhantomBets said. "Retail traders were so against Lula that you could get unlimited size at 60-80c when Lula had [essentially] already won."

Prediction markets settle at $1, with prices below that—such as $0.60 on a “yes” bet for Lula in Brazil’s election—providing implied odds of that event occurring.

The Polymarket trader added that many whales, including himself, were selling off long-term positions to get liquid capital so they could add more size to the Brazil market. Essentially, FhantomBets is claiming that retail traders with less prediction market experience inflated Bolsonaro's chances right until the very end.

Luke Nolan, senior researcher at CoinShares, pushed back on this notion and said Polymarket results reacted as polling results were released throughout the night.

"I don’t think more users are inherently a problem. They can bring liquidity and different perspectives, including local knowledge. What matters is whether participation brings different information, and whether traders can act on it," Nolan told Decrypt. "I would be wary of explaining the result simply as professionals being right and retail being wrong."

Instead, Nolan suggested Brazil’s ban on prediction markets is likely a bigger reason why the market was so harshly mispriced. Einar Knobel, CEO and co-founder of sportsbook and prediction market firm TxODDS, agreed.

"The price was set mostly by people outside the country," Knobel told Decrypt. "They were trading the first-round result, poll headlines, and the story built around them."

Brazil issued a sweeping ban against prediction market platforms in April, citing investor protection concerns and the rise of gambling addiction in the nation. Finance Minister Dario Durigan said the platforms violated betting regulations and lacked proper regulatory oversight.

Knobel added that liquidity was also relatively thin, with the market attracting just 4.5% of the volume that the 2024 U.S. presidential election garnered. "With fewer participants and almost no local knowledge in the book, little was there to correct the price," Knobel said.

Nolan believes predictors put too much weight on Bolsonaro's first-round performance, underestimated the increase in voter turnout for the second round, and assumed how voters would transfer based on the eliminated candidates.

"That said, Lula was priced at around 20% before the vote," Nolan said. "That is still some form of meaningful chance of winning. I think there are good reasons to question whether the market underestimated him. Ultimately, something that has an 80% chance of happening does not happen 20% of the time."

The mispricing of the Brazilian election was a significant upset, but Nolan cautioned against seeing it as a massive failure. He said prediction markets continue to price things better than traditional betting platforms—pointing to Betfair giving Hillary Clinton an 83% chance the day before the 2016 U.S. election.

"Across enough events, outcomes given a 20% chance should happen roughly 20% of the time. We should also compare their performance with other forecasts available at the same time," Nolan told Decrypt. "The concern would be a persistent pattern of overconfidence or a failure to incorporate relevant information, rather than an individual upset."

#4960. This snake cartwheels to escape danger

Published Oct 19, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

Snakes will bite, rattle their tails, and even spit venom to escape danger. But of all the world’s species, southeast Asia’s dwarf reed snake (Pseudorabdion longiceps) relies on an especially fascinating escape method. When threatened, the diminutive serpent rolls away like a speeding wheel.

An adult dwarf reed snake typically measures less than 12 inches long, but its unique locomotive maneuvers allow it to retreat as fast one foot per second—about four times faster than it travels by simply slithering on the ground. It’s not a flailing getaway, either. The snake also easily rolls along a straight path while somehow keeping its head both stable and facing forward.

Now, researchers finally have a better understanding of how the dwarf reed snake achieves such dizzying acrobatics. In a study published today in the journal Current Biology, a team at Japan’s Toho University detailed the specific physiology that enables its locomotion after filming the snakes with high-speed cameras. Essentially, the snake doesn’t rotate its entire body in a singular, forward motion—it whips one side of its body forward towards the ground while an arched portion swings down by its head in what the study’s authors call a “stroke.” The snake then alternates between its left and right side coming into contact with the ground for each successive stroke. Essentially, it’s like twirling a rope in a lateral circle while holding one end in your hand—except that end is the snake’s head.

The cumulative efforts bypass the traditional idea of a wheel requiring something like a central axle. Instead, this ingenious, highly controlled movement allows the animal to keep its head and sensory organs upright and facing ahead while traveling at such high speed. This is compounded by the dwarf reed snake directing regions of its body to engage in large, alternating rotations while stabilizing its head.

By gaining better insight into these biological mechanisms, researchers also highlight the difficulty in trying to predict an animal’s capabilities based solely on its physiology. A snake may have primarily evolved to slither—but that doesn’t mean it can’t get creative when necessary.

Understanding how DEX aggregators work is useful for anyone trading decentralized finance (DeFi) assets. It also helps explain the broader role of crypto aggregators, the challenges of cross-chain liquidity, and the factors that matter when comparing available solutions.

A DEX aggregator is a service that searches multiple decentralized exchanges (DEX) or liquidity sources to identify potential routes for a crypto swap. Instead of requiring you to compare individual exchanges manually, it brings relevant trading options together through a single interface.

The main objective is to improve trade execution by considering factors such as the quoted exchange rate, available liquidity, price impact, and transaction costs.

1. Search available liquidity sources. The aggregator checks supported decentralized exchanges, liquidity pools, or other eligible sources for routes that can facilitate the requested swap.

2. Evaluate possible trading routes. It compares available options based on factors such as expected output, liquidity, price impact, and applicable costs. The exact criteria depend on the aggregator’s routing algorithm.

5. Execute the swap. The transaction is submitted to the relevant blockchain. The final result depends on execution conditions, including available liquidity, network congestion, and changes in token prices.

Canada’s Draft Stablecoin Rules Cap Redemption Fees at 1% and Ban Yield

Canada proposed rules on Saturday that would force companies issuing stablecoins—crypto tokens designed to hold a steady value, usually one U.S. dollar—to keep real money behind every coin and hand it back on request. The draft, published in the Canada Gazette, the government's official newspaper, also keeps a ban on paying holders interest, otherwise known as yield.

For anyone who holds these tokens or might send money abroad with them, the rules decide whether you get real cash back if the company behind the coin collapses, how fast it arrives and what it costs.

Every coin must be backed one-to-one by reserves limited to cash and government debt maturing in three months or less, along with money market funds (pools of short-term safe assets) holding only such assets.

Those reserves must sit with a Canadian custodian in separate accounts that creditors cannot reach if the issuer goes bankrupt. Holders could redeem any amount within three business days, with fees capped at 1%.

Issuers could not pay interest for simply holding a coin, or route it through third parties: any arrangement that lets someone earn yield from holding it, including by sharing the issuer's income, counts as indirect payment. Rewards tied to using a coin, rather than holding it, would stay allowed.

“These prohibitions are intended to distinguish stablecoins from financial products that compete directly with bank deposits or other interest-bearing instruments,” the notice says. Issuers could still keep what their reserves earn, as long as reserves stay at least equal to the coins outstanding.

Issuers also could not present a coin as a bank deposit or claim deposit insurance or a government guarantee, and complaints would need acknowledging within seven days and resolving within 30.

The Stablecoin Act became law on March 26, 2026, after Ottawa announced the plan in its November budget. The Bank of Canada will register and supervise issuers.

“There is a pressing need for faster, cheaper, more transparent and more accessible cross-border payments services,” Ron Morrow, the Bank of Canada's executive director of payments, supervision and oversight, said at an Ottawa conference in September 2025.

In the United States, President Donald Trump signed the GENIUS Act, the U.S. stablecoin law, on July 18, 2025, and also bars issuers from paying holders interest.

The fight since then has been over letting crypto platforms offer yield to users, which banks oppose because they say deposits could drain toward crypto. The dispute was divisive enough that it helped lead to the failure of the Clarity Act in the Senate, a separate piece of legislation meant to set broader rules for the crypto market.

The banking lobby opposed the Clarity Act on grounds that it did not sufficiently close what they viewed as a loophole in the GENIUS Act, which currently allows for platforms such as Coinbase to offer yield to customers who hold USDC stablecoins in their accounts.

#4925. The mysterious dancing plague that killed people in 1518

Published Oct 20, 2026 8:59 AM EDT

In the summer of 1518, people in Strasbourg couldn’t seem to stop dancing—some even danced themselves to death.

It all started with one woman, who stepped into the street and began moving uncontrollably. Soon, dozens of other residents followed suit, overcome with the inexplicable urge to hop, jerk, and sway. Within weeks, the outbreak had spread throughout the city, leaving officials, physicians, and religious leaders scrambling to figure out what was happening—and, more importantly, how to make it stop.

Five centuries later, the dancing plague of 1518 remains one of history’s strangest medical mysteries. Historical records confirm that a bizarre episode turned a medieval city into a very unwilling dance floor that summer, but exactly why it happened remains a matter of debate.

The dancing plague began on July 14, 1518, when a woman named Frau Troffea started dancing in Strasbourg, then part of the Holy Roman Empire and now a city in northeastern France. Though her husband begged her to stop, Troffea kept moving as day faded to night, while a crowd of onlookers gathered around to watch. Eventually, she collapsed and fell asleep, but when she awoke the next morning, she resumed her solitary jig.

Troffea’s uncontrollable dancing, known as choreomania, seemed to be contagious. Dozens of other people were soon gyrating in the streets of Strasbourg and, by late August, as many as 400 individuals had succumbed.

At the time, the leading theory was that St. Vitus, a fourth-century Sicilian martyr believed to punish sinners by making them dance, had cursed the afflicted. Then, once St. Vitus decided his victims had suffered enough, he would relieve them of the curse.

City leaders determined that the best course of action was to keep the dancers dancing, so they cleared the outdoor grain market, built a stage, and hired musicians. They didn’t explain their rationale in any known, surviving documents, so historians don’t know exactly why they did this. But John Waller, associate professor of the history of medicine at Michigan State University and the author of The Dancing Plague: The Strange, True Story of an Extraordinary Illness, suspects they had two reasons: They believed dancing could help people purge physical impurities from their bodies, and they viewed it as a form of penance.

“If people were believed to have been cursed by God or a saint for some kind of immorality, well, dancing was associated with hedonism and that was fundamentally immoral,” he tells Popular Science. “A way of basically punishing oneself would be to dance to the point of exhaustion and pain and potentially even death. It would make sense with that penitential framework.” In other words, from the leaders’ perspective, if dancing was their punishment, let them be punished.

Regardless of their motives, the authorities’ decision had “very, very clear consequences,” he says. It was a hot summer, and the dancers were likely under the full glare of the sun. They weren’t eating or drinking much, and they weren’t resting.

“This is certainly going to be extremely dangerous for a large number of people, particularly anybody with any kind of susceptibility to heart attack or stroke,” says Waller.

The historical records are murky on exactly how many people died—one writer claimed as many as 15 per day, at least for a time—but it was enough to make city leaders completely change course.

“They essentially banned dancing, except for some people in the privacy of their own homes, and then instituted a program of penance and prayer,” says Waller. “They clearly realized that what they’d been doing before was causing harm and I suspect they got the impression that it was making things worse.”

Eventually, the authorities transported the remaining dancers to a shrine dedicated to St. Vitus about 30 miles away, where religious leaders went to work praying and leaving offerings. As days and weeks passed, the dancers overcame their choreomania and returned to their normal lives—traumatized and tired, but otherwise fine.

The dancing plague of 1518 wasn’t the only such incident of its kind. Numerous other outbreaks of uncontrollable dancing occurred in western Europe between the late 1300s and mid-1500s—and Waller thinks it’s no coincidence that many were clustered along the Rhine River and its tributaries.

“Rivers are very good for, obviously, transporting trade goods, but they’re also very good for spreading ideas,” he says. “From the headwaters in Switzerland all the way down to where the Rhine flows into the North Sea, you’ve got this belief in a saint who curses with a dance.”

Now that we have the benefit of hindsight, what might explain the dancing plague of 1518 and others like it? One theory is that the dancers were sickened by ergot, a fungus that can contaminate grain. Another holds venomous spiders bit the afflicted. Demonic possession, epilepsy, typhus, and encephalitis—brain inflammation caused by an infection or autoimmune response—have also been put forward as possible explanations.

But after considering all the evidence, Waller thinks there’s only one plausible theory that truly makes sense. He suspects the dancers of Strasbourg were all victims of mass hysteria, also known as mass psychogenic illness, brought on by intense psychological distress.

The years leading up to 1518 were rough, particularly for the lower classes, with failed crops, bad weather, economic issues, social unrest, religious tensions, and illnesses like smallpox, leprosy, and syphilis taking a toll on peoples’ psyches. They were hungry, anxious, sad, scared, and worried that God had abandoned them.

Waller suspects the people living in Strasbourg simply reached their breaking point, then entered into a trance-like state that rendered them largely insensitive to their extreme fatigue and sore feet. They danced, Waller argues, rather than weeping, fighting, praying, or engaging in some other behavior because of their belief in St. Vitus and his dancing curse.

More than 300 students in Blackburn, England, suffered from a mysterious fainting fit in 1956, for example, and hundreds of people succumbed to alternating fits of laughing and sobbing in East Africa in 1962. And although the dancing plague of 1518 took place more than 500 years ago, Waller says we can still learn plenty from it. For him, the most important takeaway is “the way in which our expression of severe distress can be culturally conditioned and channeled.”

When Waller initially started researching the dancing plague of 1518, it was such a “wild phenomenon” that his inclination was to think “as strange as the past can be, this was simply too strange to have actually happened,” he says. In other words, he says, it reminds us that psychological distress is not expressed in a vacuum. The culture, beliefs, and circumstances around us can shape how our inner turmoil manifests—and, in some cases, how it spreads from one person to another.

Crypto trading happens across many venues. Centralized exchanges custody user assets, run internal matching engines, and update balances on internal ledgers. Decentralized exchanges let users trade through smart contracts while retaining custody. OTC desks help large participants execute bigger trades without showing full order flow on public books.

Each venue model makes different trade-offs. CEXs can offer speed, customer support, and deep order books, but they introduce custody and counterparty risk. #4922. Canada opens consultation on stablecoins, citing US GENIUS Act

As part of the potential implementation of its Stablecoin Act, the Canadian government will hear from the public on a proposed framework for tokens from non-financial institutions.

Lawmakers in the Canadian government opened a 60-day public consultation on regulations proposed to address non-financial institution stablecoin issuers, including a potential prohibition on yield payments.

On Saturday, the government of Canada gave notice that it would be open to public comment on the stablecoin regulations until Dec. 23. The proposed framework cited efforts to be consistent with stablecoin regulations in the United States and other jurisdictions, to “reduce the overall regulatory burden on multi-jurisdictional issuers.”

Notably, the proposed regulations said that non-financial institution stablecoin issuers would be restricted from offering interest or yield to discourage HODLing the tokens. They would also be required to redeem stablecoins within three business days of requests from tokenholders, and not charge a fee greater than 1% of the coins’ total value.

The regulations fell under Canada’s Stablecoin Act, which was passed in March and intended to address non-financial institution issuers. According to the government, the proposed rules were required to enact the policy and include provisions for issuers to register with Canada’s central bank.

“The intention is for the proposed Regulations to come into force in fall 2027,” said the government. “Once the proposed Regulations are in effect, issuers of stablecoins in Canada who are not financial institutions would be required to apply for registration with the Bank of Canada prior to issuing a stablecoin.”

The GENIUS Act, which establishes a regulatory framework for stablecoins in the US, is scheduled to take effect in January 2027. Meanwhile, the European Union’s regulations, Markets in Crypto Assets (MiCA), could give exchanges until January to halt services for stablecoins not in compliance with the framework, likely including Tether’s USDt (USDT), the largest stablecoin by market capitalization.

#4921. Microdosing GLP-1s Like Wegovy, Zepbound May Still Lead to Weight Loss

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

People may still be able to experience weight loss by staying on lower starting doses of GLP-1 medications, such as Wegovy or Zepbound, for longer, according to research published in Biology Methods & Protocols on August 31.

Staying on lower doses of GLP-1 medications for longer may overlap with a practice commonly referred to as “microdosing,” which has become a trend among celebrities and on social media.

However, the study did not establish that participants were intentionally microdosing, and using these medications outside their approved dosing schedules is not FDA- or manufacturer-approved.

The study included electronic health records from 814 individuals who met the criteria for sustained low dose semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), defined as at least three 0.25 mg doses spanning at least six months. It also included 1,016 individuals who met the criteria for sustained low dose tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound), defined as 2.5 mg doses spanning at least six months.

They found that those taking sustained low dose tirzepatide for one year saw around a 5.5% weight loss, while those taking sustained low dose semaglutide for the same period saw around a 2.2% weight loss.

The researchers also compared weight loss trajectories between those taking low dose and high dose semaglutide. After matching participants based on age, sex, BMI, and type 2 diabetes status, they found that those on low dose semaglutide experienced 1.7% weight loss while those on high dose semaglutide had about 12% weight loss.

“These findings introduce an important treatment paradigm toward increasing the awareness around ‘microdosing’ for patients whose goal is cardiometabolic health with lesser side effects than typical dose escalation of these medicines and an associated modest sustained weight loss in the 2-7% range from baseline,” Venky Soundararajan, PhD, co-founder, chief scientific officer for nference, and co-author of the study, told Healthline.

Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, said it isn’t surprising to see that people taking GLP-1 medications can lose weight even at the lowest dose.

“I have seen this in my own practice. Although these starter doses are generally intended to help patients adjust to the medication, some people may experience meaningful weight loss even at these lower doses,” he added.

Soundararajan noted that weight reductions remained evident through the 15-month follow-up. This suggests that the initiation dose may potentially serve as a sustained maintenance strategy over many months, rather than simply a brief stepping stone to a higher dose, particularly for a patient who has already achieved the weight loss and cardiometabolic benefit they are seeking.

However, Ali cautioned, “In my experience, many patients eventually reach a point where they stop losing weight on the lowest dose, although individual responses can vary considerably. Some patients may achieve satisfactory results on a low dose, but most will need some degree of dose escalation to continue losing weight and reach their goals.”

Wegovy (semaglutide) and Zepbound (tirzepatide) are GLP-1 medications approved for weight management as well as other conditions. Other GLP-1s are only approved for the management of certain conditions like type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and obstructive sleep apnea.

Novo Nordisk, the manufacturer of Wegovy, recommends starting at a weekly dose of 0.25 mg and increasing the dose every 4 weeks. The recommended maintenance dose is 2.4 mg once weekly, although some adults with obesity may increase to 7.2 mg once weekly if additional weight loss is needed.

Eli Lilly, the manufacturer of Zepbound, recommends starting at a weekly dose of 2.5 mg. It then recommends increasing to the next higher dose every 4 weeks until you reach the recommended maintenance dose or the maximum dose of 15 mg.

Your healthcare professional may adjust how quickly your dose is increased based on your response to the medication and any side effects you experience. Talk with them about the appropriate dosing schedule for you.

It is important to keep in mind that while some people experience fewer side effects, microdosing may not reduce the side effects of GLP-1 medications for everyone.
