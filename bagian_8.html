Earlier this year, UnitedHealthcare unveiled Avery, a generative AI companion aimed at supporting care coordination for its members.

At the time, the tool was available to about 6.5 million of its members in employer plans and 160,000 people enrolled in Medicare Advantage. Now, the insurer is expanding access to 21 million of its members, growing the tool's reach at a faster clip than was initially expected.

To date, the company has found that Avery has resolved 90% of its member interactions, and in the remaining 10% of cases, can complete a handoff to a human advocate to offer member support.

"As we're seeing some of the statistics and some of the feedback that we're getting from users around the success we've had within the early phases of Avery, it's just making sense to open up that application into more and more of the population," Craig Kurtzweil, chief data and analytics officer at UnitedHealthcare, told Fierce Healthcare.

He said that a key piece of what makes Avery work is that it's built on "ground truth systems" that create a critical data foundation for the platform as it sits on top of the data available through UnitedHealthcare. With that data layer powering Avery's interactions, members get better answers, Kurtzweil said.

That accuracy is critical to helping members build trust with the tool and use it most effectively. Avery can provide key details on deductibles, out-of-pocket maximums, provider networks, pricing and claims processing, Kurtzweil said.

"Making this as accurate as possible is a big piece of gaining that trust with the consumers," he said.

Avery also fits into an ecosystem where UnitedHealthcare is making a push to put more information in its members' hands. Plan designs like Surest, which offer variable copayments, give members power to choose the providers and care that works best for them and their wallets.

With Avery, members can then have an easier time navigating the experience and understanding their options, Kurtzweil said.

The team is also building out additional functionality for Avery, including an option for it to call providers and schedule appointments on the member's behalf. Smoothing out that process can eliminate yet another friction point that can prevent people from seeking the care that they need.

Kurtzweil said that UnitedHealthcare also intends to continue building out access to Avery across its membership, with the intention to ultimately ensure that all of its enrollees can tap into the tool.

"More and more people will be impacted by this," he said, "where we're going to start to reduce that friction and make life easier for those consumers."

#5315. Modern neuroscience is rediscovering a surprising idea freud had 130 years ago

What if the human brain works in ways that Sigmund Freud began describing more than 130 years ago? A new scientific analysis suggests that some of his ideas about the mind share surprising similarities with one of the most influential theories in modern neuroscience.

Published in the scientific journal Entropy, the paper explores connections between Freud's original theories, later developments in psychoanalysis, and a modern approach to brain research known as the prediction paradigm.

This approach describes the brain as a prediction machine. Rather than simply responding to information from the outside world, the brain is constantly anticipating what will happen next. It then compares those expectations with incoming information from the senses and works to reduce any differences between what it predicted and what actually occurred.

Scientists believe this ongoing process plays a central role in how people perceive their surroundings, make decisions, behave, and regulate emotions.

Researchers Erik Stänicke, Bendik Hovet, and Line Indrevoll Stänicke, together with colleagues, argue that this modern understanding of brain function has important parallels with ideas that psychoanalysts have been developing for more than a century.

"For over 130 years, psychoanalysis has developed psychological theories about how predictions take place at a subjective level, which cognitive neuropsychology is now studying at a physiological level."

How Freud's Ideas Connect to the Predictive Brain

Although neuroscience and psychoanalysis approach the mind differently, the researchers suggest that both may be describing related processes.

Neuroscience examines the physical mechanisms behind thought and behavior, often using mathematical models to explain how the brain processes information. Psychoanalysis, which grew out of Freud's work, focuses more on unconscious mental processes, emotional experiences, and the ways past relationships influence present behavior.

In other words, one field investigates what happens inside the brain, while the other explores how those processes are experienced by the individual.

A particularly interesting connection involves the psychoanalytic concept of projection and the neuroscientific concept of prediction.

In psychoanalysis, projection involves attributing feelings, intentions, or qualities to someone else. The researchers suggest that this resembles how the brain uses previously learned expectations to interpret new experiences.

"When we attribute qualities, intentions or feelings to other people, our brain shapes our experience of the world in line with established expectations," says Stänicke.

For instance, someone who has repeatedly experienced rejection may become especially sensitive to signs that other people are rejecting them. Even an ambiguous remark or facial expression might be interpreted negatively because past experiences have shaped what the person expects.

Over time, these expectations can influence how someone approaches unfamiliar situations and forms new relationships.

"This corresponds to the neuroscientific distinction between changing one's own predictions, perceptual inference, and the attempt to make the world conform to them, namely active inference."

Perceptual inference refers to the way the brain interprets sensory information and updates its expectations in response. Active inference, by contrast, involves taking actions that help bring experiences into line with predictions or reduce uncertainty.

Together, these concepts help explain why people do not always experience the world as it objectively is. Their expectations can influence what they notice, how they interpret events, and how they respond.

Why the Brain Holds On to Negative Expectations

The researchers believe the similarities between Freud's ideas and modern neuroscience could offer valuable insights into mental health disorders, particularly those involving deeply established patterns of thinking and behavior.

Both approaches portray the mind as constantly seeking stability and predictability. This tendency is related to homeostasis, the process through which a system maintains a relatively stable internal state.

In the predictive brain model, reducing uncertainty is an important part of maintaining that stability. The brain relies on familiar expectations to make the world easier to understand, even when those expectations are inaccurate or no longer helpful.

"Psychoanalysts refer to the tendency in the mind to recreate familiar relational patterns, even when these are poorly adapted," says Stänicke.

This could help explain why people sometimes repeat harmful patterns in relationships or continue interpreting situations negatively despite evidence that challenges their assumptions.

"Rigid and persistent symptoms, such as paranoid ideas or an internalized critical voice, may be stable but not very flexible prediction models," says Stänicke.

"For example, there may be people who automatically expect criticism, rejection or hostility from others, and therefore interpret situations through this filter despite the fact that reality does not warrant it."

Such expectations can become deeply entrenched because they make experiences feel familiar and predictable. Even when the resulting interpretation is distorted, maintaining an established mental model may feel less uncertain than changing it.

According to Stänicke, this offers a possible explanation for why some psychological symptoms can persist for years and why changing longstanding patterns of thought and behavior often requires considerable time.

The connection is particularly interesting because it suggests that some mental health difficulties may involve more than consciously held beliefs. They may also reflect deeply learned expectations that automatically shape emotional responses and interactions with others.

How Psychotherapy Could Help Change Deeply Rooted Patterns

The researchers also highlight an important distinction between what people consciously believe and what they have learned through experience.

"In addition, both models give us insight into how parts of our expectations of the outside world are not only anchored cognitively, but in procedural memory that is expressed in relational ways of being," he says.

Procedural memory is a type of long-term memory involved in learned habits and skills. It allows people to perform familiar actions without consciously thinking through every step.

In the context of relationships, the researchers suggest that a similar form of learning may help explain why certain emotional reactions and patterns of interaction become automatic.

Someone might intellectually understand that other people are not necessarily hostile or critical, for example, yet still instinctively respond as though rejection is inevitable.

These expectations are not simply thoughts that can be changed through reasoning. They can also be embedded in habitual ways of reacting to people and navigating relationships.

This distinction may help explain why psychotherapy sometimes needs to address personal relationships and emotional experiences rather than focusing only on conscious thoughts.

"Therefore, psychotherapy sometimes has to work relationally. For example, new experiences in the relationship between therapist and patient can gradually help to change entrenched relational patterns."

Through repeated experiences that challenge familiar expectations, patients may gradually develop different ways of interpreting situations and relating to other people.

Bringing Freud's Psychoanalysis and Neuroscience Together

The researchers argue that connecting psychoanalysis with modern predictive brain theory could benefit both fields.

Neuroscience may provide a biological framework for understanding processes that psychoanalysts have long described through subjective experience. At the same time, psychoanalytic theories may offer more detailed explanations of how expectations are experienced emotionally, interpreted personally, and expressed through relationships.

The proposed similarities do not establish that Freud's broader theories were correct. Instead, they suggest that two very different traditions of studying the mind may offer complementary ways of understanding some of the same psychological processes.

By examining both the brain's physical mechanisms and the individual's inner experience, researchers hope to develop a more complete understanding of human behavior and mental health.

"Bringing these two fields together can open up for a more holistic psychology, in which both neurological mechanisms and subjective experience are included. In this way, we can understand subjectivity in a more scientific manner."

Hiring engineers is especially difficult because in most cases, the only way to assess a candidate is to have them screened by an expert engineer who’s already on the company’s books. But for most companies, that means pulling that employee away from actual engineering tasks to spend hours of their time looking through resumes and conducting physical assessments of the candidate’s work.

The challenge is made more difficult by the fact that many candidates these days are using artificial intelligence tools to polish their resumes and get past the initial remote interview stage that companies normally rely on to weed out weaker prospects. As a result, numerous unqualified candidates can slip through the net and land an in-person interview, leading to a serious drain on hirer’s engineering resources.

“Every unqualified candidate who reaches an engineering panel burns through senior engineering hours,” Thomas explained. “The high weekly interview volume, coupled with low pass rates and the high cost of engineering talent compounds into a large annual dollar cost. And a bad hire that slips through is worse still.”

Thomas said Colare’s “simulation-based engineering assessments” are designed to show how a candidate actually thinks, builds and solves problems, before they get called to an interview. This is not an easy thing to do. The process of evaluating engineering skills is especially difficult because for each problem, there can be an infinite number of solutions.

“Something like a structural bracket or a power board or a propulsion feed system has thousands of valid designs and thousands of subtly fatal ones,” Thomas said. “The difference lives in tradeoffs that machines can’t check, such as mass against stiffness, thermal margin against packaging, manufacturability against performance and cost against reliability. There is no simple answer key.”

Thomas says the answer is to put candidates inside real engineering environments, giving them tasks to solve that are drawn from real-world flight and manufacturing constraints. “[Hirers] upload their candidate list and they can send it [the assessment] out to their entire candidate pool,” Thomas said. “Then our model will analyze every one on their process and output to present HR teams and engineers with a detailed brief of each candidate’s performance.”

Colare uses AI to map the role to hands-on, AI-generated assessments that span areas such as computer-aided design, printed circuit layouts, thermal analysis and finite element analysis. “We score the candidates’ decisions against rubrics built by senior engineers who have shipped real hardware,” Thomas said. “Every discipline demands its own domain depth. A guidance, navigation and control problem is nothing like a design for manufacturing problem.”

Colare has already been deployed by organizations ranging from startups to multibillion-dollar aerospace manufacturer, chemical engineering firms and advanced manufacturing giants. Around half of its customer base is in the aerospace and defense industries, Thomas said.

One of Colare’s customers is a chemical manufacturing startup that’s working on developing field-induced reactor technology. Initially, it only had two people on its staff, who obviously couldn’t afford to waste time screening dozens of candidates when they were looking to hire their first electrical engineer, because they also had to keep the business up and running. Yet they needed that engineer to join the team fast, Thomas explained. Moreover, the two co-founders weren’t even qualified to properly assess the candidates who applied for the role.

“One has a chemical engineering Ph.D. and the other is a business operations lead, so neither were equipped to evaluate the founding electrical engineer they needed to hire,” Thomas said. “Where they once would have relied on outside networks to vet these applicants, Colare gave them the ability to assess candidates directly.”

In another case, the subsidiary of a publicly traded manufacturing firm was preparing to advance a candidate to a final interview with its CEO, based on their performance during in-person interviews with the human resources team. But that candidate would have wasted the CEO’s precious time, for they failed Colare’s simulated assessment.

Colare will use the money from today’s round to expand its assessment library to cover more niche deep technology disciplines, and also try to build more advanced AI models that can predict how engineers will perform on complex challenges they have never encountered before.

The company’s mission is to transform the engineering hiring process, so that verifiable skills carry more weight than paper-based qualifications. “Resumes are no longer indicators of how capable someone is,” Thomas insisted. “We want to replace evaluation to make it meritocratic, so that people are hired on skill, not pedigree.”

#5307. "Mind-blowing" Alzheimer’s breakthrough: Two hours of sleep restored without clearing brain plaques

Scientists have discovered an unexpected reason why Alzheimer's disease disrupts sleep, and their findings suggest that the problem may be reversible. In experiments involving mice with Alzheimer's-like brain changes, researchers restored more than two hours of sleep per day without removing the amyloid plaques associated with the disease.

The surprising discovery points to an overlooked culprit: the brain's own immune cells. Rather than simply protecting the brain from damage, these cells can trigger inflammation that interferes with healthy sleep.

Researchers at the University of Kentucky found that temporarily eliminating most of these immune cells allowed the mice to sleep longer and spend more time in the deep, restorative stages of sleep. The results suggest a potential new approach to treating one of the most disruptive symptoms of Alzheimer's disease.

The findings, published in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia, were reported by a team led by Shannon L. Macauley, Ph.D., an associate professor of physiology at the University of Kentucky College of Medicine, and first author Nicholas J. Constantino, Ph.D., a recent doctoral graduate.

Macauley described the discovery as "paradigm-shifting" because it challenges the prevailing understanding of why people with Alzheimer's often experience severe sleep problems.

How the Brain's Immune System Can Disrupt Sleep

To understand what the researchers uncovered, imagine a small fire breaking out in a kitchen. Instead of containing the flames, the building's sprinkler system activates everywhere, flooding the entire house and causing widespread damage.

Something similar may happen in the brain during Alzheimer's disease.

Amyloid plaques are deposits of sticky protein fragments that accumulate between nerve cells. They are one of the characteristic features of Alzheimer's and have long been investigated for their role in the disease.

The brain also contains specialized immune cells known as microglia. These cells normally protect the nervous system by removing debris, responding to injuries, and helping maintain a healthy environment for neurons.

However, when microglia detect amyloid plaques, their defensive response can become excessive. Instead of simply addressing the threat, they release inflammatory signals that may interfere with normal brain activity.

Scientists have traditionally linked sleep problems in Alzheimer's primarily to damaged neurons or the accumulation of amyloid plaques. The new findings suggest that the immune system's reaction to those plaques plays a major role.

"Basically, we showed that it is not the plaques themselves, or solely dysfunctional neurons, that cause sleep loss but actually microglia," said Macauley. "Microglia are immune cells that, when they respond to plaques, kick off this elaborate cascade of inflammation, as if the microglia are partying all night, and keeping the brain awake."

In other words, the brain's response to Alzheimer's pathology may create additional problems beyond those caused by the plaques themselves. The researchers compared this widespread reaction to a "whole house response," in which a defense mechanism ends up causing more disruption than expected.

Tracking Alzheimer's Sleep Changes Inside the Brain

To investigate the connection between microglia and sleep, the scientists studied two groups of mice. One group was genetically predisposed to develop amyloid plaques, while the other consisted of normal "wild-type" mice that experienced typical aging.

The researchers examined the animals at two ages: six months, when plaques first appeared in the genetically modified mice, and 18 months, when the disease model showed more advanced pathology.

Comparing these groups helped the scientists distinguish changes associated with Alzheimer's pathology from those that naturally occur as the brain ages.

The team used several advanced technologies to measure sleep and examine what was happening inside the brain.

Each mouse was fitted with a small device that recorded electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG).

EEG measures the brain's electrical activity, including the patterns and rhythms produced when networks of neurons communicate. These electrical signals change depending on whether the brain is awake, dreaming or experiencing deep sleep.

EMG measures electrical activity in muscles. Combining the two techniques allowed researchers to identify different sleep stages and determine how long the animals remained in each one.

The scientists also wanted to visualize the relationship between amyloid plaques and immune cells throughout the brain.

For this, they used light-sheet microscopy, an imaging technique that makes biological tissue transparent and illuminates it with a thin layer of laser light. The resulting images can be assembled into detailed three-dimensional maps, allowing scientists to examine structures that would otherwise be difficult to see.

This approach revealed the distribution of plaques and microglia across the brain, helping researchers understand where immune cells were responding to Alzheimer's pathology.

Scientists Temporarily Remove 87% of Brain Immune Cells

To determine whether microglia were actually responsible for disrupted sleep, the team conducted an experiment designed to temporarily eliminate most of these cells.

The researchers administered a drug called Pexidartinib (PLX3397), which was originally developed for cancer research. It blocks a cellular signal that microglia depend on for survival.

After the mice received the medication in their food for 14 days, approximately 87% of their microglia had been depleted.

This allowed the scientists to test whether sleep would improve when the cells driving the inflammatory response were largely absent.

The researchers also performed a detailed analysis of the animals' brain activity using a mathematical method called Fitting Oscillations and One Over Frequency (FOOOF).

This technique separates electrical signals into periodic activity (the rhythmic waves we usually think of as brain waves) and aperiodic activity (the background electrical noise).

By examining these different components, the team could identify changes in how the brain was functioning, including whether it remained unusually active during periods when it should have been resting.

The process is somewhat like monitoring a car engine to determine whether it continues running at high speed even when the vehicle is supposed to be idle.

A Surprising Discovery About Alzheimer's Brain Plaques

Macauley called the results "mind-blowing and unexpected."

One of the most surprising findings was that sleep problems did not continue worsening as amyloid plaques accumulated.

The researchers initially expected that increasing plaque buildup would lead to progressively more severe sleep disturbances. Instead, the mice developed substantial sleep problems relatively early, and those problems remained largely unchanged even as plaque levels increased dramatically.

"I expected that as plaque burden became more severe, sleep disruption would also worsen," said Constantino. "The disruptions in sleep and cortical EEG activity that occur at 6 months, when plaques first emerge, did not worsen by 18 months, despite more than double the amount of plaque burden."

The team described this pattern as a ceiling effect. Essentially, sleep disruption appeared to reach a certain level early in the disease process rather than increasing in direct proportion to the amount of amyloid in the brain.

This suggests that the initial inflammatory response triggered by plaques may be particularly important. Once microglia become activated and disrupt sleep, additional plaque accumulation may not necessarily produce an equivalent increase in sleep loss.

The discovery offers an important clue as to why sleep disturbances can emerge relatively early in Alzheimer's disease.

Alzheimer's Disrupts a Different Type of Sleep Than Normal Aging

The study also revealed a notable difference between sleep changes caused by normal aging and those associated with Alzheimer's pathology.

Sleep is not a single, uniform state. Throughout the night, the brain moves through several stages that perform different functions.

Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is strongly associated with vivid dreaming and plays an important role in processing memories. The researchers found that normal aging primarily reduced this type of sleep.

Alzheimer's pathology, however, had a different effect. It selectively disrupted non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, which includes the deepest and most physically restorative stages of sleep.

During NREM sleep, the brain performs essential maintenance functions that support learning, memory, and recovery. Deep sleep is also associated with processes that help clear metabolic waste products from the brain.

"That restorative sleep is super important for physical repair, learning and memory and washing out the toxins of the day," Macauley said.

Losing this important stage of sleep could have consequences beyond daytime fatigue.

Because healthy sleep helps maintain the brain's internal environment, disruptions may interfere with processes that normally remove potentially harmful waste. Researchers suspect this could contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle in which Alzheimer's pathology disturbs sleep, and inadequate sleep may further aggravate the disease.

Breaking that cycle could therefore become an important treatment strategy.

More Than Two Hours of Sleep Restored Without Removing Plaques

The most striking result emerged after the researchers temporarily removed most of the microglia from the brains of mice with Alzheimer's-like pathology.

The animals gained more than two hours of sleep per day.

They also experienced longer periods of restorative NREM sleep, giving them more opportunities to transition naturally into REM sleep, which is important for memory formation.

Even more surprisingly, these improvements occurred without any reduction in amyloid plaque levels.

The plaques were still present, but the mice were sleeping substantially better.

This finding provides evidence that the inflammatory response driven by microglia contributes directly to sleep disruption and that suppressing this response can reverse at least some of the associated sleep problems.

It also suggests that researchers may not need to eliminate amyloid plaques to improve sleep in Alzheimer's disease.

Although these findings are promising, the experiments were conducted in mice, and the same benefits have not yet been demonstrated in people. Temporarily removing most of the brain's immune cells is also not an established or practical treatment for Alzheimer's patients.

The researchers are now interested in whether the beneficial effects can be reproduced by adjusting microglial activity rather than eliminating the cells themselves.

If such an approach eventually proves successful in humans, it could provide a way to improve sleep and potentially reduce some of the additional stress that poor sleep places on the brain.

The work was conducted in Macauley's laboratory in the Department of Physiology at the University of Kentucky's Sanders-Brown Center on Aging.

Macauley credits the achievement to a "beautiful partnership" with students and researchers who were willing to challenge established ideas about Alzheimer's disease.

"I love people who take initiative, find their passion, are curious, and keep pushing to find an answer," Macauley said.

She encourages members of her laboratory to be "calculated risk-takers," emphasizing that meaningful discoveries often require scientists to investigate possibilities that may initially seem unlikely.

A quote from hockey legend Wayne Gretzky hangs in her office: "You miss 100% of the shots you don't take."

Constantino, who recently completed his doctorate at the university, said the laboratory's collaborative environment encouraged him to pursue questions that crossed traditional scientific disciplines.

"Dr. Macauley has also taught me to embrace uncertainty and failure as part of the scientific process," Constantino said. "Some of the most interesting studies I have been a part of emerged because our original hypothesis was wrong."

Macauley reinforces this mindset with a simple instruction to her team: "Follow the data, ask better questions, and figure out what is actually happening."

That approach helped the researchers move beyond the traditional emphasis on neurons and amyloid plaques to examine the broader role of the brain's immune system.

Could Existing Medications Help Restore Sleep in Alzheimer's?

The research team is now exploring ways to translate these findings into treatments and diagnostic tools that could eventually benefit people with Alzheimer's disease.

One promising direction involves calming overactive microglia without removing them.

Because microglia perform essential protective functions, eliminating them is not an ideal long-term strategy. Instead, the researchers hope to modify how these immune cells respond to amyloid plaques and regulate inflammation.

The laboratory is investigating existing medications, including the diabetes drug Metformin and the anti-seizure medication Stiripentol.

These drugs are being studied for their potential to alter how microglia produce and use energy, a process known as cellular metabolism. Changing that energy use could potentially reduce excessive immune activation and its harmful effects on sleep.

The hope is to prevent the brain's inflammatory response from becoming so intense that it disrupts normal sleep activity.

Researchers are particularly interested in whether intervention during the earlier stages of Alzheimer's could improve sleep and quality of life before substantial memory loss develops.

"If we can target that process, it might help with quality of life, attention, cognition and confusion," Macauley said.

The proposed drug strategies remain experimental for this purpose, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether they can safely produce similar benefits in humans.

A Simpler Way to Detect Early Alzheimer's Changes

Beyond identifying a potential treatment target, the study uncovered electrical activity patterns that may help distinguish Alzheimer's pathology from normal brain aging.

The researchers believe these patterns could eventually make EEG a useful tool for detecting and monitoring changes associated with Alzheimer's disease.

Unlike more expensive imaging procedures, EEG records electrical signals through electrodes placed on the scalp. It is relatively affordable, does not require surgery, and can be repeated to follow changes over time.

The team envisions EEG becoming a "readily accessible, affordable and longitudinal biomarker of Alzheimer's disease."

A biomarker is a measurable biological sign that provides information about a person's health or a particular disease. A longitudinal biomarker can be tracked repeatedly to monitor how a condition develops or changes.

"Portable EEG systems could allow us to monitor people in their home environments and potentially screen for changes associated with an Alzheimer's disease, without the initial need for expensive or invasive tests," Macauley said.

If future human studies confirm the usefulness of these electrical signatures, portable EEG devices might eventually help doctors identify people who need additional testing.

This could be particularly valuable in rural communities across Kentucky, where patients sometimes must travel long distances to reach specialized medical centers.

Instead of requiring every potentially at-risk patient to undergo advanced testing immediately, local clinics might use simpler monitoring tools to identify individuals who would benefit from further evaluation.

Although this application still requires validation, it offers a possible path toward more accessible Alzheimer's screening and monitoring.

Ultimately, the study suggests that treating Alzheimer's may require looking beyond the plaques themselves and examining how the brain responds to them.

By identifying the immune cells responsible for much of the sleep disruption in their mouse models, the University of Kentucky researchers have uncovered a potential way to restore an essential brain function without first removing the underlying amyloid deposits.

The next challenge is determining whether calming these cells can deliver similar benefits in people, potentially improving sleep, cognition and quality of life long before the most severe symptoms of Alzheimer's develop.

#5298. Scientists discover why some people still can’t taste food after COVID-19

Scientists have uncovered changes inside taste buds that may help explain why some people struggle to taste food more than a year after recovering from COVID-19. The findings point to disruptions in the molecular signals responsible for detecting certain flavors, along with physical abnormalities in the structure of taste buds.

The study, published in Chemical Senses, offers the first direct biological evidence connecting persistent taste problems reported by patients to measurable changes within their taste cells.

Why Does Taste Loss Linger After COVID-19?

To investigate the lasting effects of COVID-19 on taste, scientists from the University of Colorado Anschutz and two Swedish universities examined 28 people who had experienced taste disturbances for more than a year following infection. None of the participants had been hospitalized for COVID-19.

Testing revealed that eight of the 28 participants had clearly abnormal taste scores. Eleven reported difficulty detecting specific flavors, particularly sweet, bitter, and umami, the savory taste associated with foods such as mushrooms and broth. In contrast, their ability to detect salty and sour flavors remained largely unaffected.

Seeking an explanation for this unusual pattern, the researchers collected small tissue samples from the taste buds of 20 participants and examined them for biological abnormalities.

Scientists Identify a Molecular Defect in Taste Cells

The research team, organized by Göran Hellekant, PhD, of the University of Wisconsin and the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, identified reduced amounts of messenger RNA (mRNA) involved in producing a protein known as PLCβ2.

This protein plays an essential role in how taste receptor cells communicate with the brain. Specifically, it amplifies the signals generated when the tongue detects sweet, bitter, and umami flavors. Without sufficient levels of the protein, those signals may become too weak to register normally.

"PLCβ2 acts like a molecular amplifier inside taste cells," said Thomas Finger, PhD, professor of cell and developmental biology at the University of Colorado Anschutz and corresponding author of the study. "It strengthens the signal before it's transmitted to the brain. When levels are reduced, the taste signal weakens."

The discovery may also explain why some flavors are affected more than others. Cells responsible for detecting salty and sour tastes use different signaling mechanisms that do not depend on PLCβ2, potentially allowing those sensations to remain intact even when other taste signals are disrupted.

Hidden Structural Changes Inside Taste Buds

The researchers also discovered that the abnormalities were not limited to molecular signaling. When they examined taste bud tissue under a microscope, they found that some participants had changes in the physical arrangement of their taste cells.

"Some subjects had normal-looking taste buds, while others showed structural disorganization," Finger said. "This suggests that both molecular and architectural changes may contribute to persistent taste dysfunction."

Together, these findings suggest that lingering taste problems after COVID-19 may involve multiple biological disruptions, affecting both the way taste cells are organized and how effectively they transmit information.

Why Can COVID-19 Taste Loss Last More Than a Year?

One of the most intriguing aspects of persistent taste loss is how long it can continue despite the body's natural ability to replace taste cells.

Under normal circumstances, cells in the taste buds are renewed approximately every two to four weeks. Yet the researchers found signs that the molecular processes needed for normal taste signaling may remain disrupted for much longer in some people.

Most individuals who experience taste changes during COVID-19 regain their normal sense of taste within weeks or months. However, a small group continues to experience symptoms long after the infection has resolved. The new findings provide measurable biological evidence that could help explain why recovery takes so much longer for these patients.

"Our findings offer measurable evidence of long-term taste disruption in some post-COVID patients long after the virus has been cleared," the authors write.

Although the discovery sheds light on a previously unexplained symptom, important questions remain. Scientists still need to determine whether these molecular abnormalities can eventually return to normal and whether treatments designed to restore healthy taste signaling could help people recover their sense of taste.

#5286. Why some people escape Alzheimer’s despite carrying a high-risk gene

Some people carry one of the strongest genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease yet never develop it, remaining mentally sharp well into their 80s. For years, scientists have wondered what protects these individuals despite their increased genetic vulnerability.

Now, researchers at Columbia University have uncovered a biological mechanism that could help explain this remarkable resistance. Their findings point to a rare genetic mutation that protects the brain's blood vessels from damage, potentially opening a new path toward Alzheimer's treatments.

The discovery builds on research the team published two years ago involving APOE ε4, a gene variant strongly associated with Alzheimer's risk. The scientists found that some people carrying APOE ε4 also possess a rare protective mutation in the fibronectin (FN1) gene.

Fibronectin is a protein that helps support the structure of tissues. However, in people with APOE ε4, excessive amounts can accumulate around blood vessels in the brain, potentially compromising a critical protective system known as the blood-brain barrier. The rare FN1 mutation appears to limit this buildup and the damage associated with it.

That earlier discovery suggested an intriguing possibility: What if scientists could develop a treatment that recreates the protection provided by this rare mutation?

A new study published in Nature Aging brings that possibility closer by identifying how excess fibronectin damages the brain's protective barrier and revealing potential ways to interrupt the process.

"This new study takes us from a genetic clue to a disease mechanism," says Caghan Kizil, the study's corresponding author and professor of neurological sciences in the Department of Neurology and the Taub Institute for Research on Alzheimer's Disease and the Aging Brain at Columbia University Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons.

"We knew that changes in fibronectin could protect against Alzheimer's, but we didn't know why excess fibronectin was harmful in the first place. This study gives us that detailed mechanism -- and with it, ideas for how to reproduce that natural protection."

How an Alzheimer's Risk Gene Weakens the Blood-Brain Barrier

The blood-brain barrier serves as a protective boundary between the bloodstream and the brain. Made up of tightly connected cells surrounding the brain's blood vessels, it carefully regulates which substances can enter brain tissue while helping maintain conditions necessary for removing potentially harmful waste.

When this barrier becomes damaged or excessively permeable, substances that would normally be kept out can enter the brain more easily. Such disruptions may contribute to inflammation and other processes involved in neurological diseases, including Alzheimer's.

To investigate the role of fibronectin, the Columbia team combined several advanced research approaches. They examined human brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid surrounding the brain and spinal cord, using genetic and epigenetic analyses to explore changes in DNA and gene activity.

Their investigation also included single-cell sequencing, which reveals the activity of individual cells, and spatial imaging to examine where biological changes occur within tissues. Additional experiments involved human stem-cell models, three-dimensional cultures of blood vessels, and studies in zebrafish and mice.

Despite the variety of methods, the findings consistently pointed to the same mechanism: APOE ε4 promotes the production and accumulation of fibronectin around the brain's blood vessels.

In mice engineered to carry human APOE ε4, researchers measured nearly twice as much fibronectin in the brain as in mice carrying APOE ε3, another common version of the gene. The greater accumulation was accompanied by evidence that the blood-brain barrier had become leaky.

Scientists Discover How Fibronectin Causes Brain Damage

One experiment provided especially important evidence that fibronectin does more than simply accumulate in damaged blood vessels.

The researchers increased human fibronectin specifically in astrocytes, specialized support cells that help maintain the blood-brain barrier and keep the brain functioning properly.

Increasing fibronectin in these cells was enough to disrupt the barrier, allowing it to become more permeable. Conversely, reducing fibronectin improved the barrier's ability to function normally.

These results provided experimental evidence that excess fibronectin can directly contribute to blood-brain barrier damage rather than merely appearing alongside it.

"The animal experiments allowed us to move beyond association," says co-first author Prabesh Bhattarai, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "When we increased fibronectin in astroglia, the barrier became permeable. When we reduced fibronectin, barrier function improved."

The team then investigated the molecular events responsible for the damage.

They found that excessive fibronectin interferes with communication between cells by activating abnormal signals through integrins, proteins that help cells sense and respond to their surroundings, and an enzyme known as focal adhesion kinase.

These abnormal signals disrupt a network of growth factors called VEGF, HBEGF, and IGF1. Although growth factors are often associated with cell development and repair, these particular signaling molecules also help astrocytes and blood vessel cells coordinate their activities to maintain a healthy blood-brain barrier.

When their communication becomes disrupted, the barrier can lose its integrity, potentially leaving brain tissue more vulnerable to damage.

"Single-cell analysis allowed us to examine this pathway in the different cells that maintain the brain's blood vessels," says co-first author Elanur Yilmaz, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "We observed the same biological pattern across experimental models and human Alzheimer's tissue, making the mechanism especially compelling."

Evidence From Human Brains Strengthens the Alzheimer's Connection

Importantly, the researchers found signs of similar biological disturbances in human samples, suggesting that the process identified in laboratory experiments may also contribute to Alzheimer's disease in people carrying APOE ε4.

Analyses of brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid showed that elevated fibronectin levels were associated with inflammation in astrocytes.

Genetic and gene-regulation analyses also connected FN1 to blood vessel disease and changes involving VEGFA, a growth factor that plays an important role in maintaining healthy brain blood vessels.

Together, these findings provide multiple lines of evidence linking excess fibronectin to the vascular damage associated with APOE ε4.

"The convergence of these independent human datasets strengthens the case that fibronectin is part of the APOE ε4-related vascular disease process," says Badri Vardarajan, associate professor of neurological science in the Department of Neurology, Gertrude H. Sergievsky Center, and Taub Institute, who collaborated on this discovery of the FN1 mutation.

A Rare Genetic Defense Could Inspire New Alzheimer's Treatments

The potential implications are significant because APOE ε4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease. Approximately one in five people carries at least one copy of this gene variant.

Although carrying APOE ε4 does not guarantee that someone will develop Alzheimer's, it increases their likelihood of developing the disease. Understanding why certain carriers remain unaffected could reveal new opportunities for prevention and treatment.

The latest findings suggest several possible approaches.

One strategy would be to prevent harmful amounts of fibronectin from accumulating around the brain's blood vessels. Another would involve blocking the abnormal cellular signals triggered by excess fibronectin. A third possibility would be to restore the growth factor signaling that allows astrocytes and blood vessel cells to maintain the blood-brain barrier.

Researchers are already investigating these possibilities through laboratory experiments.

However, translating the discovery into a treatment will require considerable care. Fibronectin performs essential functions throughout the body, including supporting tissue structure and helping damaged tissues heal. Any future therapy would therefore need to target its harmful accumulation or effects without disrupting these beneficial activities.

By identifying the molecular mechanisms involved, scientists now have a more precise framework for designing such treatments. The findings may also help researchers determine when interventions would be most effective and how to measure whether they are successfully protecting the brain.

"By showing how fibronectin contributes to early vascular damage, this study gives us new therapeutic targets and potential ways to measure whether treatments are working," says Richard Mayeux, chair of the Department of Neurology and a collaborator on the project. "Clinically, it connects a major genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's to an early disease process that may be possible to modify."

Although these potential therapies remain experimental, the discovery offers a promising new direction in Alzheimer's research. Rather than focusing only on the damage that develops as the disease progresses, scientists may eventually be able to reproduce a natural genetic defense that helps protect vulnerable brains before significant damage occurs.

#5280. Opinion: Cancer vaccines are finally here. How do we get them to patients?

At the annual European Society for Medical Oncology Congress, a new report brought forward yet another monumental moment in cancer science: A Phase 3 study showed that the “cancer vaccine” intismeran autogene reduced the risk of recurrence or death by 42% compared with pembrolizumab (Keytruda) alone. This is the first neoantigen and mRNA therapy to have met its Phase 3 endpoint in oncology and is part of a collection of well-conducted studies showing promise for cancer therapeutic vaccines.

The question is no longer whether personalized cancer vaccines can work. The question now becomes how to accelerate development and increase access for the patients who could benefit.

The evolution of cancer genetics provides valuable insights as we consider cancer vaccine access. In 1990, a University of California, Berkeley, team led by Mary-Claire King identified the location of the BRCA1 gene, the first gene linked to hereditary breast cancer. BRCA2 was discovered five years later, and by 1996, the first commercial test for these two mutations hit the market. For the first time, people could learn whether they carried an inherited cancer risk before the disease appeared, and take potentially lifesaving action with earlier and more frequent screening, preventative surgery or prophylactic medication.

But patents and price kept testing expensive for years. Guidelines required a family history before testing. And no one in the care system clearly owned the whole process. The science arrived in the 1990s, but the delivery system never caught up. Thirty years later, the test now costs a few hundred dollars, but it’s still not reaching most patients whose cancers could have been caught earlier or prevented altogether.

If we want the coming wave of cancer vaccines to reach patients faster than BRCA testing did, there are several challenges to address now.

Today’s regulatory pathways were designed for drugs that are identical and for devices and tests that perform the same function for every patient. Forcing personalized vaccines into a traditional drug pathway has consequences. Regulatory requirements and market pressure together push drug manufacturers toward narrow indications, one tumor type and one disease stage at a time. Each narrow program carries its own trial costs, and those costs eventually show up in the therapy’s retail price.

There are early signs of movement. A forthcoming $200 million public-private partnership from the National Cancer Institute and Foundation for the NIH, called the Therapeutic Cancer Vaccines Initiative, aims to build clinical trial networks, data standards, and infrastructure to accelerate evidence development for personalized cancer vaccines and bring them to patients within the decade.

A framework that validates the neoantigen-selection pipeline and manufacturing platform, then allows faster extension to new cancers with rigorous post-market evidence, could lower costs and widen access without lowering the bar for safety.

Artificial intelligence can also cut cost and time at nearly every step in between, from tissue analysis to manufacturing quality control.

Personalized cancer vaccines are not a conventional pill or drug that can be manufactured in bulk and shipped — each vaccine is made for a specific patient’s cancer. Consider what it takes to get one patient their vaccine: A tumor sample must be collected and preserved properly at surgery. The tumor and a blood sample are then sequenced, and scientists use complex data pipelines to identify neoantigens and design the vaccine. The vaccine must then be manufactured, shipped, and administered on schedule, followed by years of monitoring.

Today, each of those steps would belong to a different organization, with its own contract, incentives, and economics, with no one accountable for the full delivery chain on something that is n-of-1 to that patient.

If we think of cancer vaccines as a drug, the cost and inconvenience of the cancer vaccine experience will drastically dilute their potential. We need to define the “product” not as what is in the vial but as an integrated end-to-end model of care that delivers a full patient experience and ensures optimal health outcomes.

For an individual patient, preventing a cancer recurrence is priceless. But to a payer, cancer vaccines will look like a very expensive form of prevention, not a required therapy to face a current disease. A health plan that expects members to churn within one to two years has little financial incentive to pay for an expensive “benefit” that another plan will collect.

Similar to cancer screenings, access becomes forced only through patient advocacy and eventual legislation. Coverage that is forced through legislation means that the friction to access the specific therapy or tool is not proactively addressed, let alone removed.

Yet the economics favor prevention of cancer recurrences. A recurrence that becomes metastatic disease can mean years of costly immunotherapy, imaging, and hospital care. Multiyear or outcomes-based contracts could let payers share in those savings, and Medicare, which covers many patients for life, doesn’t face the same churn problem. Employers and direct-to-patient programs may open further paths, though each raises equity concerns.

In practice, clinical guidelines determine what gets ordered, covered, and reimbursed. They are also slow. Many professional guidelines still have not caught up with the technological innovations that have come to market over the last 10-15 years, such as genetic and minimal residual disease testing. Personalized therapies will make guidelines even more challenging, as guideline bodies were built to evaluate a fixed product in a defined population. But if guidelines treat each new indication with a multiyear review, the new therapies will keep outrunning the recommendations.

Guideline organizations, professional societies, and payers should agree now on how they will evaluate platform therapies: what evidence is sufficient to extend a recommendation to a related setting, and how real-world data will be used to update it quickly.

At the recent STAT Summit in Boston, Gary Keblish, a New York City public school teacher, described what he called his path to a successful outcome after a high-risk melanoma diagnosis in 2019. After surgery, he enrolled in an earlier trial of intismeran autogene, and today, he is still teaching. The results presented at ESMO are not an abstraction for patients like Gary — they are the difference between planning around the next scan and planning for next year’s curriculum.

There is still much to learn about how to optimize these vaccines, which cancers they will work in, and which technologies work best. As that research continues, we must create a system within which these vaccines can reach patients as quickly as possible.

#5278. Why painful arthritis can return stronger after cancer treatment

For some cancer patients, the effects of immunotherapy can linger in an unexpected place: their joints. Painful arthritis may return repeatedly, sometimes becoming more severe with each episode. Now, scientists have uncovered a possible explanation involving the immune system's ability to remember previous attacks.

Researchers at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center have found evidence that arthritis triggered by certain cancer immunotherapies behaves like a disease driven by immune memory. Rather than launching an entirely new inflammatory response during each flare, the immune system appears to reactivate cells involved in earlier episodes.

The discovery could eventually help doctors identify patients at risk of recurring arthritis and develop treatments that control joint inflammation without interfering with the immune system's ability to fight cancer.

The study, published in Cancer Immunology Research, was led by scientists at UT MD Anderson and Yale University. The research team included Roza I. Nurieva, Ph.D., professor of Immunology; Synat Keam, Ph.D., a postdoctoral fellow in the Nurieva Laboratory; and Yuanteng Jeff Li, M.D., assistant professor of General Internal Medicine. Sang Taek Kim, M.D., Ph.D., an assistant professor at Yale University, also co-led the investigation.

"Cancer immunotherapy works by creating a lasting immune response against tumors, but that same persistence may also contribute to recurring inflammatory side effects that can be debilitating for patients," Nurieva said. "By identifying the immune cells that drive recurrent arthritis, we can inform treatment strategies that reduce the risk of future flares while preserving the therapy's effectiveness against cancer."

Why Cancer Immunotherapy Can Trigger Painful Arthritis

Immune checkpoint inhibitors are widely used cancer treatments that help the body's natural defenses recognize and attack tumor cells. These drugs are a form of immunotherapy that works by removing some of the molecular restraints that normally keep immune activity under control.

Although this approach can help the immune system destroy cancer cells, it can sometimes cause immune cells to attack healthy tissues as well. When the joints become targets, patients may develop inflammatory arthritis, a condition that causes pain, swelling, and inflammation.

For some patients, the problem does not disappear after the first episode. Approximately 20% to 50% of patients who develop this complication experience repeated arthritis flares, and symptoms can persist for months or even years after immunotherapy ends.

These recurring episodes are often more intense than the initial attack, raising an important question: Why does the immune system continue to produce inflammation, and why can its response become increasingly aggressive?

Scientists Discover Immune Cells That Remember Previous Attacks

To investigate, the researchers examined fluid taken from the joints of six patients during both their first arthritis episode and a later recurrence.

Their analysis revealed two distinct populations of immune cells that appeared during both flares. One consisted of inflammatory CD8 T cells, while the other was a specialized group of CD4 T cells expressing two markers known as PD-1 and CXCL13.

T cells are immune cells that help coordinate the body's defenses and respond to potential threats. Some can also persist after an initial immune response, allowing the immune system to react more quickly when it encounters a familiar target.

The presence of the same immune cell populations during separate arthritis attacks suggests that recurring inflammation may arise from cells already involved in the earlier episode. Instead of starting from scratch, the immune system appears to reactivate an established inflammatory response.

This immune memory mechanism could help explain why arthritis can repeatedly return even after cancer treatment has stopped.

Why Arthritis Flares May Become More Severe

The researchers also found that the recurring immune cells appeared more active during subsequent arthritis episodes. They produced increased amounts of inflammatory signaling molecules, which immune cells use to communicate and influence one another.

Another finding was particularly unexpected. Regulatory T cells, which normally act as a braking system to prevent excessive immune activity, also began producing inflammatory molecules during recurring flares.

This suggests that some of the cells responsible for keeping inflammation under control may instead contribute to it, potentially weakening the body's ability to shut down the response.

The researchers also identified extensive communication networks connecting inflammatory cells within affected joints. These signaling networks became more pronounced during the second arthritis episode.

Together, the findings suggest that recurring flares involve a coordinated and increasingly active inflammatory environment, rather than just a handful of immune cells acting independently.

Could Immune Memory Help Predict Future Arthritis Flares?

Despite growing recognition of inflammatory arthritis as a complication of cancer immunotherapy, scientists still do not fully understand why it affects certain patients while others never develop the condition.

The newly identified immune cell populations could provide an important starting point. Future studies may determine whether these cells can serve as biomarkers, meaning measurable biological indicators that help doctors recognize patients who are more likely to experience recurring arthritis.

Understanding which immune cells sustain the inflammation could also guide the development of more precise treatments. Ideally, such therapies would prevent or reduce painful arthritis flares while leaving the beneficial cancer-fighting effects of immunotherapy intact.

Because the investigation examined samples from only six patients, additional research will be needed to confirm the findings and establish whether the identified immune cells can reliably predict future episodes.

Still, the results offer a promising explanation for a difficult complication of cancer treatment: an immune system trained to remember and fight tumors may also retain inflammatory responses that continue to harm the joints.

In the winter of 2022, two parents carried their sick, coughing baby boy into an emergency room deep in California’s Sierra Nevada mountains. The small rural hospital had no pediatric specialists, no pediatric nurses, and no children’s intensive care unit. But it was hours closer than any hospital that did.

The doctor at that rural hospital recognized immediately that the baby’s condition was beyond their capacity to treat. They called regional children’s hospitals like ours for help. But the winter of 2022 was the peak of the “tripledemic” — Covid, influenza, and RSV striking children simultaneously — and every children’s hospital in northern California was overwhelmed. None of us had room for one more.

There was no single call the team at that hospital could make to locate a pediatric ICU bed. They worked a phone tree, hospital by hospital, asking for an opening. Meanwhile, the baby’s condition worsened. As the team tried to insert an infant-sized breathing tube, the baby died.

That patient’s death and others like it were galvanizing for those of us in on the other end of those desperate calls for help. Saying no to our colleagues and the patients they’re caring for feels horrible.

But the extraordinary crush of the tripledemic has continued. In 2026, the unprecedented pediatric patient crowding of 2022 has become a normal Tuesday.

In this new normal, community hospitals across the country are transferring children to dedicated pediatric centers not just because a child is critically ill, but increasingly because smaller hospitals have quietly lost the ability to manage even common conditions. A 1-year-old with an allergic reaction, a 6-year-old with an asthma attack, a teenager with gallstones — community hospitals once handled these patients locally. Now, as they degrade or eliminate pediatric care, that local safety net is disappearing. The nation’s relatively few children’s hospitals can’t accommodate every sick child, much as we would like to. We now routinely turn sick children away and hope one of our colleagues somewhere else will have a bed.

There is another story from that same winter of 2022. Two parents brought their sick baby girl to a small rural hospital in Washington state’s Northern Cascades. For 12 hours, doctors there tried to find a hospital that could take her. Again, no children’s hospitals had room.

Finally, the emergency physician called Washington’s Regional Medical Operations Coordination Center, or RMOCC — a kind of air traffic control for patient transfers that can match critically ill patients to available beds across a region. The RMOCC pediatrician who answered later recalled the terror in the calling doctor’s voice: “Don’t you know I feel like I am having a heart attack every moment I am caring for this tiny baby? It has been 12 hours. I am terrified she will die here. Why can’t anyone help?”

The RMOCC found a hospital willing to make an exception to its infection-control policies and accept the baby into a neonatal ICU. Unlike the California baby, she lived.

The Washington RMOCC closed last year after budget cuts. It is seeking renewed funding in this year’s budget.

There are many reasons for the collapse of pediatric capacity. Most are fundamentally financial. Hospital bottom lines require high reimbursement, high volume procedures, and expensive, complex treatments. Children’s care often reimburses below the cost of care, meaning that pediatric admissions for common childhood diseases lose money. Hospitals are incentivized to abandon pediatric care in favor of complex adult surgical programs and cancer treatments.

There are three changes in health care policy that could save and restore regional pediatric capacity.

First, Medicaid payments for pediatric care need to reflect the costs of that care. Hospitals can’t be asked to lose money on pediatric admissions and ED visits. Unfortunately, coming cuts to Medicaid seem likely to make reimbursement for children’s care worse rather than better. The incoming Congress must act to preserve and augment funding for pediatric care.

Second, regional RMOCCs like Washington state’s should be a standard part of regional disaster planning and a state and national funding priority. At least 19 states had regional or statewide MOCCs in 2023, though many were open only during surges, and some, like Washington’s, wound down with the end of the pandemic. When a sick child arrives at a rural emergency department, the hospital shouldn’t have to resort to a phone tree to find an accepting specialty facility. A single phone call to a regional air traffic control system for patient movement saves time and lives.

Finally, children’s hospitals need support to be regional resources to the smaller hospitals that depend on them for advanced pediatric care. Incentives for providing telehealth consultations, training, and quality improvement support for community hospitals are essential, as children’s hospitals face the same fiscal realities as their rural peers and cannot provide infinite unfunded support to their communities. Remote support from pediatric specialists can reduce the need for transfers and make local care safer, which could drive down pediatric care costs in the long term.

In pediatric medicine, we like to say that “children are not little adults.” Unfortunately, they are also not what health care executives call “priority populations.”

But children deserve to have quality care available in their own communities. They shouldn’t have to spend hours in waiting rooms and hallways awaiting transfer to overwhelmed regional centers. As pediatric physicians, we’ve gone from seeing the crisis coming to living the crisis every day. So far that crisis has been mostly hidden within the walls of our hospitals, but it’s time everyone know: Our children are out of room.

#5273. Just 3,000 steps a day may help slow Alzheimer's, scientists find

A new study suggests that even low levels of physical activity could protect the brain from Alzheimer’s disease – but not in the way scientists expected.

The researchers tracked almost 300 older adults with early brain signs of Alzheimer’s for nine to 11 years using pedometers. They found that physical activity didn’t reduce the toxic amyloid plaques that most Alzheimer’s treatments now target.

Instead, in people who already had these plaques, physical activity reduced the accumulation of misfolded tau proteins in specific brain areas. These proteins appear later in Alzheimer’s disease and are more closely linked to cognitive and functional decline. These signs of dementia were reduced by almost half in more active participants.

Benefits appeared at just 3,000 steps – roughly half an hour of walking at a moderate pace. The optimal range was 5,000 to 7,500 steps daily, after which the effect plateaued. More steps didn’t necessarily mean greater protection, which suggests a realistic target for older, sedentary people, rather than the often-cited 10,000 steps.

The study had limitations, however. It involved a fairly small group of mostly white, well-educated people in the US, and it didn’t take other lifestyle or health factors into account. Also, there may be other protective mechanisms of walking at play. But it does support other research suggesting that being physically active may lower the risk of dementia.

A UK study of 1,139 people over 50 found that those who were moderately to vigorously active had a 34-50% reduction in dementia risk when followed over eight to ten years. Among those who developed dementia, staying active reduced their memory decline, particularly in older women.

A larger 2022 UK study tracked 78,430 people for seven years using wrist accelerometers. It found a 25% reduction in dementia risk with just 3,800 steps daily, rising to 50% at 9,800 steps.

However, people who walked more also had better cardiovascular health – lower cholesterol, better sleep and blood pressure and reduced diabetes risk. Since these heart and stroke risk factors also increase dementia risk, the picture is complex.

Healthy habits often go together. People who exercise are more likely to eat well, not smoke, look after their heart health and have fewer financial stresses. This makes it hard to know which factor is having the biggest effect. The researchers tried to account for this, but because these habits are so closely linked, it’s difficult to say that exercise alone is responsible.

However, there is a strong case for this as there are multiple ways exercise might support the brain: improving cardiovascular health, increasing blood flow and boosting chemicals that promote brain-cell connections.

One such substance is irisin, a hormone produced by muscles that acts on almost all faulty brain mechanisms associated with Alzheimer’s, including inflammation. This and other chemicals, such as BDNF, associated with exercise, provide plausible biological pathways for how physical activity might directly influence brain health beyond its cardiovascular benefits.

But the relationship might work in reverse, too. People may become less active because of early Alzheimer’s symptoms. Those with hearing problems, for instance – itself a dementia risk factor – often report barriers that make them stop being active before other dementia symptoms appear.

Reduced activity then accelerates memory decline. This creates a vicious circle. Early disease symptoms – such as not hearing – can affect self-esteem and reduce engagement in physical activity, which in turn worsens cognitive decline.

Brisk walking might be particularly beneficial. A small trial of 15 people with mild to moderate Alzheimer’s who did Nordic walking (an enhanced walking technique that uses poles to work your upper body as well as your legs) maintained brain function over 24 weeks, with some functions even improving.

The 15 who received only standard care showed decline or no improvement. Though small, the trial suggests that even people already diagnosed with Alzheimer’s might benefit from increased physical activity, including brisk walking.

Getting outside, particularly in nature, may be especially beneficial for preventing dementia – possibly because it improves mood and sleep while reducing isolation – all dementia risk factors. The combination of physical movement, natural light exposure and social interaction when walking outdoors may create multiple protective effects that complement each other.

The challenge now is helping people overcome barriers to outdoor activity, such as safety concerns, fear of falling, or simply preferring the comfort of the sofa – particularly during wetter, colder months. But the evidence suggests that even a few minutes of walking could make a difference, and that modest, achievable targets – a half-hour stroll rather than a marathon training regime – may offer substantial protection against cognitive decline.

C. difficile infections (CDI) are among the leading causes of diarrhea associated with antibiotic treatment. The bacteria produce toxins that can trigger dangerous and sometimes life-threatening inflammation in the intestines. Because these infections frequently return after treatment, scientists are searching for more effective ways to eliminate the pathogen without causing additional damage to the gut microbiome.

A team led by Prof. Rolf Müller, department head and scientific director at HIPS, has now identified a promising drug candidate that specifically targets C. difficile while preserving much of the gut's beneficial microbial community. HIPS is a site of the Helmholtz Centre for Infection Research (HZI) that operates in collaboration with Saarland University.

Natural Compound Targets Dangerous Gut Bacteria

The researchers found that argyrin B, a naturally occurring antibacterial substance, was highly effective against C. difficile in laboratory tests and experiments involving infected mice. Importantly, the compound showed little activity against the beneficial microorganisms that help maintain a healthy digestive system.

"We were able to show that the natural compound argyrin B is highly effective against CDI: both in laboratory experiments and in a mouse model of the infection, where it significantly reduces the bacterial load," says Dr. Jennifer Herrmann, senior scientist in the department "Microbial Natural Products" at HIPS. "An antibacterial target of the argyrins had previously been identified and characterized in the notorious hospital-acquired pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Now we have been able to show that argyrin B also acts on this target in C. difficile and exhibits particularly high efficacy in doing so. In contrast, the argyrins have virtually no effect on the beneficial microorganisms in our digestive tract. The gut microbiome could therefore be spared during treatment with argyrin and restored to a healthy state more quickly."

A Bacteria-Killing Substance Found in Soil

Argyrins were originally discovered in myxobacteria, microorganisms that inhabit soil. These bacteria produce natural substances that help them attack other microbes and use them as a source of food.

Although argyrin B has demonstrated promising antibacterial activity, researchers must also determine whether its pharmaceutical properties make it suitable for use as a medicine. That includes understanding how the compound behaves inside the body and whether it could cause unwanted effects.

One particularly encouraging finding emerged when the team examined what happens after mice receive argyrin B orally. Very little of the compound entered their bloodstream. Instead, most of it remained in the large intestine, where C. difficile infections occur.

By concentrating in the gut rather than circulating throughout the body, argyrin B could potentially reduce the risk of side effects affecting other organs. Together with its selective activity against C. difficile, this characteristic makes the compound a promising candidate for a treatment that may be better tolerated.

Could It Help Prevent Recurring Gut Infections?

One of the biggest challenges in treating C. difficile is its tendency to return. Patients who experience multiple relapses are often treated using fecal microbiota transfer (commonly known as a stool transplant). This procedure aims to restore a healthier balance of gut microorganisms, but it can be complicated to perform and unpleasant for patients.

A medication developed from argyrin could eventually provide another treatment option. By eliminating the harmful bacteria without extensively disrupting the rest of the microbiome, such a therapy might reduce the likelihood of further infections and relapses. That could also help some patients avoid the need for stool transplants.

More research is needed before argyrin B can be considered for clinical use. The scientists plan to investigate which dosage produces the strongest antibacterial effect and use additional experimental models to determine whether the compound can also combat recurring C. difficile infections.

#5260. Scientists find 10 different diets linked to slower aging

A large study involving approximately 7,500 people suggests that eating a healthy diet may help slow biological aging, and there is no single eating plan that stands above all others. Scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) investigated 10 different dietary patterns, including the Mediterranean, Nordic, plant-based, and DASH diets. The DASH diet was specifically developed to help reduce blood pressure.

Every dietary pattern examined was associated with slower biological aging, as measured by chemical changes in DNA that reflect the body's aging process. The findings were first identified using data from DZNE's Rhineland Study and then independently confirmed in another population. The research was published in Nature Communications.

"Our study suggests that healthy eating goes hand in hand with slower biological aging. The effects are not massive, but they are measurable and relevant for prevention. By slowing the aging process, the risk of age-related diseases such as dementia or cardiovascular disorders can be reduced. Thus, healthy eating contributes to healthy aging," says Prof. Monique Breteler, Director of Population Health Sciences at DZNE and head of the Rhineland Study. "Importantly, there is probably no single 'correct' diet. According to our data, various dietary patterns are associated with slower aging, some more, some less. That is an encouraging finding, because it leaves room to tailor healthy eating to personal and cultural preferences, budget and taste."

To investigate the connection between diet and aging, the scientists analyzed dietary information and blood samples from roughly 7,500 men and women. The initial analysis included 6,470 participants in DZNE's Rhineland Study in Bonn, Germany. The researchers then tested their findings using data from another 1,034 people enrolled in the EPIC-Potsdam Study, conducted by the German Institute of Human Nutrition Potsdam-Rehbrücke (DIfE).

Rather than relying only on participants' chronological ages, the team looked for molecular signs of aging in their blood. Specifically, they measured DNA methylation, a process involving chemical modifications to DNA that help regulate gene activity and influence how cells function.

These chemical patterns change in relatively predictable ways as people grow older, allowing scientists to estimate biological age. Unlike chronological age, which simply measures how long someone has been alive, biological age offers clues about how the body is aging at the molecular level.

"We applied three different approaches, so-called epigenetic clocks, to read biological aging from DNA methylation," explains Juliana Tavares, a doctoral researcher at DZNE and lead author of the current publication. "To this end, we leveraged state-of-the-art technology, which allowed us to cover about 850,000 sites in the DNA. This is roughly twice as many as in most previous studies."

The next step was to determine how closely each participant followed the 10 dietary patterns. Researchers compared reported food consumption with the recommendations for each diet and assigned scores reflecting how well individuals followed those guidelines.

Because the diets emphasize different foods and nutritional goals, a person could receive a high score for one eating pattern while scoring poorly on another. In fact, participants who met one definition of healthy eating often did not meet the requirements of other diets.

"One and the same person could therefore rank high on one diet score and low on another. In other words: People who ate healthily by one standard were often not the same people who ate healthily by another. According to our data, hardly anyone counts as a healthy eater by every standard at once," says Tavares.

Despite those differences, the overall pattern was remarkably consistent. Better adherence to every one of the 10 diets was associated with slower biological aging. Some eating patterns showed stronger associations than others, but the absolute differences were relatively small.

"While there are measurable differences between the diets, they are modest in absolute terms. It's the big picture what matters," says Tavares. "In summary: When it comes to slowing down the aging process, there isn't just one healthy diet, but indeed a whole repertoire of possible diets with a positive effect."

One unexpected result emerged when researchers examined the association between diet and aging among smokers. The link between healthier eating and slower biological aging was stronger in smokers than in nonsmokers, even though smokers did not generally have healthier diets.

The researchers have not yet determined what biological processes might explain this difference. One possibility is that the extensive damage caused by smoking leaves greater room for measurable improvements associated with healthier eating. However, the finding does not lessen the well-established dangers of tobacco use.

"Smoking is very harmful to health," says Tavares, "One explanation for our findings might be that there is just more health benefit to gain for smokers by eating healthy because of the enormous detrimental effects of smoking."

Different Diets Share Common Aging Pathways

The scientists also investigated which biological processes might connect the different diets to slower aging. Although each eating pattern was associated with changes at a distinct collection of DNA methylation sites, the underlying biological pathways were surprisingly similar.

More than 70% of the affected pathways were shared across all 10 diets. These processes help regulate essential functions such as cellular structure, communication between cells, and metabolism. They also play important roles in aging and the development of chronic diseases.

The researchers identified some differences that reflected the specific goals of individual diets. For example, the DASH diet was associated with pathways related to heart health, while the MIND diet, which was developed to support brain health, showed distinct associations with pathways involved in memory and cognitive function.

"More than 70 percent of the affected biological pathways were shared across all diets. These involve cell structure, cell signaling and metabolism -- processes central to aging and chronic disease. Therefore, it is understandable that the dietary patterns we examined were all linked with a slowing of biological aging," says Tavares. "A few pathways were diet-specific, and they matched each diet's main objectives: heart-related pathways were unique to the DASH diet, and pathways related to cognition and memory were unique to the so-called MIND diet, which aims to support brain health. You don't need a perfect diet -- that may be the most practical message here. But moving forward, the diet-specific pathways point to future research on targeted dietary interventions. This could be helpful in designing targeted health recommendations."

#5253. Massive study of 460,000 people challenges ultra-processed food cancer fears

A massive study involving more than 460,000 Americans is challenging assumptions about ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer. Researchers found that eating more ultra-processed food (UPF) was not associated with a higher overall incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the findings revealed an important distinction: Some types of ultra-processed foods were linked to lower cancer rates, while others were associated with higher rates.

The research, led by Erikka Loftfield of the National Cancer Institute and published October 15 in the open-access journal PLOS Medicine, suggests that the health effects of ultra-processed foods may depend heavily on what those foods actually contain.

Ultra-processed foods account for more than half of the calories consumed by American adults. These products have attracted increasing scrutiny because of their potential connections to chronic diseases, including colorectal cancer (CRC). Yet the category includes a remarkably diverse assortment of foods, making it difficult to judge their health effects as a single group.

For example, some ultra-processed whole grain and dairy products have been associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer, while processed meats have been linked to a higher risk. This raises an important question about whether the level of processing alone is a useful indicator of a food's potential health effects.

A Massive Study Tracks Cancer Risk for Nearly 19 Years

To investigate these differences, researchers examined health and dietary records from 461,682 Americans who were between 50 and 71 years old when they joined the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study in 1995 and 1996.

At the beginning of the study, participants completed questionnaires describing how frequently they consumed different foods. Researchers used these responses to estimate each person's intake of ultra-processed foods.

Participants were then tracked through 2018, with a median follow-up period of 18.8 years. This allowed the team to examine how dietary patterns were associated with the development of colorectal cancer over nearly two decades.

The results showed no overall association between total ultra-processed food consumption and colorectal cancer incidence. In other words, participants who consumed more ultra-processed foods did not, as a group, experience higher rates of colorectal cancer.

However, a closer examination of individual food categories revealed a much more complicated picture.

Some Ultra-Processed Foods Linked to Lower Cancer Risk

The researchers found that higher consumption of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium from ultra-processed food sources was associated with a lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

Meat from ultra-processed sources showed the opposite pattern, with greater consumption associated with higher cancer incidence.

The team also examined what might happen when ultra-processed foods were replaced with less processed alternatives. Their analysis found that substituting minimally processed foods for 10% of ultra-processed food intake by weight was associated with a 2% lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

These findings suggest that the nutritional characteristics of individual foods may be more informative than simply classifying everything as ultra-processed. Foods placed in the same broad category can have very different associations with cancer risk.

Why the Findings Come With Important Limitations

Despite the study's large size and lengthy follow-up period, the researchers caution that its conclusions may not apply equally to everyone.

Most participants were older, white Americans, meaning the findings may not accurately reflect the experiences of younger adults or more racially and ethnically diverse populations.

Another important limitation involves how dietary information was collected. Participants reported their eating habits only once, at the beginning of the study. Researchers therefore could not account for changes in individual diets over the following decades.

The availability and variety of ultra-processed foods also increased during that time. As a result, the initial dietary assessments may have underestimated participants' actual consumption during the study period.

Because the research was observational, it also cannot establish whether particular foods directly increase or decrease cancer risk.

Rather than treating all ultra-processed foods as equally harmful, the findings support evaluating foods according to their nutritional content and specific health associations.

The results also align with existing dietary recommendations for colorectal cancer prevention, which encourage eating whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium-rich foods while limiting processed meat.

"In this large population of older US adults, we did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence; however, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention such that higher whole grain and dairy but lower meat intakes were associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence. "

Ultimately, the study highlights why the label "ultra-processed" may not tell the whole story. When it comes to colorectal cancer, the kinds of foods people eat appear to matter more than their processing category alone.

#5244. A simple 15-minute treatment could help patients avoid carpal tunnel surgery

A simple procedure that takes about 15 minutes could offer lasting relief to people with carpal tunnel syndrome, a painful condition that can cause numbness, tingling, and weakness in the hands. A new study suggests that the treatment, which uses ultrasound to guide an injection of ordinary saline around a compressed nerve, provides longer-lasting benefits than traditional steroid injections, without the risks associated with steroids.

The findings, published in Radiology, a journal of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA), point to a promising alternative for patients whose symptoms have not improved with conventional treatments. Researchers say the minimally invasive technique could also help some patients delay or avoid surgery.

Why Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Can Be So Difficult to Treat

Carpal tunnel syndrome develops when the median nerve, which extends from the forearm into the hand, becomes compressed as it passes through a narrow channel in the wrist called the carpal tunnel. Pressure on this nerve can cause pain, tingling, numbness, and muscle weakness, sometimes making everyday activities difficult.

The condition is commonly associated with repetitive hand and wrist movements. Studies suggest that approximately 5% to 10% of people develop carpal tunnel syndrome during their lifetime, and the number of cases is increasing.

One common treatment involves injecting corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and relieve pressure on the nerve. Although these injections can ease symptoms, the benefits often fade, and repeated treatments may be necessary.

"Traditional steroid injection for carpal tunnel syndrome often provides only temporary relief, thus requiring repeats of the procedure," said study co-author Anupama Tandon, M.D., professor in the Department of Radiodiagnosis and Imaging at University College of Medical Sciences and Guru Teg Bahadur Hospital in Delhi, India. "Further, steroids carry risk of localized skin changes, fat thinning and tendon weakening."

A Simple Procedure That Frees a Compressed Nerve

Researchers investigated an alternative technique known as nerve hydrodissection. Rather than relying on medication to reduce inflammation, this approach uses carefully placed fluid to separate the affected nerve from nearby tissues that may be restricting its movement.

Doctors use real-time ultrasound imaging to see the nerve and guide the injection precisely. The fluid helps loosen adhesions, which are areas where tissues stick together, allowing the nerve to move more freely.

Hydrodissection is often performed alongside steroid treatment. However, researchers have had limited evidence about whether the procedure can provide lasting relief without adding steroids.

To investigate, Dr. Tandon joined study lead author Anindita Bose, M.D., assistant professor in the Department of Radiology at Mahatma Gandhi Medical College and Hospital in Jaipur, India, and other researchers in conducting a randomized controlled trial involving 63 patients.

All participants had confirmed carpal tunnel syndrome and had continued experiencing symptoms despite conventional treatment.

The researchers randomly assigned patients to three equally sized groups. One received ultrasound-guided hydrodissection using normal saline, a sterile saltwater solution. Another underwent the same procedure with both saline and corticosteroids. The third received an ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injection without hydrodissection.

Carpal Tunnel Relief Lasted Longer Without Steroids

Researchers tracked each patient's progress using standardized measurements of pain, symptom severity, and hand function. They also performed ultrasound examinations to measure the size of the median nerve, an indicator of swelling.

These evaluations took place four weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks after treatment.

After four weeks, all three groups showed significant improvements. However, differences became increasingly apparent as the study continued.

Patients who underwent hydrodissection continued to experience improvements in their symptoms and hand function. Those who received steroid injections alone initially improved but began experiencing recurring symptoms during later follow-up visits.

"At 12 weeks and 6 months, both hydrodissection groups demonstrated further improvement in clinical symptoms and function, whereas patients who received corticosteroid injection alone experienced a recurrence of the symptoms, suggesting that the initial benefit was short-lived," Dr. Tandon said.

Surprisingly, adding corticosteroids to the hydrodissection fluid did not produce a statistically significant additional long-term benefit. Using ordinary saline alone appeared to work just as well over the six-month follow-up period.

Ultrasound measurements provided additional evidence of the treatment's effectiveness. In patients who underwent hydrodissection, the cross-sectional area of the median nerve decreased by approximately 43% to 45%. Among those treated with corticosteroid injections alone, the reduction was only about 15%.

The greater decrease in nerve size suggested that hydrodissection was more effective at reducing swelling around the compressed nerve.

"Most of our patients had been suffering from carpal tunnel syndrome for months to years and did not get much relief despite trying various treatments like pain medications, splints, lifestyle modification and corticosteroids," Dr. Tandon said. "It came as a pleasant surprise to find that a simple procedure of dissecting the median nerve under ultrasound guidance with normal saline provided them long-term relief."

A 15-Minute Treatment That Could Help Patients Avoid Surgery

Beyond providing sustained symptom relief, hydrodissection offered several practical advantages.

The procedure lasted just 15 minutes on average and was generally well tolerated. Researchers observed no significant complications, either immediately afterward or during the follow-up period.

Patients also reported high satisfaction with their treatment. The procedure was inexpensive, required neither anesthesia nor hospitalization, and allowed participants to return to work within an hour.

These advantages could make hydrodissection an attractive option for people seeking relief without undergoing surgery or receiving repeated steroid injections.

The findings are particularly relevant as carpal tunnel syndrome becomes more common worldwide.

"Overall, the study suggests that ultrasound-guided saline hydrodissection is a safe, minimally invasive treatment that can provide sustained clinical and morphological improvement in patients with refractory carpal tunnel syndrome," Dr. Tandon said. "Thus, hydrodissection merits to be a minimally invasive option for mild to moderate carpal tunnel syndrome and can defer and even alleviate the need for surgery."

Larger Studies Will Test How Long the Benefits Last

Although the findings are encouraging, the researchers emphasize that additional studies are needed before the treatment's long-term effectiveness can be fully established.

The trial included only 63 participants, and patients were monitored for six months. Larger studies involving multiple medical centers and longer observation periods could help determine how consistently hydrodissection works and whether its benefits continue beyond the initial follow-up period.

The research team has already begun another investigation comparing different fluids used during hydrodissection, including saline and dextrose, a form of sugar.

The goal is to identify which approach produces the most durable improvements for people with carpal tunnel syndrome.

"This trial features an extended follow-up period to track long-term patient outcomes and establish the true durability of the treatment," Dr. Tandon said.

#5239. Just 4 minutes a day can dramatically improve strength after 65

A daily strength training routine lasting just four minutes can significantly improve strength, balance, and mobility in older adults, according to new research led by Penn State College of Medicine. The findings suggest that maintaining physical independence with age may require much less exercise than many people assume.

Published in the journal PLOS One, the study found that adults aged 65 and older experienced meaningful improvements in physical performance after only 12 weeks. These gains are especially important because muscle strength and mobility influence the risk of falling, the ability to live independently, and overall health and longevity.

For older adults, being able to move comfortably and perform everyday activities is essential for maintaining quality of life. Simple tasks such as standing up from a chair, climbing stairs, and walking can become more difficult as strength and balance decline.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, unintentional injuries, including those caused by falls, are among the leading causes of death in adults aged 65 and older.

Despite the importance of physical fitness, many older adults believe that meaningful improvements require long, demanding workouts. Christopher Sciamanna, professor of medicine and public health at Penn State College of Medicine and lead author of the study, says the new findings challenge that assumption. Even a brief daily routine can improve important measures of mobility and physical function.

"The human body is designed to improve very quickly," Sciamanna said. "And just a few repetitions of an exercise performed regularly can lead to huge improvements. Exercise is about forward thinking -- think about what you want to be able to do and train for it."

Why Shorter Workouts Could Make Exercise Easier

Strength training, also known as resistance training, involves making muscles work against some form of resistance, such as body weight or elastic bands. Research has shown that it can substantially improve muscle strength within a few months.

However, fewer than one in five older adults meet the recommendation to perform muscle strengthening activities at least twice a week. Common obstacles include discomfort, limited time, and the physical demands of conventional exercise programs.

Another challenge is that planning an effective workout can feel overwhelming, particularly for people who are unfamiliar with exercise.

"Exercise is actually really complicated, because you have to decide how many repetitions, how far, how many sets, how much rest, and how many times per week," said co-author Smita Dandekar, associate professor of pediatrics at Penn State College of Medicine. "It's hard work, so there's huge problems with people wanting to do exercise. If we can make it short, we're part way there."

The researchers had already seen encouraging results from an earlier experiment called FAST (Functional Activity Strength Training)-1. In that smaller study, 24 older adults performed just 30 seconds of push-ups and squats each day. After six months, their ability to perform squats had improved.

Other research has similarly suggested that performing only a few sets of exercises each week can produce strength improvements approaching those achieved with considerably longer workouts.

These findings led Sciamanna and his colleagues to investigate whether an especially brief exercise program could produce measurable improvements in older adults.

Testing a Simple Four-Minute Exercise Routine

For the new study, the Penn State researchers developed a program called FAST-2 to evaluate whether a short daily workout could improve mobility and physical ability in adults older than 65.

The trial included 97 participants whose average age was 74. They were randomly divided into two groups, with one following the exercise program and the other receiving no intervention.

"Exercise is the key to freedom," he said. "Freedom is the ability to be able to do what you want to do, and I would say that if you can't do what you want to do, you're not free. I approach exercise by thinking of what I want to be able to do in 20 years, and then I train to do that."

The desire to ramp up a road to these kinds of home is understandable: Prefab houses are way less expensive than your typical home—and often just as beautiful. Indeed, many of today's models can be easily mistaken for a traditionally built home.

Buying land to put a prefab home on is part of the adventure. Do you want to live on 40 lush acres in Stockton, MO? A desert oasis in Joshua Tree, CA? Or how about an off-grid lot in Grants Pass, OR? The possibilities are endless.

If you're dreaming of buying a shiny, new modular or manufactured home, here's what you need to know before you purchase land to put it on.

Prefabricated homes are constructed via an assembly line production in sections inside a factory and then assembled on-site.

Modular homes are constructed to the same standards as conventionally built homes and must meet regional building codes. Homebuyers can choose from one- or two-story homes in a wide variety of floor plans and architectural styles, such as a Cape Cod, Craftsman bungalow, Colonial, A-frame, or farmhouse.

Manufactured homes (also known as single-wide and double-wide) don't need to comply with local building codes but must adhere to the strict federal building codes set by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development. Manufactured homes aren't as customizable as modular homes, but they offer several different floor plans and add-ons.

A great number of tiny homes on the market are prefabricated houses that are built in factories and then assembled on site.

Many accessory dwelling units, which are gaining popularity, are also prefabricated homes—otherwise known as modular ADUs.

When it comes to the costs of these types of homes, there are so many variables to consider.

First, there's the cost of the prefab home itself.

The average sales price of a manufactured home is $124,300, according the most recent HUD and Census Bureau data.

Meanwhile, a 600-square-foot, two-story tiny home that comes complete with a roof deck, glass sunroom, and up to four bedrooms is currently for sale on Amazon for $49,999.

But that's not "all-in pricing" by any means.

The cost of the land you put your prefab home on can vary widely—as can the expenses you'll likely incur next.

You've got to pay for site preparation—including clearing the land, laying the foundation, and possibly testing the soil.

Transporting the home to the site, as well as securing permits and inspections, can be quite expensive.

If your home is unassembled, you'll need to hire a contractor to put it together, unless you have the know-how to do it yourself.

And you'll need to cover the costs of setting up utilities like water, electricity, and sewage, and hooking them up as well.

All of these expenses are outlined in greater detail below.

You can't just plop down your new modular or manufactured home anywhere you like.

Many neighborhoods don't allow manufactured homes or tiny homes, so it's important to do your due diligence before signing on the dotted line.

Zoning ordinances, setbacks, and other requirements must be met before your home can be delivered.

Luckily, you don't always have to figure that out yourself, depending on what type of prefab home you buy.

"Our home consultants help future homeowners navigate the land-buying process to accommodate the home they are interested in purchasing," says Danny Warrick, Clayton Homes retail president.

Zoning ordinances designate certain areas for residential purposes. And your new home will need to meet the size, shape, height, and other requirements of your local zoning board.

As recent Realtor.com research reveals, as of 2026, only about 17% of zoned land across the country expressly allows for manufactured home parks, and manufactured housing is prevented in many jurisdictions by zoning restrictions on minimum floor sizes, architectural design, and by-right installation.

If you buy a manufactured home, your home salesperson can help determine if your home checks all the boxes for a piece of land.

A property's setbacks will also be vital to determining if a home can be placed on a property. Setbacks determine things like underground facilities and breathing room from your neighbors. You probably won't have much to worry about if you have your eye on a large parcel of land, but the setbacks could limit your options in town.

And a warning if you have your sights on a coveted corner lot: "The building department typically considers a corner lot to have two front setbacks, which are typically the largest footage from each street," says Leo Poggione, chairman of the Manufactured Housing Institute and managing partner of Craftsman Homes.

You'll probably be able to set up electricity and internet services relatively easily if you buy a lot in town. But you could hit a few snags if your property is in a rural setting.

The same goes for internet service. You don't want to end up with sketchy internet or none at all. Instead of the typical installation fee, utility companies charge by the linear foot when running cable to a rural property. And that could really add up, depending on how far the house will sit from the main line.

"If not, the property will need to pass a percolation test for a septic system and a well," says Warrick.

If the soil perks too fast or too slow, this can add a large expense. Basically, you will have to have an "engineered system" designed to accommodate the septic's leach fields.

You might not think this isn't your problem. After all, you're not the one driving the truck.

Yet, it's critical the truck transporting your new home can safely get to the property you have in mind.

Urban settings can be trickier than rural ones, Poggione notes.

Crowded towns can have low-hanging utility wires, overgrown trees, and department-of-transportation-specific routes for transporting wide loads.

"Always consult with your dealer before purchasing the land or the home, and have them do a site inspection," advises Poggione.

You'll also need to prepare the land for your home once you buy it.

"Trees may need to be cleared, grade works completed, and the home needs a home pad or concrete footings," says Warrick.

Make sure to account for all these expenses when planning the budget for your prefab home, as they can quickly accumulate.

#5232. Natural compound could fight rheumatoid arthritis at its source

Scientists have identified a natural plant compound that could offer a surprising new approach to fighting rheumatoid arthritis. In experiments with rats, the substance reduced joint swelling, protected cartilage, and calmed harmful immune activity. Researchers also discovered that it works through an unexpected mechanism involving the way the body processes fats.

The findings, published in the journal Engineering, center on a compound called obakulactone (OL), a naturally occurring tetracyclic triterpenoid extracted from Phellodendri cortex, a type of plant bark. Tetracyclic triterpenoids are plant chemicals characterized by a structure containing four interconnected rings.

The researchers found that obakulactone helps restore balance to unsaturated fatty acids, which play important roles in cellular function and inflammation. It does this by promoting the breakdown of a protein called acyl coenzyme A thioesterase 1 (ACOT1), using the cell's natural protein disposal system, known as the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway.

By identifying this previously underexplored molecular target, the study suggests that changing how cells process fatty acids could become a new strategy for treating rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Natural Compound Reduces Arthritis Inflammation and Joint Damage

To investigate the compound's effects, scientists created a rat model of rheumatoid arthritis using complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA), a substance that triggers a strong inflammatory response resembling certain features of the disease.

The animals received obakulactone for 21 days at three different daily doses: low (50 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), medium (100 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), and high (200 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹). These amounts were calculated according to body weight.

The results were encouraging. Rats treated with the compound experienced significantly less joint swelling, while the structure of their cartilage and synovium returned toward normal. The synovium is the thin membrane lining joints that produces lubricating fluid and can become severely inflamed in rheumatoid arthritis.

The treatment also improved disease-related changes in the thymus and spleen, two organs that play important roles in the immune system.

Beyond these physical improvements, researchers observed major changes in immune activity. Obakulactone reduced elevated levels of CD3⁺ T cells and CD68⁺ macrophages in joint tissues. These immune cells normally help defend the body, but they can contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue damage when the immune system becomes overactive.

The compound also shifted macrophages away from an inflammation-promoting M1 (CD86) state toward a more inflammation-resolving M2 (CD206) state. At the same time, it suppressed the development of CD4⁺ T cells into Th17 cells, a type of immune cell associated with autoimmune inflammation.

Blood tests provided further evidence of these effects. Obakulactone reduced several inflammatory signaling proteins, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α. These proteins, known as cytokines, help immune cells communicate but can also intensify inflammation when produced in excessive amounts.

The treatment also lowered rheumatoid arthritis related blood markers, including rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (CCP-Ab), C-reactive protein (CRP), and matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3). The reductions became more pronounced as the dose increased.

Scientists Uncover a Surprising Link Between Fat Metabolism and Arthritis

One of the study's most intriguing findings involved fatty acids, molecules that are commonly associated with nutrition and energy storage but also influence inflammation throughout the body.

Using several advanced analytical methods, including metabolomics, MALDI mass spectrometry imaging, and proteomics, the researchers examined how obakulactone affected the chemical processes inside tissues.

These techniques allowed them to investigate changes in small molecules, map their distribution within tissues, and measure differences in protein levels.

The analysis revealed that the compound helped correct abnormalities in the production and breakdown of unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid).

These fatty acids are important components of cell membranes and can serve as raw materials for molecules that regulate inflammation. Arachidonic acid, in particular, is involved in producing several powerful inflammatory signals.

In the rats with arthritis, obakulactone helped bring these disrupted metabolic processes back toward a healthier balance. This suggested that its benefits extended beyond simply suppressing immune activity.

A Hidden Protein Emerges as a Potential Treatment Target

To understand exactly how the compound worked, the scientists conducted additional laboratory experiments involving rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (SFs).

These cells help maintain the tissue lining the joints. However, in rheumatoid arthritis, they can become abnormally aggressive, multiplying excessively, releasing inflammatory substances, and contributing to cartilage damage and tissue scarring.

Obakulactone slowed the growth of these cells, reduced their production of inflammatory cytokines, and encouraged apoptosis, the controlled process through which the body eliminates unwanted or damaged cells.

The researchers then investigated which protein the compound was acting on directly.

Using cellular thermal shift assays, microscale thermophoresis, and surface plasmon resonance, they identified ACOT1 as a direct molecular target of obakulactone.

The binding measurements produced a dissociation constant (Kd) of (6.18 ± 0.26) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by microscale thermophoresis (MST)) and (6.34 ± 0.38) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR)).

These closely matching results provided evidence that the compound physically interacts with ACOT1. A dissociation constant describes how strongly two molecules bind to one another, with lower values generally indicating tighter binding.

Importantly, obakulactone did more than bind to the protein. It promoted ACOT1's destruction through the ubiquitin‒proteasome system, a cellular mechanism that marks proteins for removal and breaks them down.

This process reduced the activity of several downstream mechanisms associated with inflammation and abnormal cell behavior.

How Targeting One Protein Can Disrupt Inflammation

When ACOT1 was broken down, levels of another enzyme called stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) also decreased. This enzyme helps regulate the production of certain unsaturated fatty acids.

The changes subsequently suppressed two major cellular signaling networks: Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT).

These pathways act like internal communication systems, carrying signals that influence how cells grow, survive, and respond to their surroundings. When their activity becomes abnormal, they can contribute to inflammation and excessive tissue growth.

By interfering with these signals, obakulactone reduced inflammation and fibrosis, a process involving excessive buildup of scar-like connective tissue, in rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts.

Additional experiments, including rescue tests and studies using molecular inhibitors, strengthened the evidence that ACOT1 was central to these effects.

The researchers confirmed that the compound's ability to reduce inflammation, slow abnormal cell proliferation, and encourage apoptosis depended on its influence over ACOT1, the arachidonic acid pathway, and the downstream JAK-STAT/PI3K-AKT signaling pathways.

Together, the findings point to a potential way of disrupting the biological processes that sustain arthritis inflammation rather than focusing exclusively on suppressing immune responses.

A Potential New Direction for Rheumatoid Arthritis Treatment

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, particularly the joints. It affects approximately 1% of people worldwide and can cause persistent pain, swelling, stiffness, and progressive joint damage.

Because it is a systemic disease, rheumatoid arthritis can also affect organs and tissues beyond the joints.

Although existing medications can reduce symptoms and slow disease progression, they do not work equally well for everyone, and some can cause serious side effects. This makes the search for additional treatment approaches especially important.

The new findings suggest that obakulactone could become a candidate for future rheumatoid arthritis drug development. They also identify ACOT1 as a potentially valuable treatment target and highlight the importance of unsaturated fatty acid metabolism in controlling inflammation.

However, the results remain preclinical. The compound has been tested in rats and laboratory cells, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether it is safe and effective in humans.

Even with that limitation, the discovery opens an intriguing possibility: targeting the metabolic machinery that helps drive inflammation could provide another way to combat rheumatoid arthritis and potentially protect joints from lasting damage.

#5228. Coffee calories have jumped nearly 40%. Sugar isn't the biggest culprit

Americans are getting more calories from their daily coffee, even though the amount they drink has barely changed. Researchers at the Gerald J. and Dorothy R. Friedman School of Nutrition Science and Policy at Tufts University have identified a surprising reason for the increase: non-dairy creamer, which has overtaken sugar and milk as the largest contributor to added calories in coffee.

The study, published online in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, examined dietary information from 30,000 American adults aged 20 and older. Between 2003 and 2018, average coffee consumption stayed relatively constant at approximately 13 ounces per day. However, the average number of calories consumed from coffee increased from 43 to 60 per day, a rise of nearly 40%. By 2018, the leading sources of added coffee calories were non-dairy creamer, sugar or syrup, and dairy milk.

"Coffee consumption has been associated with several health benefits. However, coffee is often accompanied by the addition of sugar and cream, which can contribute to an increased consumption of calories from added sugar and saturated fat," said Fang Fang Zhang, senior author of the study and Neely Family Professor at the Friedman School. "The more we know about what people are consuming, the better we can understand the link between nutrition and overall health."

How Researchers Tracked Changes in Coffee Habits

To investigate how Americans' coffee habits evolved, the research team examined eight consecutive cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), covering 2003 through 2018. The nationally representative dataset included 30,000 adults aged 20 and older, each of whom completed two 24-hour dietary recalls.

Researchers separated coffee beverages into two broad groups: non-combination beverages and those containing additional ingredients. They then classified the additions into several categories, including dairy milk, half-and-half, dairy cream, non-dairy milk, non-dairy creamer, sugar/syrup, sugar substitutes, and other ingredients such as alcohol and cocoa powder.

Some participants reported consuming pre-mixed coffee beverages/powder, making it necessary to estimate the amounts of individual ingredients they contained. To do this, the researchers used the USDA Food and Nutrition Database for Dietary Studies corresponding to each survey cycle, along with linked Standard Reference codes.

Non-Dairy Creamer Consumption More Than Doubled

The analysis showed that Americans' overall coffee intake, including coffee consumed without additions, remained largely unchanged between 2003 and 2018. What changed was the amount and type of ingredients being mixed into those beverages.

In 2018, dairy milk was the most commonly added ingredient by weight, averaging 21.4 grams per day. Non-dairy creamer followed at 8.1 grams per day, while sugar accounted for 4.4 grams per day. Consumption of dairy milk, sugar, and sugar substitutes showed no statistically significant changes over the study period.

Non-dairy additions, however, followed a very different pattern. Their average consumption rose from 3.65 grams per day in 2003 to 10.5 grams per day in 2018. Much of this growth came from non-dairy creamer, which increased from 3.37 to 8 grams per day. Non-dairy milk consumption also climbed sharply, rising from 0.27 to 2.45 grams per day.

Other coffee additions increased slightly as well, with an average gain of 0.5 grams per day.

Why Non-Dairy Creamer Now Adds More Calories Than Sugar

Although dairy milk remained the most common coffee addition by weight, non-dairy creamer had become the leading source of added calories by 2018.

On average, non-dairy creamer contributed 18.5 calories per day, compared with 16 calories from sugar and 11 calories from dairy milk. In 2003, non-dairy creamer had contributed just 7.71 calories per day, illustrating how dramatically its role in Americans' coffee consumption had changed.

"We found that non-dairy creamer being added to coffee has now passed both sugar and dairy for its contribution to the total energy consumed from coffee. Non-dairy creamers can be high in saturated fat, which is calorie dense. This could explain the increased calories that non-dairy creamer is contributing," said Yongyi Pan, the study's lead author and a Ph.D. candidate at the Friedman School. "Even though it has been on the market since the 1950s, non-dairy creamer has increased in popularity in recent years as consumers seek plant-based or dairy alternatives."

Sleep, Work Hours, and Coffee Consumption

The researchers also investigated whether coffee consumption differed across groups of adults based on their daily routines and overall diet quality.

They found that people who slept fewer hours generally drank more coffee than those who slept longer. A similar pattern emerged among adults who worked longer hours, who tended to consume more coffee than those with shorter work schedules.

However, coffee consumption was comparable among people with higher and lower overall diet quality, suggesting that drinking more or less coffee was not strongly associated with how healthy their broader diets were.

The researchers acknowledged that the study relied on participants recalling what they had eaten and drunk, which can introduce measurement errors. Because food and beverage consumption can vary considerably from day to day, self-reported dietary information does not always perfectly reflect people's usual habits. The research team took steps to improve the reliability of its estimates.

Co-author Bingjie Zhou contributed to the research while pursuing a Ph.D. at the Friedman School.
