#5419. NHTSA Proposes Cyan Lights For AVs, And Other Conveniently Tesla-Specific Changes

The U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) has announced more proposed changes to Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 108, which governs vehicle lighting regulations. The latest changes, announced Oct. 23, include establishing equipment specifications for vehicles with automated driving systems, including displaying cyan lights to signal when an automated driving system is operational, and removal of the requirement for autonomous vehicles (AVs) with no driver controls to have manually operated turn signals, hazard lights, and headlight beam switching controls or telltales.

This goes beyond the announcement NHTSA made earlier this month that it would modernize U.S. headlight regulations, which addressed headlight glare on other road users, improving performance, updating testing and materials, and modernizing lighting technology with rules for LEDs, dusk sensors, auto high beams, and replaceable light sources.

The Tesla Cybercab is the only car on American roads that does not have physical driving controls like a steering wheel or pedals, and it's currently the subject of a safety probe because of that lack of traditional controls. These proposed changes to Standard No. 108 almost certainly have absolutely nothing to do with the fact that Tesla CEO Elon Musk has donated over $400 million to Republicans this election cycle.

Mercedes-Benz got approval to use turquoise lights on its cars equipped with Drive Pilot, the company's Level 3 autonomous driving technology, in 2023. Mercedes made this decision to let other drivers and law enforcement know that a person is allowed to be distracted while their car is legally in control.

The new proposal will not require autonomously driving cars to have these cyan lights, but it legally establishes what NHTSA calls "an 'if equipped' standard" that regulates light color, as well as setting mounting and visibility rules for companies that choose to use them. If a vehicle is equipped with Automated Driving Systems marker lamps, at least one should be visible from each of the front, sides, and rear of the vehicle, at a proposed minimum height of 15 inches, and the lights can't be brighter than the parking lights.

Other proposed changes include a removal of the headlight testing requirements that currently require headlights in all vehicles to meet performance parameters when tested at 12.8 volts. It also aims to eliminate the current regulation that forces the uppermost light in a vertically oriented headlights to operate as the standard beam, since NHTSA has tentatively concluded that it's possible to provide adequate illumination for safe driving without requiring the low beam to be placed above the high beams.

The proposal incorporates what NHTSA describes as "a process to remove unnecessary barriers to technological innovation while ensuring motor vehicle safety is not compromised." It seems the primary "barriers to technological innovation" in autonomous vehicles are the current requirement for all cars to have a turn signal operating unit, as well as the requirement for all vehicles to be equipped with a headlamp beam switching device.

Proposed modifications to the regulation would specify that these changes would only apply to vehicles that are not equipped with manually operated driving controls, so there would be no changes in regulations for vehicles with a steering wheel and pedals.

Finally, the proposal changes the requirements for telltales, which are the sounds and little lights on your dashboard that illuminate when your turn signal or hazard lights are activated, or when your low-beam headlights or high-beam headlights are activated. NHTSA now wants to specify that these telltales are only required for cars with a driver, which it defines as "an occupant of a motor vehicle seated immediately behind the steering control system." By this definition, an autonomous vehicle without a steering wheel cannot have a driver, so it shouldn't be required to have these telltales.

As autonomous vehicle technology comes closer to reality, it's likely that many other NHTSA regulations will have to change, so we will keep you updated as those changes roll out. For now, NHTSA wants your input on this crop of changes; there are several ways to do it, outlined in the Federal Register.

#5418. From Barrel to Battery: What Will Europe’s Clean Car Debate Decide

The new energy reality: 1 kWh of battery is cheaper than a barrel of oil

The EU is fiercely debating the future of the automotive industry and how far the transition to battery electric cars will be slowed down. Last time we had this debate back in 2022, Europe was likewise in the middle of an energy crisis. But today, much has changed and the debate is completely out of touch with reality.

In 2022, the European Green Deal set the direction and demonstrated true political leadership: the future is electric. New cars sold after 2035 would no longer rely on polluting, expensive, imported fossil fuels.

At the time, Europe was suffering from the aftermath of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. Oil prices soared to $120 per barrel, while lithium ion battery prices stood at $170 per kWh.

Today, however, the EU is on the verge of dismantling the Green Deal and specifically its poster child, the clean car targets. Meanwhile, another major geopolitical event has sent oil prices surging back towards 2022 levels. The conflict in the Middle East and the resulting spike in energy prices have once again laid bare the consequences of fossil fuel dependency. European drivers are paying the price, just as they did in previous crises. But unlike in the past, the solution is now readily available.

Since 2022, average battery prices have dropped dramatically to $105 per kWh in 2026 globally.

While oil prices repeatedly breached $105 per barrel between March and May, over the past three weeks they have experienced a sharp surge, continuously climbing well past the $105 mark.

This is a symbolic moment. A kWh of battery capacity costs less than a barrel of oil.

Batteries benefit from compounding learning curves and economies of scale that will continue to drive prices down. Oil, by contrast, remains an inherently volatile commodity bound to geopolitical instability and supply shocks.

Over its lifetime a car will consume more than 100 barrels of petrol which requires more than 200 barrels of crude oil. By contrast, the electric car needs around 50 kWh of battery to travel the same distance. Unlike burned fuel, that battery remains a reusable, recyclable asset long into the future.

This has profound practical implications for European drivers. This year, high road fuel prices led to an oil crisis premium of €55 billion for cars, or €220 euros per car every year. Electric vehicle drivers on the other hand have been largely unaffected by the higher energy prices and currently pay on average 2–3 times less for fuel than petrol and diesel car drivers.

This simple comparison underscores a bigger story, one of political leaders failing in their responsibility to protect European households and the wider economy.

While the EU weathers what the IEA has called the “mother of all energy crises,” the European Parliament and Member States are formalising a deal that includes a backwards-looking vision for the future of the automotive industry. One that allows biofuels cars to replace electric cars, despite all the evidence showing it’s an economic, industrial and technical dead end.

Many political leaders are trying a last desperate tactic to extend the life of the combustion engine by any means possible. Effectively, the proposals discussed would gut the car CO2 regulations by allowing carmakers to comply without selling a single electric car, shielding fossil fuel and biofuel interests while undermining the business case for Europe’s homegrown EV and battery industries.

Circumstances have changed dramatically since December 2025, when the European Commission first proposed easing the 2035 combustion-car phase-out. Politicians must act decisively to protect citizens and make this fossil fuel crisis Europe’s last one. We need an immediate reset of the electric car debate to protect European industry and consumers, as demanded in June by a broad coalition of industry groups and civil society.

The electric car market is surging, with the two biggest markets, France and Germany, seeing the sales of electric cars double in the past few months, compared to the previous year. Now is not the time to choke off that momentum and tell drivers and industry leaders that they just have to live with our debilitating oil dependency.

This autumn, the European Parliament and Council must send a signal to industry: it should focus on the technology of the future, or Europeans will pay the price of remaining in the fossil fuel past.

#5414. Wouldn’t It Be Funny If Audi Manufactures EVs In The US?

Audi is the only upscale automaker without a factory of its own in the US, and that is about to change. On Saturday, Audi announced plans for a new, $4 billion factory in Peach County, Georgia. US President Donald Trump could not resist making political hay out of the news, but then again, the joke may be on him. Audi’s plan for the US market reportedly includes plug-in hybrids with a battery range of range of 70-80 miles. So much for the war on EVs…

Plug-in hybrid EVs have fielded a fair share of criticism for only going halfway — or less — down the decarbonization trail. However, here in the US where long distance commutes are common, plug-in hybrids provide drivers with a gasoline-soaked security blanket that is difficult to shake. For those with less onerous driving schedules, the high cost of gas provides ample incentive to log as many miles on battery power as possible.

Regardless of driver habits in the US, Audi CEO Gernot Döllner is evidently among those anticipating that the US market will come around to full battery EVs eventually, following the lead of China and Europe. “In China, we see a complete transition to battery electric, and the same is true for Europe in a 10-year perspective,” Döllner told a group of reporters in August, including US News & World Report managing editor Alex Kwanten.

“And we see it totally differently in North America, especially the U.S.; we see for the next 10, 15 years still a dominance in combustion engine and hybrid drivetrains, and we will have to reflect that in our global portfolio,” Döllner added.

While waiting for the US market to come around to full battery EVs, Audi has been leaning on plug-in hybrids as a workaround. “We will take first steps to bring plug-in hybrids to the US,” Döllner affirmed to US News & World Report in July.

“I can definitely say that the RS model that will come to the market will be a plug-in hybrid one, and we are evaluating which other segments also could make sense in bringing that to the U.S. market,” he added.

According to reports, the new Audi RS 5, 630-horsepower high-performance plug-in will be the first to arrive, sometime later this year or early next year. For the performance class, drivers can expect a battery range of 50 miles. Regular hybrids will clock a range of 70-80 miles.

Automotive News also added some details in July. “Audi of America President Vito Paladino confirmed the German luxury brand will offer hybrids beyond the upcoming RS 5 sedan and Nuvolari supercar, which feature different plug-in hybrid systems,” Automotive New summarized in a Facebook post.

“We will have hybrids in our lineup,” Paladino told Automotive News, though reporter Jack Walsworth noted that Paladino was rather vague on the details.

Another detail left dangling is how much, if any, of Audi’s plug-in hybrid lineup for the US will be manufactured at the new US factory. That’s a good question. Maybe, maybe not. Grading of the site scheduled for 2027 and construction will begin the following year. With full production not expected until 2031, the timeline provides Audi with some wiggle room to adjust its electrification strategy for the US market.

The office of outgoing Georgia Governor Brian Kemp (he’s term-limited) issued a celebratory press release on October 24 that also suggests Audi may adjust its plans as the market evolves, noting that the new factory “will be designed around Audi’s future manufacturing requirements, with a focus on efficiency, flexibility, and the ability to respond to evolving customer needs and market demand.”

“In choosing Georgia, Audi cited speed-to-market, workforce, access to consumer markets, and proximity to an established automotive supply chain,” the Governor’s office further explained, while also highlighting the 4,500 new jobs Audi is expected to bring to the state.

Left unmentioned in the October 24 press release was Georgia’s vanguard position in the domestic EV manufacturing field. That’s a funny position for a politically conservative state to be in, but Governor Kemp has been among the rare Republican office holders to support the vehicle electrification movement.

If the Audi factory does churn out hybrid EVs in Georgia, it will be in good company. “Since 2018, 35 EV-related projects have contributed $23 billion in investments in Georgia,” the Georgia Department of Economic Development reported back in 2023.

And then there’s EV startup Rivian. In November of 2024, Rivian scored a $6.5 billion loan from the Biden administration to build a new factory in Georgia. Somehow the automaker managed to keep much of the funding after Trump took office and began waging war against EVs. A groundbreaking ceremony took place in September of 2025, and if all goes according to plan, Rivian’s electric cars will roll off the assembly line in 2028.

As for Trump, EVs were probably not top of mind when he stumped for Republican candidates at a rally in Perry,  Georgia on Saturday. Somehow he convinced Döllner to step on stage with him to announce the new factory.

Döllner’s appearance with Trump was met with a storm of criticism from both sides of the pond, including from Christiane Benner, who heads the leading German trade union IG Metall and also sits on the supervisory board of Volkswagen as its deputy chair.

“Christiane Benner…called Döllner’s US appearance ‘extremely embarrassing, politically unacceptable and unworthy of the company, but above all of Audi’s employees,” The Guardian reported on October 26.

“She accused Döllner of being in ‘flagrant breach’ of the company’s code of conduct, ‘which expressly requires political neutrality in the run-up to elections,’” The Guardian added.

The Guardian further noted that Benner threw down the gauntlet, advising that the supervisory board is supposed to be involved in major decisions like the Georgia factory.

Oopsies! Maybe that new factory won’t get built after all. Adding insult to injury, The Guardian notes that Volkswagen “aims to slash tens of thousands of positions and close plants in Germany.”

First spotted by CarMoses, the patent was filed last year and published last week. Titled “Extended Expansion Engine With Variable Compression Ratio for Longitudinal Mounting Applications,” it describes a mechanical arrangement designed to change piston movement and vary the engine’s compression ratio.

In a conventional engine, pistons move up and down inside cylinders, with connecting rods transferring that motion to the crankshaft. GM’s proposed design, which is an inline-six according to the sketches, adds a more elaborate arrangement of gears, links, and control components to change the relationship between these moving parts.

#5391. Ford’s new sub-$30K electric truck can power your home for a lot less

Ford wants to make powering your home with an EV a lot cheaper. Its upcoming sub-$30,000 Fathom electric truck will feature a new bidirectional charging system that ditches the need for an expensive external inverter.

Arriving in 2027, the Fathom will be the first EV from a US automaker priced below $30,000 that can deliver more than 10 kW of AC power to a home without a separate wall-mounted inverter.

And Ford says it’s cutting the cost of the other equipment needed for home backup power, too.

EV batteries store electricity as direct current (DC), but our homes use alternating current (AC). To send power from an EV battery into a house, traditionally, that meant installing a pricey external inverter.

Ford has come up with a different approach for its new Universal EV Platform, which will underpin the Fathom.

After acquiring startup Auto Motive Power in 2023, Ford redesigned its bidirectional charging system from the ground up. Instead of relying on a separate wall-mounted inverter, it built one directly into the truck’s E-box, one of the vehicle’s five main modules.

Fewer parts mean lower costs, and Ford says the savings will extend to the home charging equipment, too.

Fathom owners will have two ways to use the truck’s battery to power their homes.

Home Power Management will let owners charge their truck when electricity rates are low, then use that stored power at home when rates are higher. That could mean real savings for households on time-of-use electricity plans. They’ll need Ford’s new Home Power Station, which the company expects to cost about half as much as its first-generation bidirectional charger.

Home Backup Power will let the truck keep the lights on during an outage. For that, owners will need both the Home Power Station and Ford’s Home Backup Panel. Ford says the backup hardware should cost at least 60% less than its first-generation equipment.

Ford hasn’t released prices for either piece of hardware, so we don’t yet know what a complete home installation will cost.

But pairing a sub-$30,000 electric truck with substantially cheaper home power equipment could make bidirectional charging a lot more appealing.

Making the hardware cheaper is one thing. Making sure utilities can handle thousands of EVs that send electricity back into homes and potentially the grid is another.

Ford formed a working group in July with American Electric Power, BC Hydro, Consumers Energy, Exelon, and Pacific Gas and Electric. They’re working on making it easier for customers to connect bidirectional EVs before the Fathom arrives next year.

For example, someone using their truck solely for home backup power during an outage shouldn’t have to go through the same approval process as someone sending electricity to the grid.

The group is also looking at how to update utility procedures to accommodate newer bidirectional charging standards and make it easier for EV owners to participate in virtual power plants (VPPs). In other words, the batteries in parked EVs could become a useful source of electricity when the grid needs it most.

Ford says the Fathom’s system is designed to meet applicable UL 1741 requirements and IEEE 1547 interconnection standards. It also uses SAE J3068 communications technology, which Ford says helps reduce charger costs.

The idea is to get the technical requirements and utility processes sorted out before affordable bidirectional EVs start arriving in much larger numbers.

Affordable EVs already offer some form of bidirectional power. Two of the cheapest electric cars in the US, the Chevy Bolt and Nissan Leaf, are both available for under $30,000.

The Bolt supports vehicle-to-home (V2H) power through GM Energy equipment, while the Leaf offers vehicle-to-load (V2L) capability through an external device plugged into its charging port.

Ford’s system takes things much further. It can deliver more than 10 kW of AC power to a home without an expensive external inverter, and Ford says the rest of the equipment will cost considerably less, too.

That’s a pretty compelling proposition, especially for people who live in areas prone to power outages or have electricity plans with higher peak-hour rates.

When power outages last a while the area still affected shrinks. While a whole county may be out day one, by day 3 it may just be your neighborhood left. At that point you could drive across town and recharge the car and be good to power your house for another few days.

There are still some unknowns. Ford hasn’t announced the final hardware prices, and installation costs will vary. Plus, utility rules need to catch up if EV owners are going to take full advantage of bidirectional charging.

But this seems to be going in the right direction. If automakers can bring costs down and the industry can agree on consistent standards, having an EV that doubles as a home battery could eventually become a normal part of EV ownership rather than an expensive bonus feature.

#5364. Scientists discover how deep sleep builds muscle, burns fat, and boosts the brain

A good night's sleep does much more than leave you feeling refreshed. During deep sleep, the body releases growth hormone, a powerful chemical messenger that helps build muscle, strengthen bones, and regulate fat metabolism. For teenagers, getting enough sleep is especially important because growth hormone plays a major role in reaching their full height potential.

Scientists have long known that insufficient sleep can interfere with growth hormone production. What remained unclear was exactly how the brain controls this process, particularly during non-REM sleep, the deep, restorative stage that typically dominates the earlier part of the night.

Now, researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, have uncovered the brain circuitry behind this relationship. In a study published in the journal Cell, the team identified the neurons that regulate growth hormone release during sleep and discovered a previously unknown feedback system that helps maintain a delicate balance between hormone levels and wakefulness.

The discovery reveals that the relationship works in both directions. Sleep stimulates growth hormone production, but growth hormone also sends signals back to the brain that influence when we wake up.

The findings could eventually help scientists develop new approaches to treating sleep disorders associated with metabolic diseases such as diabetes, as well as neurological conditions including Parkinson's and Alzheimer's.

"People know that growth hormone release is tightly related to sleep, but only through drawing blood and checking growth hormone levels during sleep," said study first author Xinlu Ding, a postdoctoral fellow in UC Berkeley's Department of Neuroscience and the Helen Wills Neuroscience Institute. "We're actually directly recording neural activity in mice to see what's going on. We are providing a basic circuit to work on in the future to develop different treatments."

Why Deep Sleep Matters for Growth and Metabolism

Growth hormone is best known for its role in childhood development, but its importance extends well into adulthood. It supports the maintenance of muscle and bone, helps the body regulate how it uses stored fat, and influences blood sugar levels.

When sleep is consistently disrupted, growth hormone regulation can suffer. Because this hormone helps control glucose and fat metabolism, inadequate sleep may contribute to an increased risk of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.

To understand how the brain coordinates these processes, the Berkeley researchers focused on a region called the hypothalamus. Located deep within the brain, this small but essential control center helps regulate hunger, body temperature, hormone production, and the daily cycle of sleeping and waking. Its basic functions have been preserved throughout mammalian evolution.

Within the hypothalamus, the researchers examined neurons that produce growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) and two distinct types of neurons that produce somatostatin.

These chemical messengers have opposing functions. GHRH encourages growth hormone secretion, while somatostatin suppresses it. Together, they help control how much growth hormone enters the bloodstream, largely by regulating its release from the pituitary gland, a small hormone-producing structure at the base of the brain.

The scientists also investigated how growth hormone influences the locus coeruleus, a region in the brainstem involved in alertness, attention, thinking, and the drive to explore new or unfamiliar experiences.

Disruptions in the activity of this region have been linked to several psychiatric and neurological disorders.

"Understanding the neural circuit for growth hormone release could eventually point toward new hormonal therapies to improve sleep quality or restore normal growth hormone balance," said Daniel Silverman, a UC Berkeley postdoctoral fellow and study co-author. "There are some experimental gene therapies where you target a specific cell type. This circuit could be a novel handle to try to dial back the excitability of the locus coeruleus, which hasn't been talked about before."

Scientists Map the Brain Circuit That Controls Growth Hormone

The research was conducted in the laboratory of Yang Dan, a professor of neuroscience and of molecular and cell biology at UC Berkeley.

To investigate how the circuitry works, the team studied mice using electrodes implanted in their brains. The scientists recorded neural activity while using light to stimulate specific neurons in the hypothalamus, allowing them to observe how different parts of the system responded.

Mice are particularly useful for studying sleep regulation because their sleep patterns differ significantly from those of humans. Rather than sleeping through one long stretch each night, mice typically sleep in brief episodes lasting only several minutes, scattered throughout the day and night.

This gave the researchers repeated opportunities to examine how neural activity and growth hormone regulation changed as the animals moved between sleeping and waking.

Using advanced techniques to trace connections between brain cells, the team discovered that GHRH and somatostatin behave differently depending on the stage of sleep.

During rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, the stage commonly associated with vivid dreaming, activity involving both GHRH and somatostatin increases. Although these hormones normally exert opposing effects, their coordinated activity during this stage contributes to increased growth hormone release.

During non-REM sleep, the pattern changes. Somatostatin activity decreases, reducing its inhibitory influence, while GHRH activity rises moderately. This combination also promotes growth hormone secretion.

The discovery helps explain how different sleep stages can support the release of the same essential hormone through distinct patterns of brain activity.

Growth Hormone Also Helps Control When We Wake Up

Perhaps the most surprising finding was that growth hormone does not simply respond to signals from the sleeping brain. It also influences the brain circuits that control wakefulness.

As growth hormone gradually builds up during sleep, it stimulates neurons in the locus coeruleus, increasing their activity and helping promote the transition toward waking.

This creates a feedback loop in which sleep encourages growth hormone release, and the resulting hormone activity helps regulate the sleep-wake cycle.

The researchers also identified an unexpected complication. Although greater activity in the locus coeruleus generally promotes alertness, excessive stimulation can have the opposite effect.

In an earlier study published the same year, Silverman demonstrated that when neurons in this region become overexcited, they can paradoxically promote sleepiness rather than wakefulness.

Together, these findings reveal a carefully regulated system in which the brain must balance competing signals to maintain healthy sleep and hormone activity.

Scientists describe this type of self-regulating process as homeostasis, the body's ability to maintain stable internal conditions even as its environment and physiological demands change.

"This suggests that sleep and growth hormone form a tightly balanced system: Too little sleep reduces growth hormone release, and too much growth hormone can in turn push the brain toward wakefulness," Silverman said. "Sleep drives growth hormone release, and growth hormone feeds back to regulate wakefulness, and this balance is essential for growth, repair and metabolic health."

A Surprising Connection Between Sleep and Brain Health

The newly identified feedback system may have implications that extend beyond physical growth and metabolism.

Because the locus coeruleus helps regulate attention, alertness, and cognitive function, growth hormone's influence on this region suggests it could also play a role in how effectively the brain functions after waking.

This raises the possibility that maintaining healthy sleep and growth hormone rhythms may support both physical recovery and mental performance.

"Growth hormone not only helps you build your muscle and bones and reduce your fat tissue, but may also have cognitive benefits, promoting your overall arousal level when you wake up," Ding said.

By identifying the specific brain cells and signaling pathways involved, the researchers have provided a foundation for exploring therapies that target disrupted sleep and hormone regulation.

Such approaches could eventually prove useful for people with sleep disturbances related to metabolic disorders or neurological diseases. However, the experiments were conducted in mice, and additional research will be needed to determine how these mechanisms operate in humans and whether they can be safely targeted with treatments.

The findings underscore a fundamental principle of sleep biology: Rest is not simply a period when the brain switches off. It is an active, carefully coordinated process in which hormones and neural circuits constantly communicate to support growth, repair, metabolism, and wakefulness.

#5355. Scientists discover that male and female skin play by different rules

Female mice heal skin wounds faster than males, and scientists have uncovered a surprising biological difference that may help explain why. By watching skin stem cells in living mice for five months, researchers found that female cells work together in a more coordinated way, while male cells tend to behave more independently. These differences are regulated by sex hormones and could eventually help scientists develop more personalized treatments for wound healing and skin diseases.

The discovery comes from researchers at the Université libre de Bruxelles (ULB), working with scientists at the University of Cambridge. Their findings suggest that biological sex influences how skin renews and repairs itself, even though the skin is not part of the reproductive system.

Historically, much of the research into biological differences between males and females has focused on reproductive organs. However, sex can also influence other tissues and organs throughout the body. Understanding these differences could be important for improving the prevention and treatment of inflammatory diseases and cancer, yet researchers have often overlooked their significance.

Published in Nature, the study was led by Prof. Cédric Blanpain, MD/PhD, an investigator at the WEL Research Institute, director of the Laboratory of Stem Cells and Cancer, and professor at the Université libre de Bruxelles. The research was conducted in collaboration with Prof. Benjamin Simons and his team at the University of Cambridge, along with scientists from several other institutions.

Using an advanced microscopy technique, the researchers were able to record individual cells through the skin of living mice. This allowed them to follow the same stem cells and their descendants for five months, an unprecedented period of observation. Although their original goal was to understand the mechanisms behind skin renewal, the team unexpectedly discovered striking differences between females and males. Further experiments revealed that sex hormones were responsible.

The skin's outermost layer is constantly replacing itself. This process begins with stem cells located at the bottom of the layer, where they divide to produce new cells. Some of the resulting "daughter" cells retain their ability to function as stem cells, while others begin developing into specialized cells.

These specialized cells form the protective barrier that shields the body from its surroundings. As they mature, they lose the ability to divide (a process called differentiation), gradually move toward the surface, and are eventually shed.

Scientists have debated the precise organization of this renewal process for decades. By repeatedly observing the same areas of skin over five months, the researchers found that stem cells do not immediately become fully mature skin cells.

Instead, they pass through an intermediate stage in which they can divide only a limited number of times before completing their development into specialized cells. This finding helps clarify a long-standing question about how the skin maintains itself.

Female Skin Cells Work in Surprising Synchronization

One of the most unexpected findings involved the behavior of newly formed cells.

In female mice, two "sister" cells produced by the division of a single parent cell frequently make the same developmental decision at the same time. Both may divide again, or both may begin specializing into mature skin cells.

Their behavior resembles two rowers moving their oars in unison. In male mice, however, sister cells are less closely synchronized and tend to follow more independent patterns.

"We initially set out to understand how the skin renews itself, and whether there is a hierarchy with stem cells at the top. We were surprised to discover strong differences in the way stem cells renew the skin in females and males," explains Doctor Dan Engelman, the first author of the study.

Sex Hormones Control How Skin Cells Behave

To determine what caused these differences, the scientists investigated the influence of male and female sex hormones.

They first examined what happened when male mice were castrated, removing the primary source of testosterone. Without this hormone, their skin cells began behaving more like those in females. When the researchers administered dihydrotestosterone (DHT), an active form of testosterone, the cells returned to their typical male pattern.

These experiments showed that male sex hormones help regulate how rapidly skin cells mature and move out of the stem cell layer.

The team also investigated the role of female sex hormones. Removing the ovaries (ovariectomy) reduced the rate at which stem cells multiplied and almost completely eliminated the characteristic synchronization between sister cells.

Treatment with estradiol, a form of estrogen, reversed the reduction in stem cell proliferation.

Together, these findings revealed that testosterone, which is generally more abundant in males, influences the speed of skin cell maturation. Estrogens, which are generally more abundant in females, promote stem cell proliferation and help coordinate the behavior of newly produced cells.

The researchers also tested testosterone on human skin reconstructed in the laboratory. They found that the hormone accelerated stem cell differentiation, suggesting that at least part of the mechanism observed in mice may also operate in humans.

Why Skin Wounds Heal Faster in Female Mice

The differences in stem cell behavior were accompanied by another important finding: female mice recovered more quickly from skin injuries.

When researchers examined wounds on the animals' backs, they found that wounds closed substantially faster in females, particularly during the first few days after injury.

This early advantage was partly associated with skin stem cells becoming active sooner and working in a more coordinated manner. As healing progressed, the difference between males and females gradually became smaller.

The results suggest that the hormonal regulation of stem cells may help explain why skin repair differs between the sexes.

What the Discovery Could Mean for Wound Healing and Skin Cancer

Doctors have long recognized differences between men and women in wound healing, the severity of certain inflammatory conditions, and the frequency of some skin cancers. However, the underlying biological explanations have remained incomplete.

By identifying how sex hormones influence stem cell activity, the new research provides a possible explanation for some of these observations.

The findings could eventually contribute to medical treatments that account for a patient's sex. Potential applications include improving recovery from burns and surgery, as well as treating chronic wounds in people with diabetes, a problem affecting millions worldwide.

Understanding these differences may also help researchers develop new approaches to preventing skin cancer, one of the most common forms of cancer.

For now, faster wound healing and coordinated stem cell behavior were demonstrated in mice. Additional research will be needed to determine how closely these processes match those in human skin and whether they can be used to improve medical care.

Beyond their potential clinical importance, the findings also provide new insight into the fundamental process of skin renewal, helping address a question that has challenged scientists for decades.

"This study is a technological tour de force, and the discovery of differences between male and female skin stem cells is fascinating and entirely unexpected. It sheds light on a series of clinical observations that previously lacked a biological explanation. It opens up a wide range of new questions, spanning from fundamental stem cell biology to clinical issues such as wound healing, cancer development, and autoimmune diseases. Sex is a parameter too often overlooked in biology and medicine. We will now investigate the differences between males and females during the formation and progression of skin cancers, as well as the molecular mechanisms responsible for these differences," says Prof. Cédric Blanpain, who led the study.

The project brought together Prof. Blanpain's team (ULB, Belgium) and Prof. Simons' group (University of Cambridge, UK), along with research teams led by Michele De Luca (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia, Italia), Yulia Shwartz (Broad CIRM Center, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, US) and Esther Hoste (VIB-Ghent University, Belgium).

#5347. Scientists uncover 3 key brain pathways linked to lasting depression relief

Scientists at Mount Sinai have identified three important brain connections associated with long-term improvement in patients receiving deep brain stimulation (DBS) for severe depression that has not responded to conventional treatments. The discovery could help researchers make this experimental therapy more precise and effective.

Published October 1 in the American Journal of Psychiatry, the research examined the largest group of patients assembled across multiple independent cohorts to date who had received subcallosal cingulate deep brain stimulation (SCC DBS) for treatment-resistant depression.

The researchers found that lasting improvements were consistently associated with electrical stimulation reaching three specific white matter pathways: the cingulum bundle, forceps minor, and medial uncinate fasciculus/ventral amygdalofugal pathways.

These findings strengthen the case for using detailed maps of the brain's connections to determine exactly where electrodes should be implanted, rather than choosing their positions based primarily on anatomical landmarks. Known as connectome-based targeting, this strategy has already helped shape the ongoing Abbott TRANSCEND clinical trial, a randomized, controlled study that uses individualized brain connectivity maps to guide SCC DBS treatment.

"This study underscores the importance of individualized surgical targeting for achieving optimal outcomes with deep brain stimulation for depression," said Helen Mayberg, MD, Mount Sinai Professor in Neurotherapeutics and founding Director of the Nash Family Center for Advanced Circuit Therapeutics at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. "In any randomized controlled trial of a neuromodulation treatment, precise lead placement and confirmation that the intended brain target has been engaged are essential first steps. We still have much to learn about which patients are most likely to benefit from stimulation of this particular target, but developing imaging-based signatures that can match patients to their optimal treatment remains a central goal of our research."

How Deep Brain Stimulation Targets Severe Depression

For people with treatment-resistant depression, standard therapies have failed to provide adequate relief. Researchers are investigating deep brain stimulation as a possible treatment for individuals living with severe, persistent symptoms.

The procedure involves surgically placing electrodes inside the brain to deliver controlled electrical signals to circuits involved in depression. However, positioning the electrodes correctly remains one of the greatest challenges.

Electrical stimulation does not simply affect the brain tissue closest to an implanted electrode. It can also influence larger networks through the white matter pathways that connect different brain regions. As a result, two patients with electrodes implanted in nearly identical locations may experience very different outcomes depending on which connections are actually stimulated.

Earlier research from Mount Sinai and Emory University helped establish the importance of these networks. In studies published between 2014 and 2023 in Biological Psychiatry, Molecular Psychiatry, and Nature, the teams identified white matter pathways associated with successful SCC DBS treatment and developed a method for planning electrode placement using brain connectivity maps.

The latest investigation sought to determine whether those connections were also important in patients treated at other research centers. Scientists also wanted to understand whether differences in the pathways reached by stimulation could help account for patients who did not benefit.

Three Brain Connections Linked to Lasting Improvement

To investigate, the researchers examined information from several clinical studies, including the BROADEN trial and a patient cohort at the University of Toronto, where SCC DBS was originally developed.

They used computer models of the electrical fields generated by implanted electrodes alongside maps of brain connectivity. This allowed them to estimate which pathways received stimulation and compare those patterns across different patient groups.

The results showed a consistent relationship between sustained improvement and stimulation of the cingulum bundle, forceps minor, and medial uncinate fasciculus/ventral amygdalofugal pathways.

One finding was especially notable. Across the study sites, patients who had not responded to treatment after two years showed a lack of stimulation engagement with the left cingulum bundle.

The researchers did not investigate whether moving electrodes to reach this pathway would improve those patients' symptoms. Nevertheless, the pattern offers a possible explanation for why apparently similar electrode placements can produce different results. An electrode might be positioned in the correct general region but still fail to activate all the connections associated with lasting improvement.

"This study addresses an important question in DBS: not simply which area of the brain to stimulate, but which connections within that area need to be engaged," said Brian Kopell, MD, Director of the Center for Neuromodulation at Mount Sinai. "For a network disorder such as depression, millimeters can determine whether stimulation reaches the pathways we intend to modulate. Identifying and reproducibly engaging those pathways gives us a more precise surgical target and a framework for understanding why otherwise similar electrode placements may produce different clinical outcomes."

Toward More Personalized Depression Treatments

Although reaching these three pathways appears important, the researchers emphasize that electrode placement alone is unlikely to determine whether a patient improves.

The investigation concentrated on identifying the brain connections that should receive stimulation. Additional studies are needed to determine which individuals are most likely to benefit from SCC DBS and how electrical stimulation should be adjusted as treatment continues.

There were also limitations to the brain imaging data. Individual diffusion MRI scans, which can provide information about a person's white matter connections, were not available for every participant. Consequently, much of the analysis used generalized maps of brain connectivity rather than maps created specifically for each patient.

For the Emory patient group, the researchers were able to compare results obtained from generalized connectivity maps with those based on individual diffusion imaging. Both approaches produced consistent findings, providing further support for the importance of the identified pathways. However, more research is needed to establish the value of patient-specific imaging.

Future investigations will explore whether additional brain imaging measurements, including white matter integrity and functional connectivity, can help identify suitable patients, improve electrode placement, and guide treatment settings.

"Our findings show precisely engaging these white matter pathways is a critical first step toward sustained response," said Ki Sueng Choi, PhD, Associate Professor, Imaging Core Leader at the Center for Advanced Circuit Therapeutics at Mount Sinai, and corresponding author of the study. "The next step is to characterize abnormal functional and structural brain states along these pathways to build more detailed models for setting patient-specific stimulation parameters, including amplitude, frequency, and pulse width, for precision medicine."

By combining increasingly detailed brain maps with personalized stimulation settings, scientists hope to better understand why some people experience lasting relief while others do not. The findings represent another step toward developing more targeted treatments for severe depression, although further clinical research is necessary to establish how much these approaches can improve outcomes.

#5329. Scientists discover a natural compound that could protect the brain from too much salt

Consuming too much salt can affect more than blood pressure. Growing evidence suggests that excessive salt intake may also interfere with memory, thinking, and emotional well-being. High salt (HS) consumption is already a major public health concern because of its connection to hypertension, but its potential effects on the brain make finding ways to reduce the damage even more important.

Researchers in Japan have identified a promising possibility in LPC70, a compound derived from soy. Previous research found that this ingredient could reduce high blood pressure and cognitive problems associated with a high-salt diet (HSD) in mice. The latest study takes a closer look at how it works, revealing that the compound influences important chemical signals in the brain and kidneys.

How a Soy Compound May Counter the Effects of Excess Salt

The research centers on prostaglandins (PGs), signaling molecules made from a fatty acid called arachidonic acid (AA). These substances act much like local hormones, helping regulate essential functions throughout the body.

In the cardiovascular system, prostaglandins help control blood pressure by balancing the widening and narrowing of blood vessels. Their effects in the brain are more complicated. Depending on which receptors they activate, they can trigger signals that either protect nerve cells or contribute to harmful processes, potentially affecting memory, thinking, and emotional responses.

LPC70 is a form of soy lysolecithin in which lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) accounts for more than 70% of the phospholipids. Although earlier experiments showed that LPC70 could ease salt-related hypertension and cognitive difficulties, scientists had not fully understood the mechanisms responsible, particularly its influence on prostaglandin signaling.

To investigate, a research team led by Professor Akihiro Mouri and Dr. Hisayoshi Kubota, working with Dr. Kazuhiro Kagotani, examined how LPC70 affected these signaling pathways in mice consuming a high-salt diet.

Dr. Kubota, the study's first author, played a central role in the experiments. He assessed blood pressure, monitored behavioral changes, and analyzed molecular activity associated with prostaglandins. His work helped clarify how LPC70 produces different effects in the organs involved in salt-related cardiovascular and cognitive problems.

Prof. Mouri and Dr. Kubota are affiliated with the Department of Regulatory Science for Evaluation and Development of Pharmaceuticals & Devices at Fujita Health University Graduate School of Medical Science, as well as the International Center for Brain Science (ICBS) at Fujita Health University in Japan. Dr. Kagotani is affiliated with Tsuji Oil Mills Co., Ltd, in Japan and the Tsuji Health and Beauty Science Laboratory at Mie University, Japan.

The findings were released online on May 14, 2026, and published in Volume 197 of Neurochemistry International on July 1, 2026.

"We wanted to understand the role of LPC70 in coordinating changes in PG signaling across organ systems," mentioned Prof. Mouri. "Our study originated from a collaborative research project with Tsuji Oil Mills Co., Ltd, a company that develops naturally derived food ingredients," he added.

Soy Compound Improves Blood Pressure and Memory in Mice

As expected, mice that consumed a high-salt diet developed elevated blood pressure. However, the consequences extended beyond their cardiovascular health.

The animals also became less socially interactive and had difficulty recognizing objects they had encountered before, indicating problems with memory. Interestingly, their general movement and anxiety-like behavior showed no substantial changes. This pattern suggests that excessive salt selectively disrupted more complex cognitive and social functions rather than causing widespread behavioral impairment.

When researchers supplemented the animals' diets with LPC70, several of these effects were reduced. Blood pressure improved, and the mice performed better on tests of social interaction and object recognition memory.

These improvements prompted the researchers to examine what was happening inside the animals' kidneys and brains.

Different Protective Effects in the Kidneys and Brain

In the kidneys, a high-salt diet increased the expression of two components involved in prostaglandin signaling: cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and the PGE2 receptor EP3. Both may contribute to the development of high blood pressure associated with excessive salt consumption.

LPC70 reduced these salt-related increases, suggesting that the compound helps correct changes in kidney signaling that can interfere with normal blood pressure regulation.

The researchers found a different response in the prefrontal cortex, a brain region essential for thinking, memory, and social behavior.

Excess salt reduced the expression of the PGD2 receptor DP1, which plays a role in protective and anti-inflammatory signaling in the brain. LPC70 restored expression of this receptor without changing DP2, another receptor associated with potentially harmful effects on nerve cells.

This distinction is important because it suggests that LPC70 does not simply increase or suppress all prostaglandin activity. Instead, it may selectively favor signaling pathways that help protect the brain, potentially explaining the improvements in memory and social behavior observed in the mice.

A Surprising Change in Chemical Signaling

The team also discovered an unexpected change involving arachidonic acid, the fatty acid the body uses to produce prostaglandins.

Mice consuming excess salt had lower levels of circulating arachidonic acid. Yet when they received LPC70, levels of prostaglandins derived from this fatty acid, including PGE2 and PGD2, increased.

This seemingly contradictory result suggests that LPC70 may help the body use available arachidonic acid more efficiently to produce prostaglandins under high-salt conditions.

Taken together, the findings reveal a coordinated but highly selective response. In the kidneys, LPC70 appears to suppress signaling changes associated with hypertension. In the brain, it restores signaling linked to protection against inflammation and damage.

The compound's effects therefore depend on both the organ involved and the specific prostaglandin receptors being regulated.

Could Soy-Based Ingredients Help Protect Against Excess Salt?

The results suggest that LPC70 could eventually become a useful functional food ingredient for reducing some of the health consequences of excessive salt intake.

Its ability to improve blood pressure and cognitive performance in mice raises the possibility that it could support cardiovascular and brain health, particularly among people whose diets contain high amounts of salt.

However, the findings come from animal experiments, and further clinical studies are needed to determine whether the same benefits occur in humans.

"Our study elucidates LPC70's role in PG signaling, laying the groundwork for future clinical research and dietary interventions aimed at reducing hypertension-related cognitive decline and promoting healthy aging globally," concluded Prof. Mouri.

The research provides a clearer picture of how a soy-derived compound can influence the body's response to excessive salt, opening new possibilities for nutritional approaches to protecting long-term health.

#5322. Magic mushroom ingredient takes the fight out of aggressive fish

Psilocybin, the psychedelic substance found in more than 200 species of mushrooms, appears to have an unexpected effect on aggressive fish. Most mushrooms containing this compound belong to the gilled mushroom genus Psilocybe. In mammals, psilocybin interacts with serotonin receptors in the brain, influencing emotions and behaviors such as mood, appetite, and aggression. However, scientists still know relatively little about how the substance affects social interactions among animals.

To investigate this question, researchers in Canada examined the effects of psilocybin on the amphibious mangrove rivulus fish (Kryptolebias marmoratus). Their findings, published in Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience, suggest that even a brief exposure to a low dose can significantly reduce aggressive behavior without disrupting normal social interactions.

"We show that an acute, low dose of psilocybin significantly reduces activity and aggressive attack behavior during social interactions in adult mangrove rivulus fish, a species that is naturally highly aggressive," said first author Dayna Forsyth, a research associate and former MSc student at Acadia University in Nova Scotia.

"These findings provide the first evidence that psilocybin can selectively reduce escalated aggression in a vertebrate model without suppressing social interaction," added senior author Dr. Suzie Currie, a biologist at The University of British Columbia.

Why Scientists Studied Aggressive Mangrove Rivulus Fish

Mangrove rivulus fish are naturally combative, particularly when they encounter another member of their species. Their aggressive responses are easy to recognize and measure, allowing researchers to detect even small changes in behavior.

These fish also have an unusual reproductive ability. They can fertilize themselves, producing offspring that are genetically identical. This makes them especially useful for experiments because scientists can minimize genetic variation and more confidently attribute behavioral differences to psilocybin rather than inherited traits.

For the experiment, the researchers worked with three genetically distinct lines of fish bred in the laboratory. One group received psilocybin, another provided the fish used to provoke social interactions, and the third helped researchers measure how much psilocybin entered and accumulated in the animals' bodies.

The researchers first established a baseline for each fish's behavior. They placed a fish into a tank already occupied by another fish, with the two separated by a fiberglass mesh barrier and an opaque cover. The mesh prevented physical contact while allowing the fish to see and smell each other once the cover was removed.

After allowing five minutes for the fish to adjust to their shared environment, the team removed the opaque cover and recorded their interactions.

The following day, 24 hours after the initial observation, the researchers placed the same fish in water containing dissolved psilocybin. Following 20 minutes of exposure, they returned it to the tank with the same fish it had encountered previously. The opaque cover was then removed again so the researchers could observe any changes in behavior.

Magic Mushroom Compound Reduces Aggressive Attacks

The results revealed a striking change. Fish exposed to psilocybin moved around less and displayed fewer bursts of aggressive swimming than fish that had not received the substance.

To assess these changes, the scientists measured overall activity (time spent moving) and several signs of aggression (including swimming bursts). These rapid bursts are particularly important because they indicate that an encounter is escalating toward an attack, even when the fish cannot make physical contact.

"Swimming bursts are high-energy attack behaviors that represent an escalation of aggression towards the stimulus fish without making physical contact," explained Currie. "Other types of aggressive behaviors, like head-on displays, are more about communication and social assessment and require very little energy."

Interestingly, psilocybin did not suppress every form of aggressive or social behavior. While the fish became less likely to engage in intense, energy-demanding attacks, their less physically demanding social displays remained largely unchanged.

"Psilocybin's calming effect appears to selectively reduce energetically costly, escalated behaviors while lower-energy social display behaviors remained largely unchanged," said Forsyth. "This suggests that this compound can selectively dampen escalated social conflict rather than shutting down behavior altogether."

The compound also reduced general activity. When placed alongside another member of their species, fish treated with psilocybin spent less time swimming than untreated controls.

Together, these findings suggest that psilocybin may influence specific aspects of aggressive behavior rather than simply making animals less responsive to their surroundings.

What the Findings Could Mean for Psychedelic Research

The researchers believe animal studies like this one could eventually help scientists better understand how psychedelic compounds influence behavior and identify possible directions for therapeutic research.

By examining which social responses are most sensitive to psilocybin, researchers may be able to uncover biological mechanisms that are difficult to investigate directly in people. Such insights could ultimately inform studies of potential clinical applications.

However, the team emphasized that the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a treatment for aggression in humans. The experiment involved fish, did not evaluate any clinical intervention, and cannot establish whether similar effects would occur in people.

There are also important questions about how long the calming effect lasts. The researchers examined only single doses and short exposure periods. They did not investigate repeated treatments, long-term behavioral changes, or whether the fish might adapt to the substance over time.

Additional experiments will be necessary to determine whether psilocybin's ability to reduce aggressive attacks can persist beyond the brief period examined in this study.

"Future studies can build on this work to explore how psilocybin alters neural signaling, which serotonin pathways are involved, and why some aspects of social behavior are affected while others are not," concluded Currie. "These are questions that are difficult or impossible to answer directly in humans."
