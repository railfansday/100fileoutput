Tollulah Oni, right, running with teammates during an event at the International Conference on Sustainable Urban Mobility in Lagos, Nigeria, in 2025.Credit: LAMATA/The 2025 International Conference on Sustainable Urban Mobility Run

In 2016, public-health physician Tolullah Oni was jogging through the streets of Dakar when she saw someone doing push-ups in the middle of a busy roundabout. Cars were puffing fumes around him as the Sun baked down. As a physician, she knew that this had health implications.

More than half of the global population now lives in urban areas, but many cities are not conducive to human health and well-being. Oni, who is a researcher at University of Cambridge, UK, describes herself as an urban epidemiologist whose patients are cities. She works with colleagues around the world to diagnose and treat ‘sick’ cities with the goal of improving the health and quality of life of their inhabitants. This involves monitoring several variables, such as air quality and temperature, and their impact on the city’s residents. Those data can inform advocacy and interventions.

Frustrated by a lack of infrastructure on the ground in such cities, and by the struggle to connect researchers and residents, she set up the non-profit organization UrbanBetter in 2019 to support her research and advocacy. By providing training on data collection and analysis, the organization helps citizens and local researchers to diagnose their own cities and to use that evidence to lobby for change. Its research arm, the UrbanBetter Academy, has a centre in Cambridge, and its civic arm, called Cityzens Hubs, operates in 11 cities in Africa, as well as in Bogotá and Bangkok.

Oni, who was born in Nigeria, identifies as African diaspora, which she classifies as a “sixth distinct region of the African continent”. Now based in the United Kingdom, she recognizes the friction that comes with trying to treat cities that she does not live in. Oni tells Nature how she wants to create lasting virtual infrastructure through UrbanBetter that channels the diaspora’s intellectual capital back into the continent and heals sick cities.

Cities can’t tell you that they’re sick, so you must bring the symptoms to light first. What are the lungs or air quality doing? What is happening in the green spaces? How is the city metabolizing energy? What is happening to its waste? If you don’t have the data, you don’t know whether the city is sick and in a lot of our cities, we’re not even measuring those things.

It is important that the people who are identifying the symptoms are on the ground in that city, rather than me, a professor, sitting thousands of kilometres away in Cambridge. People there are breathing the air and moving through the city. We have to equip local people with the skill set to diagnose a city. The diagnostic tests can be done by sensors and their data can be mapped.

In cities, we need treatments to be grounded in lived realities. Outsiders can assess a city and propose a solution, backed by evidence, but it might not work for many reasons. We need to include residents. They can identify priority areas and advocate for change. For example, in Nairobi, a group of Cityzens collected air-pollution data and convened their own community assembly in 2025 to identify potential solutions, such as cleaner public transport, adding trees and green spaces and reducing open burning of rubbish. These points fed directly into the government’s Nairobi City County Air Quality Action Plan.

Developing treatments for a city is a collective effort and accountability is spoon-feeding that medicine in the form of small, incremental changes so that the city can get better.

I set up UrbanBetter in 2019 as an experimental platform that would situate research differently by combining it with action and advocacy. We started with a US$300,000 unrestricted grant from the funding organization Unorthodox Philanthropy, which is sponsored by the Lampert Byrd Foundation based in San Francisco, California.

Each of UrbanBetter’s hubs has different strengths — Lagos works on physical activity, air pollution and heat, whereas Bogotá has done work on transport, cycling and car-free days. This means that when another city wants to work in a new area, they don’t have to reinvent the wheel.

Ahead of the 2025 Lagos City Marathon, the local Cityzens Hub partnered with World Athletics’ project Running for Clean Air. The marathon takes place in February, which is among the hottest times of year in Lagos, and the city has high levels of air pollution.

Throughout 2025, a group of residents ran the marathon route each month, starting in the January before the marathon. They wore air-quality sensors and collected geotagged data with the UrbanBetter app. Some runners found that there was a 60% reduction in air pollution on the actual marathon day because cars were barred from the roads. These findings have informed a shift in transportation-policy implementation in the city, which is now targeting net-zero emissions by 2050. Government initiatives include increased cycling and walking, an overhaul of public transport and increased air-quality monitoring.

The Cityzens Hub then identified heat as another major issue for city dwellers, so they reached out to the UrbanBetter Academy for help analysing complex data. In 2025, researchers from UrbanBetter, local partner institutions and the Lagos Transport Authority received a £2-million [roughly US$2.6-million] grant from the biomedical funding charity Wellcome to investigate heat exposure and health risks in Lagos in a project called Hotsteppers. This is how UrbanBetter works — that research question about heat came from people on the ground raising the issue.

#1116. Paramount and Warner Bros merge into one Hollywood giant

Paramount has completed an $81 billion purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery, as its owner Skydance creates a new entertainment behemoth. Billionaire David Ellison's hostile takeover bid defeated rival efforts from Netflix.

Paramount on Tuesday finalized its takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, completing a hostile acquisition that was months in the making and almost faltered at several hurdles.

The takeover cost a total of around $81 billion (roughly €72 billion), or almost $111 billion including the Warner Bros. debts passed on as part of the deal.

The merger brings together two of the oldest US filmmaking studios and will form a new giant called Skydance Corporation.

The new name Skydance hails from billionaire investor David Ellison, who first bought out Paramount and its subsidiaries and then moved on to target the much larger fish that is Warner Bros.

The bid faced stiff competition in a bidding war with Netflix, and later scrutiny from US regulators and politicians, but ultimately went through.

"Today is a historic day, not just for Skydance but for our entire industry," said Ellison, whose family has ties to US President Donald Trump.

Skydance shares began trading on the New York Stock Exchange on Tuesday under the ticker SKYD, while Warner Bros. Discovery shareholders will receive about $31 per share.

The deal closed less than a week after a federal judge in California approved a settlement between Paramount and 12 US states that had sued to block the transaction on antitrust grounds.

As well as the two giant film studios, Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros. Pictures, the deal also brings together the CBS News and CNN news networks, and the HBO and CBS television brands. Ellison says he plans to merge the subscription streaming platforms Paramount+ and HBO Max.

Ellison will serve as chairman and CEO of the new company. Former Mattel boss Ynon Kreiz is operating as co-CEO in charge of day-to-day operations.

Skydance Corp. looks set to have a total revenue in the region of $70 billion, based on the performance to date of its component parts. It says it's targeting cost savings of around $6 billion within three years as it takes advantage of overlaps created by the merger.

#1096. Emerging economies are embracing solar power and skipping fossil fuels much faster than Europe or the US

Renewable electricity is cheaper than ever. While the West got rich on coal and oil, some of the world's poorest countries are banking on solar, wind and water instead.

As emerging economies and global living standards rise, is the world in for another fossil fuel boom? The data says not. Countries from Southeast Asia to Sub-Saharan Africa aren't investing in fossil fuels as the West did; they're investing in electricity powered by renewables.

When the US and Europe industrialized in the 19th and 20th centuries, they did so using fossil energy. From coal ovens, gas heating and combustion-engine cars to electricity produced in coal and gas power plants, entire societies relied on polluting fuels.

Yet while burning fossil fuels allowed industrialized countries to boom, it also caused the climate crisis that is linked to extreme weather events around the world today.

Many of the same countries that became economically powerful off the back of fossil fuels are now trying to transition to renewables, but centuries of legacy infrastructure hold them back.

Because a gas boiler won't run on wind power and a diesel truck cannot be solar-powered, societies looking to shift to green energy need to use more electricity and increase their share of renewables.

That is where many middle- and lower-income countries have an advantage. With less existing energy infrastructure built around fossil fuels, they are better placed for a faster rollout of renewable electricity sources.

In many instances, solar power in particular is now cheaper to produce than fossil electricity. And unlike large coal or gas plants, which take years to construct and millions in investments, solar and wind farms can be built quickly and with much less upfront cost.

At the same time, according to an analysis by renewables think tank Ember, electronic devices like refrigerators, electric stoves or air conditioners have all become much cheaper in the last decades, making electricity a more affordable option as an energy source for cooking, heating and more.

While electric cars are still a luxury item in most places, their prices are coming down too. And a UN study found that in India, Indonesia and Vietnam electric scooters and motorbikes already cost the same as petrol models. Yet a lot less to fuel.

All this means that renewable electricity is quickly becoming the best choice for the wallet — not just for the climate. And countries are taking note.

"We're seeing a leapfrog take place right across the emerging markets,” said Kingsmill Bond, energy strategist at Ember. "The whole of Asia is electrifying quickly, and the whole of Europe and the United States is stagnant."

In 2023, only around a quarter of global electricity was generated by renewables. But that figure is rising fast, and almost all new capacity added these days is based on solar or wind power.

Lower-income countries already use less fossil fuels and more renewables than higher-income ones.

And large middle-income economies are outpacing richer ones as well. In 2023, solar power alone made up at least six percent and ten percent of electricity in India and Vietnam, respectively. The global average was five percent.

This boom has meant middle- and lower-income countries are electrifying fast, consuming less energy directly from fossil fuels than richer countries. Vietnam, for instance, is already using more electricity than the US, Europe and even China, and India is following the same path.

But the potential for energy leapfrogging might be particularly high in low-income countries, especially across Sub-Saharan Africa.

Countries such as Kenya, Ethiopia and Namibia are rapidly expanding their energy infrastructure to meet the demands of a growing, young population. But rural areas in many countries are not connected to electricity grids.

Joel Nana, project manager at South African NGO Sustainable Energy Africa says that "even when there is access to power, it's often not reliable."

Until a few years ago, he says, people defaulted to diesel generators to meet their energy needs. That came at a cost because importing fuel is particularly expensive in many African countries, meaning energy crises and oil price fluctuations hit much harder.

These factors make solar power a solution for both energy access and independence. And it has become so cheap that Nana says it's a "no-brainer."

"Some businesses can make back their investment in just two years, so why would you want to deploy anything else?” he said. "Solar has rapidly become the de facto default technology that everyone falls back on.”

And once it is set up, it is very effective. Sub-Saharan Africa is extremely well-suited to solar power. It gets more sun than almost anywhere in the world, and has enough space for solar farms to cover electricity demand hundreds of times over.

"If you add solar plus batteries, you can get cheap electricity about 90 percent of the time from the sun alone”, said Kingsmill Bond.

Countries like Namibia, South Africa, Kenya and Senegal have expanded their solar capacity massively in the past few years.

In a 2026 report for African Tech Futures Lab and Ember, Nana analyzed import data from China and estimated that around 100,000 solar panels were installed across Africa every day in 2026, up 45 percent from just the previous year.

Aleksi Lumijärvi, director for knowledge, policy and finance at the International Renewable Energy Association says these decisions can have a major impact on the global economy in the coming decades.

"Industrial companies will find that it will be cheaper to move production to, for example, somewhere in Sub-Saharan Africa, because they have this cheap, clean solar power plus batteries available.”

Kingsmill Bond says where electricity is cheap and abundant, wealth will follow.

"You're going to have faster growth in the emerging markets, you will have fewer financial crises. It will make a material difference to people's quality of lives.”

Nana says the next step for African countries is to establish policies that ensure everyone benefits from the solar boom.

"We are heading towards the most decentralized grid in the whole world within the next five years,” he told DW. His mantra for governments, he says, is: "This is happening, whether you like it or not.” He expects a wave of new energy regulations, many of which are now in the final stages of negotiation, to come into effect across Africa in the coming months and years.

"Countries are realizing it's in their interest to integrate this technology into national strategies, to share the benefits and to make use of this competitive advantage."

#1086. Nobelitis — How winning a Nobel Prize can give you delusions of grandeur

After being chosen for one of the world's most prestigious awards, some Nobel laureates lose touch with reality and suddenly start spouting unscientific nonsense. They're afflicted by what some have called "Nobelitis."

Linus Pauling won not one, but two Nobel Prizes — the first, for chemistry, in 1954 for his research into the nature of the chemical bond, and the Peace Prize in 1962 for his work in the anti-nuclear weapons movement.

The American chemist not only won two Nobel Prizes, but won them on his own, a rare feat for any scientist. Such an honor often turns laureates into global superstars overnight, with millions hanging on their every word.

That instant fame can be dangerous, said Nils Hansson, a professor in the Department of History, Theory and Ethics of Medicine at Heinrich Heine University in Düsseldorf. Especially, he said, when these world-renowned experts "start spreading crazy, unproven scientific theories" — ideas that, when disseminated by Nobel laureates, risk being taken seriously.

That's what happened with Pauling. Beginning in the mid 1960s he began claiming that vitamin C was a universal remedy, a cure for everything from cancer to cardiovascular conditions and degenerative diseases like Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis or osteoarthritis.

Pauling developed an alternative treatment he called orthomolecular medicine, based on specific doses of vitamins and minerals for each patient. To this day, some 60 years later, evidence for his methods remains inconclusive.

Pauling's case is a classic example of "Nobelitis," or "Nobel disease" — not an official diagnosis, but an informal term used to describe how some laureates begin to suffer delusions of grandeur later in life. It was first coined by Paul Nurse, president of The Royal Society, the UK's national academy of sciences, and himself a winner of the Nobel Prize.

"Be skeptical of everything, including yourself and your own ideas," Nurse said in a 2019 interview with KU Leuven University in Belgium, when asked what made someone a good scientist. In cases of Nobelitis, that self-skepticism is the first to go.

That was also the case with virologist Luc Montagnier, who was jointly honored in 2008 for his discovery of the human immunodeficiency virus, HIV, with colleague Francoise Barre-Sinoussi in the early 1980s.

Montagnier would later go on to support homeopathic medicine, another alternative treatment of disease and claim that HIV transmission in Africa could be diminished through better hygiene and nutrition.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Montagnier said the SARS-CoV-2 virus had been created in a lab with parts of the HIV genetic code. "It's difficult for a practicing virologist to say that a Nobel laureate in virology is spreading nonsense," said German virologist Christian Drosten on NDR's Coronavirus Update podcast in May 2020. "But that is utter nonsense."

James Watson, who was never known for his modesty, also let fame go to his head. In 1962, he was awarded the Nobel Prize along with Maurice Wilkins and Francis Crick for their discovery of the structure of the DNA molecule. But in the early 2000s, Watson repeatedly spread racist theories that Black people were less intelligent than white people.

For medical historian Hansson, Watson is a prime example that the Nobel Prize is not automatically awarded to a person who should be considered as a role model.

"You may have made a fantastic discovery, but that doesn't make you a universal genius," he said.

But the great expectations of an admiring public may also contribute to the development of Nobelitis. Paul Nurse, who won the Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine in 2001, soon found himself getting daily requests for interviews on all sorts of topics.

"As if suddenly I was acknowledgeable about all workings of the world and of human beings," he told DW.

Nurse had this advice for researchers: "Be careful quite what you say. Talk about things that you do know something about that would be fine. And if you don't know so much or you're not sure about something, be really clear that you're not sure."

Hansson has also noticed that the media eagerly report whatever Nobel laureates say — even when prize winners stray far from their area of expertise.

Nobelitis sufferers are leaders in their fields and rightly honored for their work with the Nobel. But they're also regular individuals, not immune to megalomania and other behavioral problems — meaning that homeopathy, orthomolecular medicine and other pseudosciences don't automatically become credible just because they're backed by a Nobel Prize winner.

All that is known for certain, at the time of writing and first publication (October 5, 2026), is that pneumonic plague is highly contagious.

Pneumonic plague is a form of plague that infects the lungs. Like every form of the disease, it is caused by Yersinia pestis, a bacterium usually found in small mammals, such as rodents and in the fleas that feed on them.

The World Health Organization describes the pneumonic form as the most virulent type of plague.

Its incubation period can be as short as 24 hours, and it is the only form that passes easily from person to person through respiratory particles released when a sick patient coughs. This is why, according to the WHO, it can trigger severe epidemics.

Symptoms appear quickly: high fever, headache and weakness, followed by a rapidly worsening pneumonia with shortness of breath, chest pain and a cough that often brings up blood.

A person can catch pneumonic plague by breathing in the bacteria directly, or by developing it when bubonic and/or septicemic plague go untreated and spread to the lungs.

Pneumonic plague is not a separate disease. It is one of three forms of plague: bubonic, septicemic and pneumonic.

What sets them apart is where the infection settles in the body, and that shapes how the disease spreads and how deadly it is.

Bubonic plague is the most common form and the one most people think of when they hear the word "plague." It usually follows a flea bite. The bacteria travel to the nearest lymph node, which swells into a painful lump known as a bubo. According to the WHO, it rarely spreads between people.

If bubonic plague goes untreated, the bacteria can move further through the body. If they spread into the bloodstream, the disease can become septicemic plague, which causes bleeding under the skin, tissue death in the fingers and toes, and organ failure. If the bacteria reach the lungs, the illness becomes pneumonic plague, the form that can pass from person to person through the air. Both septicemic and pneumonic plague can also develop directly, without a bubo appearing first.

Left untreated, bubonic plague kills between 30-60% of patients. The septicemic and pneumonic forms are always fatal without treatment.

Pneumonic plague is curable, but only if treatment starts quickly. The WHO warns that without treatment it can kill within 18 to 24 hours of the first symptoms.

Common antibiotics can cure the infection, and recovery rates are high when patients are treated within about a day of falling ill.

Speed is what decides the outcome. Doctors are advised not to wait for laboratory confirmation and to start antibiotics as soon as plague is suspected, according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The drugs themselves are not rare or specialized. They are standard, widely used antibiotics, and patients usually need a course of up to two weeks. To stop the infection spreading, patients are kept in isolation, and wearing masks helps protect those around them, the WHO says.

People close to a patient are treated as well, even if they have no symptoms. Both the WHO and the CDC recommend a seven-day course of preventive antibiotics for close contacts of anyone with pneumonic plague.

The WHO does not recommend a plague vaccine for the public. It reserves vaccination for high-risk groups, such as laboratory staff who work with the bacterium and health workers.

Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai won the Nobel Prize in chemistry for their groundbreaking discoveries related to the origin of chirality and life's building blocks.

The Nobel Prize in chemistry on Wednesday was awarded to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis."

Kagan, who is 95 years old and was born in France, is affiliated with France’s Université Paris-Sud, and Soai, who is 76 years old and was born in Japan, is based at the Tokyo University of Science.

The scientists share the Nobel Prize for answering longstanding questions in chemistry about life's origins.

Specifically, the duo solved an age-old mystery that has long puzzled chemists: some molecules, like amino acids, exist as two variants that are mirror images of one another, but just one of the variants out of the two is found in living organisms.

The two answered questions about why life chooses one variant over another.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the winner in Stockholm.

In chemistry, life's building blocks are what scientists call homochiral, after the Greek words for "same" and "hand."

The Nobel Committee said the chemistry prize recognizes discoveries that have enabled chemists to drive chemical reactions that lead to homochirality.

"Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously," Heiner Linke, chair of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, said in a news release. "The chemical reactions they have developed are spectacular."

The duo's discoveries, the news release said, "have been decisive for chemists who design reactions for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals."

#1046. '90s art scene in the UK: An international, edgy trendsetter

The 1990s are remembered for Brit-pop and provocative fashion, but beyond the nostalgia, it was a decade of cultural experimentation and social change. Tate Britain's new exhibition looks past the familiar icons.

The 1990s in Britain are often remembered through a haze of nostalgia consisting of rave culture, Britpop and provocative art and fashion by the likes of Tracey Emin and Alexander McQueen.

Undeniably, the decade was a time of major change in British society — the long reign of Margaret Thatcher had come to an end and the West entered a period of relative post-Cold War stability.

Meanwhile, British cultural icons like Oasis, the Spice Girls and Vivienne Westwood garnered international fame.

But Tate Britain's new exhibition "The 90s: Art and Fashion," which opens on October 8 and runs through February 14, 2027, urges viewers to look beyond the familiar names and images.

Even though now prominent artists such as Damien Hirst and Juergen Teller are among the show's 70 contributors, the exhibition also aims to highlight the creative input of those in communities which were left out of the mainstream discourse.

After all, says chief curator Edward Enninful in the exhibition's program, "the energy of the 90s was not concentrated in a single movement, but distributed across communities, disciplines and geographies that have not always been equally represented."

For Enninful, the exhibition is personal: The Ghanaian-born British fashion editor, stylist and creative director became the youngest-ever fashion director of i-D Magazine in the 90s at age 18, helping shape the diverse and experimental London fashion scene of the 1990s. He later became editor-in-chief of British Vogue in 2017. Enninful writes that he and his co-curators aimed to present a body of work which "reflects a broader landscape of practices and voices that shaped the period, many of which operated beyond the structures of visibility at the time." The exhibition pays special attention to artists of color and women whose work was not necessarily showcased as prominently at the time.

Black masculinity became "a site of cultural negotiation" in Britain in the 90s, writes co-curator Helen Little in the program text: "Figures such as Chris Ofili, Steve McQueen, Keith Piper, Koto Bolofo and Ozwald Boateng reimagined the Black masculine, challenging the racist specter of Black men, who had been defined as part of a masculinity crisis linked to post-industrial decline and excluded from the narrative of 'Cool Britannia.'"

Just like "Swinging London" in the 1960s, "Cool Britannia" became a widespread phrase to refer to the 1990s as an era during which British youth culture, pop music, art and political optimism boomed.

The 90s also offered artists plenty of chances to create without waiting for support from traditional gatekeepers. Britain entered the decade in recession, but that economic uncertainty also left artists free to do what they wished.

Empty buildings and industrial spaces were turned into informal venues. A 23-year-old Damien Hirst orchestrated "Freeze," a self-organized student exhibition in 1988 in a disused London warehouse. It became a landmark early art exhibition for the generation of artists known as the "Young British Artists" or YBA.

Artists Sarah Lucas and Tracey Emin opened The Shop, a temporary art gallery and social space in 1992, as a place where exhibitions could develop outside established galleries. Lucas and Emin also paved the way for women artists thanks to their deeply personal works and their edgy expressions of female liberation. While the Spice Girls helped make feminism accessible to a younger generation of Brits in the 90s by popularizing the "girl power" slogan and shaping "identity through agency power and resistance," as Tate curator Helen Little argues, Emin and Lucas meanwhile "presented a new visual language of clothing as art to tackle societal taboos around female sexuality, vulnerability and desire" — from t-shirts with provocative slogans to a grunge look of scuffed Dr. Martens boots with razor blades. Their work "held a mirror up to gender, behavior and identity," Little writes.

At the same time, the British fashion industry came into the international spotlight.

In a feminine sensuality counter to the gender stereotypes dismissed by Emin and Lucas, waif-like Kate Moss came to represent the feminine ideal of the 1990s. Dubbed "heroin-chic," the style was an antithesis to the glamorous, athletic physiques of the supermodels who preceded her. Corinne Day's photographs of the model led to the raw aesthetic to be labelled "grunge" after the anguished style of rock music hailing from Seattle and led by Nirvana.

The trend has undergone significant cultural reexamination since then: "Along with the look also came criticism that the strung-out look of drug or alcohol addiction was glamorized while encouraging serious eating disorders in a generation of young women," points out Alex Farquharson, director of the Tate Gallery in his foreword to the exhibition.

The 1990s were also an exciting era for Britain in haute couture, as legendary designers John Galliano led Dior, while Alexander McQueen started directing Givenchy at the age of 27.

McQueen's runway presentations were more akin to performance art than commercial fashion shows — and they often included historical critiques. Among them was the famous finale of his Fall/Winter 1998 collection at a London warehouse, during which the model stood in a circle of flames. Titled "Joan," the show was a reference to the killing of Joan of Arc. McQueen designed several other runway shows that reflected on British history, with topics of conquest and punishment repeatedly coming to the forefront. His 1995 presentation "Highland Rape" referenced the history of Scotland's subjugation, with the designer describing it as "England's rape of Scotland."

As co-curator Jessica Vaughan points out in her exhibition text: "Art, fashion and photography were not simply transgressive reactions … but reflected a genuine public unease with Britain's global identity and its past." In a world that can now feel increasingly mediated by screens, algorithms and established platforms, the artwork of the 90s offers a reminder of the value of physical spaces and fresh DIY creativity of the last smartphone-free decade.

For more than two millennia, Archimedean screws have been used for water pumping and, more recently, as turbines in hydroelectric power stations[1]. Their helical-blade structure, essential to their function, is also found in chiral molecules and nanostructures, where injected electric current has been proposed to generate torque-mediated rotation[2,3]. Here, we present a torque-driven molecular machine with a rotor actuated by electric current and conductance enhanced by its rotation. Suspending a helicene molecule between two electrode tips, we show that an applied voltage induces cyclic molecular motion with 100% directional preference that can be inverted by the voltage’s polarity. Above a voltage threshold, the current through the rotating molecule is significantly enhanced, and the enhancement onset is tuned by stretching or compressing the helical molecule. Such effects were absent in previous helical molecular junctions, likely due to structural and operational differences[4–6]. Calculations show that molecular rotation by current-induced forces explains both the observed cyclic motion and current enhancement. This mechanism predicts a transition from stepwise to continuous rotation at a threshold voltage, yielding a current increase as rotation allows adopting highly conducting configurations not accessible at rest. Our work introduces a prototype for a new class of torque-driven molecular machines with perfect rotor unidirectionality and significant current modulation.

#1024. How a multibillion-dollar oilfield has ignited the UK-Argentina feud over the Falklands

Argentina and the UK once fought a war over the Falkland Islands. Now, the multibillion-dollar Sea Lion oil project has reignited old tensions, with Buenos Aires seeking to put a stop to the plans.

Home to fewer than 4,000 people and located in an isolated corner of the South Atlantic Ocean, the Falkland Islands don't often feature in global business headlines.

However, a UK-Israeli oil exploration project in the North Falkland Basin is stoking long-running tensions between Argentina and the people of the archipelago.

Argentina has laid claim to the islands, which it calls Las Malvinas, for two centuries. In 1982, the dispute over the islands' sovereignty led to a war between the UK and Argentina, though British control was quickly restored after Argentine forces invaded the islands.

A 2013 sovereignty referendum on the islands returned a 99.8% vote in favor of remaining tied to the UK as a self-governing British Overseas Territory.

The Sea Lion oilfield project has brought the issue back to the surface. Last month, Argentine President Javier Milei threatened to launch an international legal challenge against the UK unless the project was stopped within two weeks.

The oil project is being run by Israeli company Navitas Petroleum and the UK's Rockhopper Exploration and has the full support of the Falkand Islands Legislative Assembly, which has pursued the project since 2010. The companies plan to begin extracting oil from the Sea Lion oilfield by early 2028.

Milei says the project is "the illegal plundering of our resources" and that he will take the matter to the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, an independent judicial body responsible for the application of the United Nations' Convention on the Law of the Sea.

Stacy Bragger, a member of the Falklands Islands' 12-member assembly, told DW that the dispute was "nothing new" and that Argentina is using the oil project as "a vehicle to pursue their sovereignty claim."

He thinks Milei may be stepping up rhetoric ahead of the 2027 elections in Argentina.

"When there's elections coming up, the Falklands is a very convenient tool to use in order to rally people together and distract them from domestic problems," he said.

Henry Ziemer, an analyst with the Americas program at the Center for Strategic and International Studies in Washington, says the Falklands is "a perennial sore spot in Argentine politics" and that since the 1980s, various governments have raised the issue in an attempt to rally support and to "stoke the fires of nationalism."

Howerver, he thinks Milei's intervention is noteworthy given that he had previously been open to better relations with the UK on the issue. He agrees that it may be a strategy to "boost approval ratings at a time when the government is under a lot of strong domestic pressure."

Even if Milei sees political gain in the issue, the possibility of the Sea Lion project being successful would significantly raise the economic stakes for all concerned.

The project has a budget of $1.8 billion (€1.6 billion) and could generate revenues of more than double that for the Falkland Islands. The companies hope that at its peak, the field will produce up to 50,000 barrels per day and could last for up to 35 years.

That would substantially increase the Falklands' current annual GDP, which is almost entirely dependent on the volatile fishing sector and tourism. The administration is even looking into the possibility of creating a sovereign wealth fund if the oil project is successful.

"It's a very significant project for us with a lot of potential for our future," said Bragger. "It's been a big effort to get to this point and assuming all goes to plan, we want to retain as much value as possible."

However, beyond playing to domestic politics, Milei may also spy an opportunity for Argentina to position itself as an energy player amid the war in Iran and the troubles in the Strait of Hormuz.

Argentina's own domestic oil sector is growing, with production surging at Patagonia's Vaca Muerta, one of the world's largest shale developments. Were Milei's legal efforts to prove successful, there may be further gains.

"When natural resources overlay existing territorial disputes and bad blood, that tends to galvanize a lot of conflict," said Ziemer.

However, Bragger counters that Milei and Argentina have no legitimate claim to either the Falklands or whatever oil wealth may be extracted over the coming years.

"They don't respect our right to self-determination," he added. "They don't respect our right to develop our own resources. They just want to colonize us."

Milei plans to put pressure on the Falklands by stepping up existing economic sanctions. Andrea Clausen, chief executive of the Falklands since 2025, recently described this campaign as "economic warfare."

Many international suppliers and shipping companies already refuse to supply the Falklands for fear of angering Argentina. Now, Milei's government has initiated sanctions against 60 people involved in the Sea Lion project.

In a televised address last month, the Argentine president also vowed to sanction the companies involved in the project. Such a move could cast doubt on the project, which previously collapsed in 2014 and 2020, due to financing troubles, oil-price weakness and delays caused by sanctions.

Navitas this week confirmed that two contractors had pulled out of the project due to sanctions concerns, and that it was replacing them. There are also concerns that Milei could try to garner international support in favor of his position, with US President Donald Trump recently suggesting that US support for the status quo on the islands was "under review."

Bragger played this down, pointing to recent comments from US Secretary of State Marco Rubio confirming support for the Falklands. Ziemer said it would be "very shocking" if the US came down on the side of Argentina.

While uncertainty reigns over whether the oil project ultimately gets going or not, there is little expectation that the dispute could lead to a return of armed conflict like in 1982.

"That's a step too far, and the situation would have to dramatically deteriorate before we would see the potential for military conflict," said Ziemer.
