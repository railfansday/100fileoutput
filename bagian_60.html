#120. Japanese prosecutors charge US marine suspected of robbing and killing woman

Devin Jacob Ballard, 20, denies charges that he fatally strangled Anna Yagi, 39, in a hotel on the island of Okinawa

Prosecutors in Japan have filed formal charges against a US marine suspected of murdering and robbing a woman while stationed on the country’s island of Okinawa, according to Japanese media.

Devin Jacob Ballard, a 20-year-old lance corporal assigned to Marine Corps Air Station Futenma in Okinawa, is charged with fatally strangling Anna Yagi, 39, at a hotel in the city and stealing from her a backpack containing a wallet, the public broadcaster NHK reported.

Ballard had initially been arrested in Yagi’s killing on 4 October, about a day after she was found dead in Naha, Okinawa’s capital. Prosecutors indicted him in the case on Sunday, and he has reportedly denied the charges.

NHK and other Japanese media outlets had previously reported that Ballard was arrested after surveillance camera footage from near the scene of Yagi’s killing showed a man entering a room with a woman and later leaving the scene with her belongings.

Ballard evidently matched the description of the suspect seen in the footage, was confronted and agreed to accompany investigators voluntarily to a local police station, Japanese media reported.

While being questioned by police, Ballard denied having a hand in Yagi’s killing, saying, “I don’t know anything about it,” NHK reported. But during subsequent questioning Ballard was alleged to have “made statements that partially hinted at his involvement”, the broadcaster reported.

A statement reported by NHK and reported to Yagi’s family on Sunday expressed a hope that Ballard “will confront what he has done and make amends commensurate with what he has done”.

“She lost her life at the young age of 39,” the statement from Yagi’s family added. “She … wanted to live much, much longer.”

Yagi’s killing prompted protests in Okinawa and the Japanese capital of Tokyo, with political leaders publicly condemning her killing. It also triggered a curfew and alcohol ban for US military members stationed in Okinawa, where most of the 54,000 US troops in Japan are based.

Crimes committed by US troops in Japan – including rape, assault, hit-and-run and drunk-driving – have long strained relations between the countries, which are close military allies.

A Japanese court in June 2025 sentenced US marine Jamel Clayton, then 22, to seven years in prison after he was convicted of choking and sexually assaulting a woman in her 20s.

In 2017, a former US marine and civilian military base worker named Kenneth Shinzato was sentenced to life in prison for the rape and murder of a local Okinawa woman.

US military forces in Japan that same year banned all personnel from consuming alcohol after an intoxicated US marine crashed his vehicle into a mini-truck, killing a local driver, age 61.

The Yomiuri Shimbun newspaper reported that about 6,000 US military personnel have been arrested since the US returned Okinawa to Japan after the second world war in 1972.

There were 80 such arrests in 2024, constituting a 20-year high.

After Ballard’s arrest, Shinjiro Koizumi, Japan’s defense minister, said: “Incidents and accidents involving US military personnel cause great anxiety among the local community and must not be allowed to happen.”

#119. Anthropic Flags Gas Supply as One of Its Biggest Near-Term Risks

Data centers’ reliance on private natural gas plants is a key near-term risk to Anthropic. In the company’s IPO filing on Monday, it warned that the data center gas buildout could drive prices higher in some regions, affecting its AI ambitions and other data center operators around the country.

As America’s data center boom keeps growing, the facilities are set to consume a staggering amount of natural gas, with many companies opting to build their own plants rather than wait years for grid connection. Energy analyst company Cleanview estimates that behind-the-meter gas projects in development as of October 2026 at 110 gigawatts, up from just 20 gigawatts in July of 2025.

Competition from other natural gas-fired data centers could leave Anthropic exposed to “fuel price volatility and supply disruptions that are outside of our control,” the company wrote in its IPO filing, and “could drive up the price of natural gas in such areas and may adversely affect our ability to contract for firm fuel supply.”

The surge in demand for gas comes on top of an already tight market for natural gas. US export terminals are sending more gas abroad for a variety of reasons, including geopolitical conflicts and federal support for exports. Export demand is expected to double over the next decade.

Still, data centers could add a hefty chunk of demand, even if just some of what’s in the pipeline is built. BloombergNEF estimates that grid-connected data centers alone could add an additional 15 billion cubic feet per day of natural gas demand by 2035, a 15 percent increase in overall demand. Adding on a conservative estimate of onsite gas capacity in the pipeline would add another 3 billion cubic feet per day by the next decade.

Anthropic has traditionally relied on renting compute from major cloud service providers. Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and SpaceX are specifically named in the IPO. (Anthropic is renting server space from the latter’s Colossus data centers in Memphis, Tennessee, which is facing a lawsuit for relying on unpermitted gas turbines.)

But the company has moved in recent months to directly lease and develop data centers, in a bid to lessen its reliance on other tech giants and more directly control its own data centers. In the IPO, it singles out its $50 billion partnership with Fluidstack, announced last year, which “provides data center development and operations services” for the company. It has announced data center sites in Texas and New York.

Doing so, though, exposes it to more risks, including the rises in natural gas prices. The company has also been tied to developing data centers with major on-site gas projects in states as spread out as Louisiana, Texas, and West Virginia. The latter could eventually be among the largest gas plants in the US, according to developer Nscale’s own IPO filing.

The geographical dispersion of its data centers opens Anthropic up to regional gas supply shocks and markets. That’s particularly true in the Southeast, where projects will have to compete with LNG exports.

“It wouldn’t be surprising if you start to see gas start to get more expensive in Louisiana and Texas, where all the LNG projects are concentrated,” says Enrique Gonzalez, BNEF’s head of gas research for the Americas.

Texas is also the national hotspot for behind-the-meter data center development, putting multiple developers in competition for the same, potentially limited gas supply. Anthropic acknowledges this risk in its IPO filing, writing that “we expect increased competition among users for the same natural gas supply and pipeline capacity in those areas. This competition could drive up the price of natural gas in such areas and may adversely affect our ability to contract for firm fuel supply.”

Any strain on gas supply could have knock-on prices for home energy bills. Gonzalez thinks that natural gas producers learned their lesson from the energy crisis of 2022, when high gas prices caused production to increase, in turn knocking down prices. While markets are unlikely to be that extreme in the coming years, “if hub prices rise, there’s a chance—unless you have legislation—that it goes to consumers,” he says.

One solution for this is, of course, to look at renewables, which also have the added climate benefit of being carbon-free. But Anthropic mentions them once in its IPO filing, and only to dismiss them. “Newer sources of generation, such as solar, wind, battery storage, nuclear, and geothermal power, may not be available in the quantities or on the timelines we require, or may be available only at higher cost,” the prospectus states.

The German agency responsible for finding a final repository for nuclear waste (BGE) has eliminated five states from its search.

Berlin, Hesse, Rhineland-Palatinate, Saxony and Saarland were deemed unsuitable for long-term radioactive waste storage.

In a report issued Tuesday, the BGE said it had limited its search to 5% of Germany, down from 25% in 2025. The federal agency aims to suggest suitable locations for final evaluation by the end of 2027.

Regions in northern, northwestern, southern and eastern Germany remain under consideration as locations for final storage facilities.

Germany's nuclear waste is currently stored in 16 above-ground interim storage facilities.

The BGE assessments evaluate regions' geological conditions and their ability to safely contain highly radioactive material. Granite, salt, and clay are generally considered potentially suitable types of rock and areas with damaged or compromised subsurface conditions, places with mines or volcanos have been active, are not eligible as final disposal sites.

Germany needs to find a repository for approximately 27,000 cubic meters of highly radioactive nuclear waste generated over more than 60 years of nuclear power generation. The site is expected to ensure safety for 1 million years. The last German nuclear power plants were decommissioned in April 2023.

BGE said its goal remains finding the site that offers the highest level of safety for highly radioactive waste.

The agency's recommendations, however, are not legally binding and it will be up to German lawmakers to determine the final disposal site.

Browser maker Opera has introduced a new web standard that lets users run AI models directly in its browser, without relying on the cloud.

The new standard, WebAI, is designed to work with a variety of AI models. Opera name drops Google’s Gemma — an open-weight model that Google introduced two years ago — as one example. On Macs, WebAI also supports Apple’s on-device foundation model, the company says.

But Opera says WebAI isn’t tied to any particular model and is built to support a wide range of open-weight AI systems. WebAI grew out of previous work by Opera to bring locally run models into its browsers.

Opera is making the new AI features available as an early preview in its flagship Opera One browser. The company says Opera GX, its browser built for gamers and streamers, and Opera Neon, its browser that uses AI agents to perform tasks, will soon get the same treatment. Users have to choose to opt into the feature, according to the company.

Open-weight models offer a number of benefits to users. For one, they are typically free to download and use. Their underlying model weights are also publicly available, which gives developers more freedom to customize how they work. Although unlike fully open source software, open-weight models still come with some restrictions.

There’s the additional advantage of privacy, the company said. Users can tap into AI features without having to send their personal information to a company’s servers or pay for a cloud-based AI subscription.

“Developers can add AI features without running AI infrastructure or paying for every request,” the company said in its announcement. “Users don’t need AI subscriptions, the information the local AI handles stays on their own device, and they stay in control of the AI on their computer.”

The company said WebAI also allows anyone to generate text, classify content, and understand how pieces of information relate, all directly in the browser.

Privacy has become a growing concern as AI assistants become more powerful and gain access to personal information. Users often surrender vast amounts of personal information with AI chatbots, sometimes without a clear understanding of how that data is stored or used.

Locally run models offer an alternative because the processing happens on the user’s own device — and not on a company’s cloud server. That doesn’t eliminate every privacy risk, but it does give users a bit more control. It makes sense then that Opera has made digital privacy one of its primary selling points over the years.

There are trade-offs, however. Running AI models locally puts more demand on a user’s computer, which can affect performance. Opera notes that model performance will depend on the device’s hardware.

#112. Nanotyrannus skeleton sells for $47.3m in New York auction

The mounted skeleton of a newly-identified dinosaur once mistaken for a young Tyrannosaurus rex has sold at auction for a colossal $47.3m (Â£35.5m).

Nicknamed Jodi, the roughly 10-foot-tall (3m) skeleton was auctioned by Sotheby's in New York. The sale comes a year after researchers confirmed that the skeleton belongs to a distinct genus.

The Late Cretaceous carnivore was expected to fetch at least $9m when it went under the hammer on Tuesday. The auction comes after Sotheby's sold a Tyrannosaurus rex, dubbed Gus, for a record $50.1m in July.

The sale has reignited debate in the scientific community over whether dinosaur remains should be sold privately or displayed publicly.

Sotheby's described Jodi as an "excellent exhibition-ready" skeleton with about 191 fossil-bone elements and a "beautifully preserved, virtually complete skull".

The auction house added that the Nanotyrannus is "one of only three such skeletons known to science". The specimen is the first to come to market since the existence of the genus was confirmed by US researchers last October, Sotheby's said.

The identify of the buyer, who won after a thrilling 12 minutes of auction bidding between six interested parties, has not been disclosed.

It becomes the second-most valuable dinosaur fossil ever sold, behind Gus. The third-most valuable was a Stegosaurus named Apex, which was sold by Sotheby's in July 2024 for $44.6m.

Megan Jacobs, a palaeontologist at the University of Portsmouth, told BBC Radio 4's Today programme on Tuesday that Nanotyrannus "was for a very long time believed to be sort of a teenager of T. rex".

"But it's a lot smaller and its a lot more gracile. So it's very sort-of sleek, slender, [with] blade-like teeth for slicing through meat," Jacobs said.

Tyrannosaurus rex and Nanotyrannus lived alongside one another during the same geological period approximately 67 million years ago.

Jodi was initially exhibited as a juvenile T. rex at London's Frieze Masters international art fair in October 2023, before the Nanotyrannus genus was classified.

The skeleton was discovered in the US state of Montana by rancher Jodi Pierson on her land in the Hell Creek Formation, a heavily studied geological site. It was later excavated by commercial palaeontologist Clayton Phipps.

Phipps also discovered the famous "Dueling Dinosaurs" fossil. That specimen, which shows the remains of a Nanotyrannus and Triceratops apparently in combat, was found eight miles (13km) away from Jodi in 2006.

The "Dueling Dinosaurs" discovery became crucial to the research published by Lindsay Zanno and James Napoli that classified Nanotyrannus as separate from T. rex. Their paper ended decades of debate over whether the dinosaurs were distinct from one another.

The auction of Jodi comes as some scientists warn that auctions could herald a new era in fossil collecting by the ultra-rich.

Researchers told the BBC after the sale of Gus that such remains should be exhibited in public spaces where they can be more freely studied.

Prof Steve Brusatte, a palaeontologist at the University of Edinburgh and consultant on the Jurassic Park films, told BBC World Service's What in the World programme in July that the T. rex's auction was "concerning".

"I don't know of any museum or university that can afford a price tag like that," he said. "Imagine if the Mona Lisa was just owned by a super-wealthy person and it wasn't on display and people couldn't see it, couldn't enjoy it."

It also cancelled all of Bolsonaro’s passports, including the diplomatic one he holds as a congressman, and ordered him to hand them over within 24 hours.

Bolsonaro was the first to speak publicly about the matter, before Brazilian media were even aware of the judge’s decision.

#64. Harry Kane: Ballon d’Or for Bayern Munich striker

Bayern Munich striker Harry Kane has won football's most prestigious individual prize, beating Kylian Mbappe and Lamine Yamal. Kane has developed even more in the Bundesliga despite an uneven league.

To most Bayern Munich fans under the age of 40, Karl-Heinz Rummenigge was the club CEO who regularly spoke his mind. He was also the last Bayern man to win the Ballon d'Or — in 1981. The last Bundesliga player to win was Borussia Dortmund's Matthias Sammer, in 1996.

That long drought ended on Monday, with Harry Kane, born 12 years after Rummenigge's win, picking up the 2026 trophy at a ceremony held, as a one-off, in his native London.

"I am almost speechless. Being here in London has made it extra special,” he said on stage.

Not many people would have thought it, and maybe even I wouldn't have thought I'd be this far," Kane later told British sports streaming plaftorm DAZN.

Lamine Yamal, the prodigious Barcelona and Spain forward who won the Euros at 16, finished third. At the end of the 2025-26 season, on which the award is judged, Yamal won the World Cup with Spain as well as the La Liga title. Kylian Mbappe came in second.

Kane's 73 goals for club and country last season made for a strong case, and the Bundesliga's most expensive signing of all time has already racked up 156 goals in 159 games for the serial German champions. But it's not just the numbers that tell the story, according to his club coach.

"People in England saw him as a goalscorer during his time playing there," Vincent Kompany said of Kane last season. "We've seen other qualities too, such as his footballing intelligence. "He always has the right attitude, always leads the way and sets the tone," Gnabry said ahead of Bayern's 2-2 draw in the Champions League against Arsenal last week. "He never strays from his path, helps the team with his performance and is a top-tier player."

Kane scored a penalty in that match and provided a sensational pass from which Alphonso Davies scored Bayern's opener, another reminder of his all-round game. "The pass was one of my best ones," Kane said after the match. "I knew Phonzy [Davies] would be able to get there with his speed. Whatever I can do to help the team, I'll do. At the moment, it seems to be with assists more than goals."

That attitude has been with Kane from the start. His first-ever coach, Dave Bricknell, from Ridgeway Rovers, an amateur club in northeast London, told DW after Kane joined Bayern from Spurs in 2023: "Harry's mentality is his greatest strength. His England coach, Thomas Tuchel, has also emphasized Kane's leadership. "He is the first one to push everyone, and that is his mindset, and he became a very, very hardworking forward on top of his finishing skills, Tuchel said in September as Kane extended his England goal-scoring record against the Czech Republic. "He is a complete package."

There are detractors who point to perceived "stat-padding," or scoring against inferior opponents in international qualifiers and in the Bundesliga, where there are fewer elite teams and a lot less money, than in England. Kane winning the award is also a boost in profile for the Bundesliga, which has been unable to keep pace, both commercially and on the pitch, with other top European leagues of late, particularly the Premier League and La Liga.

Winning it in a league outside of England or Spain perhaps makes Kane's feat even greater. Other than Ousmane Dembele, who won it last year after Paris Saint-Germain's Champions League win, and Lionel Messi, a serial Ballon d'Or winner, the last player to take the Ballon d'Or for a season played outside England or Spain was was Kaka at AC Milan in 2007, when Italy's Serie A was considered a stronger league.

Kane's career hasn't been straightforward, something he admitted in his speech. After signing his first senior contract with Spurs in July 2010, he was sent out on various loans to lower league sides between 2011-2013 without ever making a huge impression.

"My journey wasn't straightforward. I wasn't the kid who went straight to the top. I had to experience the downs, I had a lot of loan moves, I had to fight my way to be the very best right now, and I hope that can give you determination as well," Kane said in his speech.

Kane will be 35 by the time of the next Euros, in the UK and Ireland in 2028, and has a deal with Bayern until the following year. His form since joining Bayern suggests that he could get better still.

On October 2nd, just as Major League Baseball’s Division Series was kicking off, the main MLB Instagram and TikTok accounts posted a video hyping up the teams still in the running for the World Series. It featured clips of players from the Milwaukee Brewers, Los Angeles Dodgers, Chicago White Sox, and more sliding into home plate, hitting homers, and celebrating hits — standard fare for a highlight reel. What really makes the montage, though, is that it’s set to an Olivia Rodrigo song.

The song, “My Way,” is an album track — not even a single — from Rodrigo’s latest record. It’s not exactly the soundtrack one might expect from an MLB-produced video. (“And here is the part where the girl gets pissed / And the girl is me, did you get that hint?” she sings.) But as evidenced by the comments, it hit exactly the right people: “I see I’m part of a very niche part of MLB fans,” one person said. “Target audience reached,” another responded. The clip has over 2.1 million views on Instagram and 615,000 views on TikTok.

“One of our favorite things is when a community says, ‘Oh, MLB knows ball,’ or, ‘Oh, I can’t believe my interests [are] converging,’” says Cameron Gidari, MLB’s vice president of social media and innovation. The social team is a fan of Rodrigo’s to begin with and loves the song, Gidari says, “but then also we thought we could make a really interesting edit with it, and we thought that community would appreciate being seen.”

Acknowledging the combination of baseball–Olivia Rodrigo fans is smart. It also might be essential for the survival of the sport. People love baseball, of course, but it often feels like it’s trailing other sports, not just in viewership but also culturally. Baseball is famously stats heavy — there are thousands of games every season across the league, and teams play almost every day. What happens on the field today is often contextualized through what happened in games in the past, making it hard to jump in without a history of watching (or learning about) the sport. Go back a few years, and you’d find MLB social channels were more “editorially focused,” Gidari says, focusing on facts of the game or highlights. Speaking to fans who have other interests — or who are simply more casual — has been a deliberate choice, he says.

“Our tone overall on social is the tone of fandom,” Giardi says. “We think there is something really inviting and magnetic about that, and it was a conscious shift.”

Here’s a post of Dua Lipa and Callum Turner at a Yankees game, the night the team was eliminated from the postseason. Here’s an edit spliced together with shots of athletes looking shocked and in awe, as if they were reacting to a song by the K-pop group Stray Kids. Here’s a highlight reel of White Sox first baseman Munetaka Murakami being funny.

The nature of social media algorithms all but requires MLB to branch out. Recommendation-based social feeds mean that following an account doesn’t necessarily mean followers will see the content — feeds have become reorganized into content niches, topics, and trends, and Gidari says “really aggressive algorithm changes” meant the league had to rethink what it posts. Content from the MLB in English- and Spanish-language social accounts racked up 19.1 billion views during the 2026 regular season, Gidari says, a record.

A significant part of that shift has been MLB’s embrace of influencers and content creators. So far this year, the league has tapped 400 creators who posted a combined 3,800 pieces of content, and in the postseason, MLB has had content creators at every single game. The league expects to have 100 content creators at the World Series, depending on how many games it runs.

The openness and collaboration with content creators is relatively new for MLB. As recently as 2024, content creators (including Jimmy “Jomboy” O’Brien, who does viral lip-reading breakdowns) complained of getting wide-ranging copyright takedowns on their content. In 2025, Commissioner Rob Manfred acknowledged the league fumbled this slice of the fanbase and that changing its tune had been “a really good thing” for MLB. (The league even acquired a stake in O’Brien’s media company last year.) More recently, a TikTok partnership gave select creators access to the league’s archives to use in their content.

For the first time, MLB has expanded beyond having influencers only at big events. The league now brings content creators to regular season games, too, and has broadened its reach to include more than just baseball and sports creators. Rachel Richter, director of influencer strategy, says MLB works with food, fashion, lifestyle, gaming, and family influencers, too.

“There are casual fans or [people] that aren’t even fans yet, that maybe they’re going to go to a game just because they get to put on a cute outfit, or they want to try this new food item,” Richter says. The idea is to get those creators to a game and let them incorporate baseball into their usual content.

The league is building on momentum, especially coming off a successful season last year: Game 7 of the 2025 World Series between the Dodgers and the Toronto Blue Jays was the most-watched World Series game since 2017, and more international viewers watched last year’s matchup than ever before. But as other sports have found, it can be a learning curve to balance longtime fans and the culture of the sport with an insurgent, at times unpredictable, class of promotional power brokers: The biggest story out of this year’s US Open, for example, was not the tennis itself but the many complaints and debates about the influencers who attended.

MLB is working to expand what constitutes a fan, reaching into corners of the internet and culture that previously were ignored or, at the very least, untapped. At the same time, it’s hurtling toward an uncertain 2027 season as the collective bargaining agreement between team owners and the MLB Players Association is set to expire in December. If the union and owners don’t reach a deal in time, players likely would be locked out, and the 2027 season could be shortened. Messaging that conflict would be a challenge in itself for the league — especially after adopting the voice of new, casual, and eager fans who are looking for a reason to tune in.

When attorneys general from across the US announced a $17 billion settlement with Meta this summer, the sum was unprecedented. Similar claims were resolved by the settlement with most other states. The outcome raised an obvious question: Should these states have asked for more?

Mahoney said in a statement that the company is “confident we will prevail on appeal” and called New Mexico’s lawsuit an attempt to impose “a tax on speech.” If the fine sticks, however, it could create a new appetite for Big Tech’s opponents to stick it out in court — or ask for bigger payouts to settle.

“For the settling AGs, they’re going to look at this and maybe experience some regret for all the claims that they waived, and start looking around and trying to figure out what’s left that they didn’t waive, if they can try to get some money like this too,” says Daphne Keller, director of platform regulation at the Stanford Program in Law, Science & Technology. For the many individuals, school districts, and tribes with outstanding claims, “this is very encouraging and will make them hopeful that continuing to litigate aggressively will lead to a big payout,” Keller says.

The $25 billion court order covered millions of violations of New Mexico’s unfair practices law, after a jury found Meta had misled users about how it protected their data and policed misinformation and hate speech – claims stemming from the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal. In most states, such claims were covered by a section of the kids online safety settlement, resolved with a $459 million payment.

While that number looks comparatively puny, there were upsides to taking it. A settlement provided more certainty and an expedited payout for states even though Meta will be making the payments over 10 years. It also secured product commitments from Meta, like restrictions on teen usage, that might have been more difficult to get from a court ruling. And it allowed those to be implemented soon after the settlement was approved, which advocates say is important to prevent further harm to kids. Other state attorneys general also may have been working with different laws or evidence that could result in outcomes different from those obtained in New Mexico.

“Assuming that even a fraction of this award gets upheld, then they clearly took pennies on the dollar compared to New Mexico”

But New Mexico’s win, combined with other recent legal victories for AGs and private plaintiffs — including a separate judgment of nearly $1 billion against Meta in New Mexico — may embolden anyone still litigating privacy and kids online safety cases. “Assuming that even a fraction of this award gets upheld, then they clearly took pennies on the dollar compared to New Mexico,” Keller says of the settling states. Imagining she’s an AG with similar claims — like public false statements about privacy or content moderation — “I become less willing to settle in those cases,” Keller adds. “Or if I do settle, I ask for more.”

As for whether the $25 billion ruling makes other states rethink their strategy, Reid says there could be a range of approaches, depending partly on whether the figure is upheld on appeal and whether Meta loses yet more cases. “Is that a game that every state attorney general wants to play?” he asks. “And I think the settlement in the other case suggests maybe not.” Still, the new figure may change what AGs think they can achieve in court. And the more losses Meta and other tech companies rack up, the more ambitious they might get.

Anthropic wants to have it both ways. With its impending IPO, it wants to tap the vast riches of the public markets and insists it doesn’t want to compromise its core mission of developing AI safely and for the benefit of humanity. But greater model capabilities bring greater risks, and shareholders eager for returns may have little patience for costly safety measures that slow products on their way to market.

Sam Altman, the CEO of Anthropic’s archrival, OpenAI, has cited investor pressures as one reason the company is not pursuing an IPO this year. Anthropic hopes it can resist investor pressures with a mind-boggling governance structure.

A “unique and complicated governance structure that has not been used before…creatively designed for this founder-led and mission-driven company.”

University of Pennsylvania law professor Elizabeth Pollman told The Verge that Anthropic has proposed going public with a “unique and complicated governance structure that has not been used before” and that has been “creatively designed for this founder-led and mission-driven company.” Experts say it should give Anthropic freedom to pursue profits without letting shareholders force it to cut corners on safety or rush systems out before they’re adequately tested. But even the strongest legal protections can only go so far, and no corporate structure, no matter how well designed, can completely insulate a public company from the demands of the market.

Foremost, Anthropic is a public benefit corporation. Simply put, it is legally required to focus on more than just maximizing shareholder returns, balancing these with its stated social mission and the interests of stakeholders, such as employees and specific communities. Patagonia and Kickstarter are among the better-known examples, with missions centered on environmental conservation and supporting creative communities, respectively.

Another key safeguard aims to protect Anthropic’s mission by limiting shareholder influence over one of the biggest levers of corporate power: the company’s board of directors. Directors oversee the company and appoint its leadership, including the CEO. Directors can also hold executive roles, as is the case with Anthropic, with CEO Dario Amodei and president Daniela Amodei both sitting on the board.

Shareholders typically have considerable sway over who sits on the board, giving them a means to replace directors or push for changes if they are unhappy with how the company is being run. For example, if shareholders are unhappy at what they perceive as unnecessary delays to market for optional safety testing, they may remove directors and replace them with ones more aligned with their interests, such as making money and being less cautious.

“Anthropic is entirely insulated from profit seeking pressures, in the sense that ordinary shareholders have no ability to select the board or make business decisions.”

Under Anthropic’s proposed governance structure, ordinary shareholders won’t have much of a say over who is on the board at all. Instead, four of the board’s seven directors will be chosen by a special body called the long-term benefit trust, explains Jens Dammann, a professor in business and commercial law at the University of Texas School of Law. It is a highly unusual setup for a major technology company.

The trust is made up of “five financially disinterested voting trustees” with expertise in “AI safety, national security, public policy, and social enterprise.” Through a special class of share called Class T stock, the trustees can appoint or remove directors in pursuit of their stated purpose: ensuring “that Anthropic responsibly balances the financial interests of stockholders with the interests of those affected by Anthropic’s conduct and our public benefit purpose.” The trust is designed to operate independently of the company’s commercial pressures, allowing it, in theory, to put Anthropic’s mission first.

Ordinary shareholders technically have a say in the board’s three remaining seats. However, Anthropic has created another safeguard that keeps power firmly in the hands of its founders, called “Founder LLC,” which will control 50.1 percent of the voting power on shareholder matters. Given that many shareholder votes are decided by a simple majority, Founder LLC basically gives Anthropic’s founders the ability to block or force through decisions, regardless of how other shareholders vote. This means that they will effectively choose the remaining three directors, Columbia Law School professor Dorothy Lund told The Verge.

The prospectus says Founder LLC will “initially” be seven of Anthropic’s founders: Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Tom Brown, Jack Clark, Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, and Christopher Olah. “Our seven co-founders will initially be the sole members.” Notably absent is another cofounder, Ben Mann, who co-leads the company’s experimental Anthropic Labs arm, as reported by The San Francisco Standard.

All in all, the arrangements would give Anthropic’s founders and the trust “tremendous power” over the company’s direction, shielding it from many of “the pressures associated with being a public company,” Dammann told The Verge. “Given the unusual governance structure Anthropic has chosen, I don’t see how going public, by itself, would undermine its ability to prioritize safety over commercial interests.”

Ann Lipton, a professor at the University of Colorado Law School, agreed, telling The Verge that, legally speaking, “Anthropic is entirely insulated from profit seeking pressures, in the sense that ordinary shareholders have no ability to select the board or make business decisions.” But Lipton cautioned that the founders themselves have financial stakes in the company, which could influence their decisions.

“Investors may not mind their lack of voice as long as things go well. But if things go sour, they may become quite frustrated.”

And there’s a big difference between legal protections and immunity from market pressures. Anthropic, like all AI companies, “desperately needs capital,” Lipton said. “It’s not clear to me that any governance structure will protect against the market reality that unless Anthropic takes advantage of profit making opportunities at a faster pace than its competitors, it will not be able to fund its operations.” Lund expressed similar concerns. Anthropic will still “need to stay in the good graces of their investors,” she said, adding that “competitive pressure and capital needs can erode safety commitments over time.” Anthropic did not respond to The Verge’s request for comment on whether it felt the company could actually resist shareholder pressure to massively accelerate AI at the cost of safety.

Another potential vulnerability emerges if Anthropic’s founders start leaving. In its prospectus, the company says its success is “highly dependent on the continued service” of its senior team, particularly its founders. Dario and Daniela are singled out as being especially “critical” to advancing the company’s research and mission. But beyond the loss of leadership, their departure could trigger significant changes in how the company is structured, potentially stripping away some of the safeguards preventing Anthropic from pursuing profit over safety.

Under the proposed structure, Founder LLC would “sunset” if a majority of its members leave or the group chooses to dissolve it. Once that happens, ordinary shareholders could gain the power to weaken the trust’s protections, even against the wishes of the trustees, and without the support of the founders or employees, who hold different classes of stock, Dammann told The Verge. Other means of subverting the trust also exist, he explained, including coordinated action by the public shareholders, employee stockholders, and founders.

For now, it seems, Anthropic can probably have it both ways. But the protections designed to preserve its commitment to AI safety are far from ironclad, and much depends on market pressures, whether its founders stick around, and directions of the trust. Dammann said he sees a “fundamental dilemma that cannot be resolved in a fully satisfactory way” between Anthropic’s mission and investor demands. As long as things are going well, this tension may be manageable. “Investors may not mind their lack of voice as long as things go well,” Dammann said. “But if things go sour, they may become quite frustrated.”
