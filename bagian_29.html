#3220. Anthropic's IPO is risky business

Anthropic's much-anticipated initial public offering is giving potential investors some perspective on the risky business of artificial intelligence.

More than 90 pages of Anthropic's IPO prospectus, revealed Monday, center on the AI lab's multitude of corporate risk factors, many of which go beyond the kinds of boilerplate scenarios typically found in corporate filings.

From the end of humanity to the company's current clash with the Trump administration, Anthropic's IPO document underscores how one of the most well-capitalized startups in history foresees the future of its operations.

The Claude maker's risks include a laundry list of ways its models can cause harm, including potentially "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity."

It's imperative for Anthropic to emphasize AI safety because failure to do so could adversely affect its "brand, business, financial condition, and results of operations," the filing said. But fierce competition, market pressure and stakeholder expectations make the task of safely developing and implementing bleeding-edge foundation models particularly challenging.

Anthropic can't guarantee that its AI safety efforts will be adequate, as the technology's inherent complexity makes it difficult for the company to plan for and predict every potential scenario.

The public's souring position on data centers poses a major risk for Anthropic, which relies on an ever-expanding portfolio of state-of-the-art computing facilities to train and maintain its prized foundation models.

And with the midterm elections approaching, the tech industry's massive data center build-out has turned political, with lawmakers in 16 states considering moratoriums, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures.

"Opposition to data center development  has also become more organized and coordinated across jurisdictions and our public profile may draw particular attention to projects associated with us," the filing said.

Anthropic's future success depends not only on building giant data centers, but also on the electrical and related power infrastructure needed to juice those computing facilities.

The lack of "sufficient electrical power" across the nation and world poses big risks to Anthropic and its rivals, the filing notes. Even if energy becomes more readily available through "on-site natural gas-fired generation," that power could become more expensive with so much demand, the filing said.

Rivals like OpenAI and Meta have come under fire from lawmakers and plaintiffs' attorneys over allegations that the design and implementation of their AI models and apps have fostered certain child safety-related harms. Meta recently agreed to pay roughly $17 billion in a settlement with multiple state attorneys general in litigation involving child mental health and technology addiction.

Thus far, Anthropic has largely avoided scrutiny, but the filing noted that the company could potentially face its own legal headaches. Based on its terms of service, minors aren't allowed to access Claude and related products.

Anthropic knows it has work to do protecting children "as adoption of our models grows, including by minors and other vulnerable users, notwithstanding our efforts to restrict such access."

The company calling to pace the frontier also says it's a risk to move too slowly.

Anthropic needs to keep racing and needs the rest of the business world to keep up. If companies curb their AI spending, Anthropic could feel the burn.

"If the market for our models, platform, and applications develops more slowly than we expect or declines, our growth may slow or stall, and our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected," the filing says.

The prospectus also cites reduced demand for frontier models as a risk. Customers might find that lower-cost models work fine for them and that "incremental performance gains at the frontier do not justify the additional cost," instead choosing cheaper, or open-source alternatives.

#3213. Apollo to buy nearly 10% stake in Abu Dhabi-backed alternative asset manager Mubadala Capital

Investing giant Apollo Global Management is finalizing a deal to buy nearly a 10% stake in Mubadala Capital, the alternative asset manager subsidiary of Abu Dhabi's sovereign wealth fund, Mubadala Investment Co., according to people familiar with the matter who spoke on condition of anonymity.

The deal is expected to be announced later Monday morning, they said. Financial terms were not immediately known.

The transaction is significant on multiple fronts. It further advances Apollo's ambitions in Abu Dhabi often called the "capital of capital." The firm has spent the past 15 years building key strategic relationships there, with both the country's investment arm and its core leadership.

Apollo already has a multibillion-dollar partnership with Mubadala Investment Co., first established in 2022 and extended two years ago. At the time of that extension, Apollo said the agreement would strengthen the firm's Capital Solutions platform by enabling the firms to jointly source and provide customized debt and equity financing to companies worldwide.

In 2023, the institutions formed a $2.5 billion joint venture designed specifically to co-invest in global private credit opportunities over a five-year horizon.

For Mubadala Capital specifically — the region's only large-scale asset manager, advising and administering more than $755 billion in assets — the deal further advances its growth strategy under the direction of CEO Hani Barhoush, and its relevance on the global investing stage. It also marks an inflection point for the company's core alternatives business, which has grown to more than $60 billion.

John Zito, co-president of Apollo's Asset Management arm, and Barhoush have worked closely together over the past several years and, according to sources, their close-knit relationship and the success of prior partnerships led to talks of deepening ties. So did the belief that scale in the asset management business matters more than ever.

"Capital as a moat is a big thing," said one person familiar with the deal.

Importantly, the deal also continues a notable shift in the relationship between American investment firms and the Middle East. While the region, and Abu Dhabi specifically, has long been a sought-after source of capital, this deal further reverses that dynamic, with Apollo making a significant direct investment in the country and its investment vision.

Mubadala Capital's international offices include London, New York, Rio de Janeiro and San Francisco.

Apollo's investment in Mubadala Capital is similar to deals the firm has done with other asset management platforms, including Diameter Capital, Thoreau, Motive Partners and others.

Spokespersons for both firms declined to comment.

#3211. Frozen housing market sends remodelers mixed messages

Home remodelers have always served two different markets, but the current state of housing is now pulling them opposite directions. First, people tend to remodel when they move, but high interest rates, high home prices and weak consumer sentiment have put the housing market in a deep freeze. Homeowners also tend to renovate when they are unable or don't want to sell. So which way will the remodeling market shift?

Annual spending on home improvements and repairs is expected to remain relatively flat through late 2027, according to the latest Leading Indicator of Remodeling Activity (LIRA) from the Remodeling Futures Program at the Joint Center for Housing Studies of Harvard University. It puts spending growth on renovation and repairs at just 1.5% year-over-year by the third quarter."Retail spending on building products has been flat to negative, and remodeling permits have stagnated, which points to continued sluggish renovation activity," said Rachel Bogardus Drew, director of the remodeling futures program at the Center, in a release. "We expect annual spending to hover around $525 billion through the third quarter of next year."

The U.S. Census Bureau's monthly read on single-family housing starts for September came in well below expectations, as did building permits, an indicator of future construction. Pending home sales, measured by signed contracts, also plunged beyond expectations, according to the National Association of Realtors.

"These ongoing headwinds could slow growth in remodeling spending even further in the quarters ahead," said Chris Herbert, managing director of the center in the release.

A separate October report from the National Association of Home Builders on sentiment among home remodelers was slightly more positive, but also did not forecast big growth in 2027.

"Remodeler sentiment remained stable in the third quarter, although remodelers in certain parts of the country continue to report issues with high material costs and finding enough labor to finish projects on time," said Elliott Pike, national chair of the NAHB remodelers council and a remodeler from Homewood, Alabama. "Remodelers also report that economic uncertainty is making some potential customers hesitant to move forward with projects."

Finally, another October report from Houzz, an AI platform for construction and design professionals and homeowners, showed an improved outlook, with some caveats.

"Construction pros are closing out 2026 with optimism, as expectations reach their highest level since early 2022 following a rebound from a long stretch of slow activity. Design firms are more measured, though cautiously optimistic," said Marine Sargsyan, head of economic research at Houzz. "Fuel costs remain a pressure point for most businesses, with some pros passing them on to clients through higher prices and surcharges and others absorbing them in their margins."

Amazon laid out its AI usage guidelines for software engineers in a memo to staffers, and discouraged employees from turning to "tokenmaxxing" in their push to embrace artificial intelligence tools.

The memo, which was circulated internally in September and posted to the company's website on Tuesday, revises how the company defines the role of software development engineers and managers in the age of AI.

"We're just seeing the beginning of AI's effect on software engineering," the company wrote. "While we have a vision for what the future looks like, there's a lot that's still emerging. We are certain that whatever future best practices are, AI will be at the center of software engineering practice."

Under CEO Andy Jassy, Amazon has rapidly integrated AI across the company's sprawling business operations. Jassy has previously predicted that AI will "change the scope and speed" of day-to-day work, and potentially shrink its corporate workforce in the next few years through automation, though he's noted it will also create "other jobs."

As employees rush to adopt AI tools, companies have grappled with the technology's surging costs and how to reliably measure whether the technology leads to a productivity boost or other valuable returns on investment.

Companies have recently cracked down on tokenmaxxing, in which developers use AI as much as possible with little regard for output. Some companies have set limits on employees' usage of tokens, or the units of data that are processed and generated by AI models, in a bid to slow rising AI bills. Others have switched to cheaper, open-source models for certain tasks.

An Amazon spokesperson said the company hasn't instituted caps on employees' token usage. Amazon in May shut down an internal leaderboard after discovering employees were tokenmaxxing to climb the ranks.

In the memo posted Tuesday, Amazon said metrics like token usage, the rate of adoption of AI tools, and code changes or reviews remain "deeply flawed" ways of measuring teams or organizations' AI usage, adding that they can lead to higher costs and too much complexity.

It stopped short of telling staffers to ignore those metrics altogether, however. Token usage and code generation should be considered "with care and judgment" alongside other data, Amazon said.

"We believe that it is best to measure, and be measured by, long-term outcomes," the company wrote.

Marc Brooker, a vice president and distinguished engineer at Amazon Web Services, told CNBC in an interview that the company has looked to metrics such as "feature velocity" to measure how AI is improving engineer productivity.

The metric looks at things like, "how many launches are we doing, how many successful changes are we making in production," he said.

"Broadly, we think about this as a return on investment," Brooker said. "If there is an important problem to solve for our customers, important problem to solve for our business, we're going to invest tools, time, tokens, human effort, etc. into those."

He added, "What we don't want to see is wasted effort where we are going and spending those tokens solving problems that don't matter and sometimes it's hard to know, so we do need to have some appetite for experimentation."

Amazon also discouraged staffers from using AI when it doesn't make sense for a given task.

"If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing," Amazon said in the memo.

The company added, "An engineer who can explain where AI does not yet help their work is demonstrating the judgement we are asking for."

A credit spread is the difference in the yield between the corporate bonds and Treasurys of similar maturities. When spreads tighten, it means investors are becoming more confident about the bond issuer.

The market action over the last few days shows that investors are recognizing that "credit fears may have been overblown," said CNBC contributor Paul Meeks, head of technology research at Freedom Capital Markets.

"I expect that these better feelings, maybe not good but better, may continue as companies announce their quarters over the next few weeks."

Still, spreads on CDS remain wider over the past year due to a surge of debt issuance and free cash flows turning negative across the AI space. Meta's CDS spread, for instance, is still up 177% year to date, while Amazon's spread has gained 129%.

"It's not necessarily fundamentals," said CreditSights analyst Jordan Chalfin. "It's more, I'd say, 80% driven by technicals and sentiment."

Alphabet and Amazon pose the next big hurdle for tech bonds as they update their capex plans and free cash flow forecasts. Microsoft's earnings are also due Wednesday, but the company has yet to issue any bonds to pay for its AI spending.

S&P Global Ratings estimated in an August report that the hyperscalers will have negative free operating cash flow in 2026 and 2027.

More critical will be their outlooks, Cheng says. "The singular focus on the investors' minds is really what the capex guidance trajectory looks like across the hyperscaler space."

Meeks believes elevated capex is a sign of strong AI demand. The hyperscalers have "decided that with all the evidence they have — and they have more than you and me — that this is worthwhile spending, and not just worthwhile; it's required," he said.

It's the smallest combined market size for a World Series since the St. Louis Cardinals and Kansas City Royals met in 1985. These realities are even more striking once you consider that, in 2026, the Brewers swept the dynastic Los Angeles Dodgers in the NLCS, while the Rays vanquished the historically preeminent New York Yankees in the AL East race and then again in the ALDS.

How do you argue that super-spenders like the Dodgers and Yankees, or even disappointments like the Mets and Blue Jays, are breaking baseball when this is your World Series? MLB will, of course, argue it anyway, but the narrative packs less of a punch this October.

To be sure, there's a definite element of ownership neglect with those payroll figures. Both teams are revenue-sharing recipients on balance, and such clubs that run low payrolls make enough via revenue sharing to cover most or, in some cases, all of their cash payroll. Circumstance plays an obvious role, yes, but so does the will to compete and win on the part of owners.

Still, the question remains: How have the Brewers and Rays built championship-grade rosters without spending much at all?

In terms of a tactical approach, both have seen high contact rates on offense as a neglected trait across the league, and they've selected for that. Like most other clubs, they prioritize velocity and repertoire depth on the pitching front. For these purposes, though, we're talking about the paths to roster construction, and, unsurprisingly, the Brewers and Rays have a lot in common in their preferred means of player acquisition (and not just because Brewers president of baseball operations Matt Arnold spent nine years in the Rays front office under Andrew Friedman).

The temptation is to think that both of these small-budget teams are heavily homegrown. That's the best way to get cheap production across the roster, especially when many of those homegrown talents are in their salary-controlled years. As we'll see, though, that's not really the case. These two teams are among the very best at identifying and developing talent acquired via the draft and through amateur international signings. As noted, these two clubs don't invest as much as they should in player payroll, but they do have large and wide-ranging baseball operations and scouting staffs within their front offices. The Brewers and Rays know their own players, of course, but if there's a superpower, it's how well they appraise players, especially young ones, in other organizations. That skill informs much of their current success.

The "never trade with the Rays" contemporary chestnut of recent baseball wisdom is overstated -- they can and do lose trades -- but there's no question that trades have done the heavy lifting in the present roster's high level of success.

The Rays made their boldest stroke in acquiring 22-year-old burgeoning superstar Junior Caminero via trade with the Guardians in November 2021, when he was just an 18-year-old in the Dominican Summer League. There's some connective tissue here, too. The most famous trade in recent Brewers history was one made by Arnold's predecessor, David Stearns (coincidentally, he now leads the Mets, who traded for Peralta in the 2025-26 offseason). That was the 2018 blockbuster that brought Yelich to Milwaukee. Yelich is post-peak, but the newly named captain is still a vital presence for Milwaukee. Elsewhere -- and more germane to the current Brewers model -- trades for William Contreras, Jake Bauers, Andrew Vaughn, Trevor Megill, Robert Gasser, and Chad Patrick played a vital role in the team's success this season. Arnold, like the Rays' Neander, was also active at the deadline, as he fortified the pitching staff with Dustin May, JoJo Romero, and Antonio Senzatela.

Trade additions claimed both LCS MVP awards (Peralta for the Rays, Bauers for the Brewers), and it likewise wouldn't be shocking if a player acquired by the Rays or Brewers via trade winds up earning top individual honors in the World Series. That's a predictable consequence when the two World Series clubs have combined for, oh, almost 50 trade acquisitions.

In broad terms, the Rays and Brewers have built their championship rosters in the ways that low-spenders must. Milwaukee has made quite a lot of trades, but extracted most of its value in 2026 from homegrown players. The Rays have made even more trades, and most of their 2026 contributions have indeed come from outsiders.

When it comes to World Series teams of a recent vintage, this is something of a departure -- one worth appreciating and, no doubt, emulating if you're a team that aspires to reach the heights that the Brewers and Rays presently occupy.
