‘As a young boy coming through at Celtic, you strive to be a first-team player, and to think where I am now and where I was a year ago today, it’s been a rollercoaster.

#1400. Hearts star Claudio Braga expects fiery welcome for Rangers boss Derek McInnes on his return to Tynecastle

By JOHN MCGARRY, SCOTTISH CHIEF SPORTS FEATURE WRITER

Published: 22:30 GMT, 26 October 2026 | Updated: 22:30 GMT, 26 October 2026

Claudio Braga expects Derek McInnes to face a hostile reception when he returns to Tynecastle on Wednesday night – a ‘consequence’ of leaving Hearts for Rangers four months ago.

Last season, the 55-year-old took the Gorgie men to within three minutes of a first Scottish title since 1960.

After just one season in the capital, though, he was head-hunted by his former club as Danny Rohl’s replacement and joined Lawrence Shankland and Cammy Devlin in moving along the M8 to Govan.

While Braga is adamant that the players who McInnes previously managed still have the utmost respect for him after they almost rewrote Scottish football history, the Portuguese can well understand that supporters may see it differently.

‘Yeah, I believe so. And I understand a little bit why,’ he said.

‘Of course, we shared the locker room with them and we know them personally. And I couldn’t just hate them just because they left the club. That’s what a football player professionally needs to do. That’s their choice. I just could never judge that.

Rangers boss Derek McInnes can expect it to be hostile when he returns to Gorgie this week

Hearts striker Claudio Braga says  there will be 'consequences' for McInnes at Tynecastle

Lawrence Shankland and Cammy Devlin also swapped Hearts for Rangers in the summer

‘But I believe it will be a strong atmosphere for them. That was their choice. So now, of course, they have the consequences. And they knew that, I think, when they left.

‘But yeah, I think our focus needs to be on the game and not on the backstage.’

Leaders of the Premiership from the autumn until the closing moments of the campaign, McInnes’ Hearts side came as close as anyone to breaking the Old Firm’s stranglehold on the big prize since Aberdeen in 1985.

As a result of sharing a campaign which still contained far more highs than lows, Braga insists McInnes is still held in high esteem by the squad he left behind.

‘Personally, I really like Derek,’ said the forward. ‘I have nothing bad to say about him, Shanks or Cammy. I shared one amazing year with them and really love them as people. So, of course, it will be good to face them, but I have nothing but love for them.

‘I feel like we had such a wonderful year, even though, of course, it ended the way it ended.

‘When it’s a bad year, maybe the relationships won’t be that strong. But a season like that, you just literally become a family. And, of course, the bonds are created. And I hope we do the same this year.’

It’s boom time for billionaires. In the next few months, the world could see a fresh crew of technology entrepreneurs join that club, and the numbers of multi-millionaires would swell even more.

How a new wave of science philanthropists can have the greatest impact

How a new wave of science philanthropists can have the greatest impact

Two giants of artificial intelligence, OpenAI and Anthropic, plan to go public in the next year or so, floating stocks that are expected to rocket some of their employees into the ranks of the ultra-wealthy. Some analysts have projected that Anthropic’s initial public offering (IPO) might bring in US$2 trillion, the largest in history. OpenAI is reportedly targeting $1.4 trillion for its IPO, although the company delayed going public until 2027 amid renewed concerns about AI safety.

If these IPOs go as planned, the effects might extend well beyond sales of superyachts, mansions and private jets. The windfall would probably also add to the flurry of private investments and philanthropic donations from tech-savvy entrepreneurs who are eager to jump-start their favoured areas of biomedical research, such as cancer biology, longevity and reproductive technologies. “It’s a very exciting time,” says Andrew Lo, who studies entrepreneurship at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Cambridge. “We’re going to see the creative energy of the tech sector unlocked.”

Wealthy technology entrepreneurs have long put their cash and creative energy into medical research. And as their fortunes have grown, so has the scale of their investments, and the variety of ways in which their funds are doled out. One major player is Altos Labs, a longevity-focused company in Redwood City, California, which launched in 2022 with $3 billion in funds raised from tech titans Yuri Milner and Jeff Bezos, among others — an unprecedented sum for a fledgling biotechnology company with no drug candidates. The Chan Zuckerberg Initiative (CZI) , founded by Facebook co-founder Mark Zuckerberg and his wife, physician Priscilla Chan, has spent about $4 billion on basic scientific research and other causes, and plans to spend up to $10 billion more over the next decade.

Ultra-wealthy research investors follow a different strategy than do government funding agencies such as the US National Institutes of Health (NIH). Organizations such as the CZI and Altos Labs boast a commitment to high-risk, long-term research. Tech entrepreneurs are also often keen to invest in new technology, and to harness the power of AI for biomedicine. In April, for example, the CZI announced that it would spend $500 million on funding predictive AI models of human cells. Last year, OpenAI Foundation, the non-profit arm of OpenAI in San Francisco, California, pledged to spend $25 billion on health, disease cures and AI resilience. The foundation has announced more than $100 million for Alzheimer’s disease research and another $125 million for life-sciences data projects this year.

But this investment comes at a time when the public is suspicious of the tech elite and its political influence. It is difficult to assess the success of philanthropic programmes, and many are geared towards encouraging the use of the same technology the philanthropists profited from developing. Meanwhile, high-profile private investment in firms that study longevity or controversial reproductive technologies, such as gene editing in human embryos, raises questions about whether tech entrepreneurs will skew research towards applications that benefit the wealthy, rather than helping a broader swathe of society. “In philanthropy, no one gets elected,” says Rob Reich, a political scientist at Stanford University in California. “There’s no way of holding them accountable.”

US philanthropists have a long history of influencing what research gets done, and how. In the early twentieth century, grants from industrialists such as Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller helped to transform higher education and solidify the role of US universities in scientific research. Their giving focused on ensuring that the institutions they supported succeeded; today’s philanthropists are more likely to seek a particular outcome rather than support the long-term health of a university, says Eric John Abrahamson, a historian at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, Maryland. In 2024, non-profit organizations, including philanthropies, supported an estimated $25 billion in research and development — about 11% of total research funding in the United States, according to the Science Philanthropy Alliance (see ‘Support sources’).

It is difficult to track all donations from technology magnates comprehensively, but individual examples show that their influence has been sizeable. In 2000, Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates established the Gates Foundation (formerly called the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation), which made massive investments in vaccine development and the study of infectious disease, among other fields. It remains an influential behemoth: last year, Gates announced that he would donate about US$200 billion over the next two decades.

Other large tech donors include Sergey Brin, a co-founder of Google, who has donated more than $1 billion to Parkinson’s disease research. Sean Parker, an early executive at Facebook, has given hundreds of millions to cancer-immunotherapy research. In 2021, several philanthropists, including tech entrepreneurs, donated $650 million to found the Arc Institute in Palo Alto, California, in the heart of Silicon Valley, to understand the root causes of complex diseases. And US software magnate Larry Ellison announced that he will spend billions of pounds over ten years on a new campus near the University of Oxford, UK. The campus will focus on health care, as well as agriculture, climate change and AI.

In addition to charitable donations, tech entrepreneurs have been fuelling biomedical innovation through private-sector investments, such as funding Altos Labs. Another company, Calico Life Sciences, was founded in 2013 with backing from Google. With a focus on combating ageing and curing disease, Calico, in South San Francisco, California, was conceived as a long-term ‘moonshot’ programme. Sam Altman, a co-founder and chief executive of OpenAI, has invested about $180 million in Retro Biosciences, another longevity-focused company, in Redwood City, California.

In some cases, the line between for-profit investments and philanthropic donations are becoming more blurred, says Louis Shekhtman, a network scientist at Bar-Ilan University in Ramat Gan, Israel. The CZI, for example, was set up as a limited liability company (LLC), rather than as a tax-exempt foundation. Doing so comes at a cost: depending on how they are constructed, LLCs might not be eligible for the tax breaks that more-conventional charities enjoy. But an LLC can be more flexible in how it spends its money, and has fewer requirements to publicly disclose its activities, says Joan Heminway, a business-law scholar at the University of Tennessee in Knoxville.

In a 2015 statement shortly after the launch of the CZI, Zuckerberg said the LLC structure would give the initiative “the flexibility to give to the organizations that do the best work — regardless of how they’re structured”. He also said that any net profits from CZI investments would be put towards furthering its philanthropic mission.

Favourable US tax policies and a high concentration of billionaires — many of them in the technology industry — mean that the United States is unmatched among wealthy nations for charitable giving, says Shekhtman. It is also a major hub for biomedical research, and home to nearly half of the world’s biopharmaceutical companies.

And US tech entrepreneurs have a penchant for experimentation in how they structure their investments, says Keith Yamamoto, vice-chancellor of science policy and strategy at the University of California, San Francisco. When it comes to philanthropy, private donors are more able to take risks with their investments than are government funding agencies, which are beholden to taxpayers to ensure that their money is spent wisely. “Public money limits your activities,” says Harold Varmus, a former director of the NIH, who has also been on advisory boards for the Gates Foundation and the CZI. “There is a freedom of motion that is associated with private money.”

Some tech titans have experimented with various ways of structuring their grants. The Arc Institute, for example, boasts a “no-strings-attached” funding system that gives its investigators more freedom than do standard government grants awarded to biomedical researchers.

Philanthropists are also able to fund long-term, interdisciplinary projects that can be difficult for government grants to take on. Convergent Research, a non-profit organization funded in part by Eric Schmidt, the former chief executive of Google, is forming ‘focused research organizations’ that bring teams together to solve important scientific or engineering challenges that could benefit a wide swathe of research. “We’re looking for the pieces of critical, fundamental infrastructure missing from a particular field,” says Anastasia Gamick, a co-founder and president of Convergent Research, which is headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts.

Projects include developing cheap ways to map the neurons in mammalian brains, and large-scale screening to catalogue the off-target effects of small-molecule drugs.

Meanwhile, Yamamoto points to the CZI’s Institute for Advanced Biological Imaging as another example of a long-term, high-risk project that aims to develop pivotal technology. He hopes that the team there — which has brought engineers, physicists and computer scientists together with biologists — will succeed in refining a difficult technique called cryo-electron tomography so that it can image processes in a cell at the level of individual proteins.

Tolerance for risk is also evident in some tech entrepreneurs’ investments in for-profit companies. Some investors hope to reshape how fundamental research is conducted. Arcadia Science in Emeryville, California, for example, was launched with backing from Altman and from Jed McCaleb, a cryptocurrency entrepreneur in the United States, and pledges to “rethink the entire research cycle” by branching out from the usual laboratory model systems, such as mice and the bacterium Escherichia coli, to study more diverse organisms. Like Calico Life Sciences, the company warned investors from the start: this will take time.

Arcadia has also taken an unusual step for a for-profit company and embraced open science to a degree not typically found in industry, says Yamamoto. Its lab protocols, computer code and data sets are published directly online.

Silicon Valley investors are also sometimes willing to wade into controversial territory. Last year, Brian Armstrong, chief executive of the cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase, helped to fund a company dedicated to developing methods to edit the genome in human embryos to prevent disease. The company, named Preventive and based in San Francisco, will first have to grapple with the fundamental science of heritable gene editing to learn whether and how it can be done safely, says Lucas Harrington, a co-founder of Preventive. Private investment will enable the company to do that research, which US legal restrictions prohibit government grants from funding.

#1371. What would Francis of Assisi make of his fame today?

The Catholic saint renounced wealth and fame to become a mendicant friar. Yet throughout history, his teachings and life have inspired popes, Dante and even Mussolini.

One could safely say that Francis of Assisi's life — and afterlife — is defined by paradox.

He embraced poverty, yet lies entombed today in a grand basilica in his native Assisi, Italy, with frescoes by renowned medieval artists.

He advocated meekness, yet hundreds of thousands venerated his remains when they went on public display earlier this year to commemorate 800 years since his death on October 3, 1226.

So, why has this Catholic saint inspired papal encyclicals, ecological activism, global pet‑blessing rituals and even the propaganda of former Italian fascist leader Benito Mussolini? And what might Francis make of this fame that he'd shunned while alive?

Born in Assisi, a hill town in central Italy, in 1181 or 1182, he was first named Giovanni di Pietro di Bernardone. His father, a cloth merchant with trade links to France, renamed him Francesco.

Early Franciscan hagiographers, that is medieval writers who recorded his life and shaped how later generations understood him, describe him as a lively, sociable young man who enjoyed singing, parties and the privileges of wealth.

However, he didn't want to join his father's trade; he dreamed of becoming a knight.

In 1202, Francis joined Assisi's forces in a battle against neighboring Perugia. He was captured, spent a year in prison and returned home shaken and ill. This, and a series of subsequent events, precipitated a slow shift in his outlook.

One occurred at the small, ruined Church of San Damiano outside Assisi, where Francis believed he heard Jesus Christ calling on him to "rebuild my church." Taking it literally, he began repairing local chapels. When he ran out of money, he sold cloth from his father's shop, which enraged the latter and led to a dramatic episode in Assisi's town square.

Before the bishop and gathered townspeople, Francis stripped off his fine clothes, renouncing his father, his inheritance and all material comforts. He committed himself to a life of poverty, humility, care for the poor and sick and a deep kinship with nature.

As a wandering preacher, he drew followers and eventually founded the Order of Friars Minor, known today as the Franciscans.

Widely considered the first saint to have miraculously received the stigmata — physical wounds resembling those on the crucified body of Jesus Christ — Francis was canonized by Pope Gregory IX on July 16, 1228. This unusually swift recognition amplified his fame beyond Assisi.

In the following centuries, Francis became one of the most frequently depicted saints in Western art, inspiring works from Dante's "Divine Comedy" to films, children's books and even a 1980 Marvel comic.

"Most saints are seen as unapproachable or unrelatable, but Francis' story of lifelong conversion and the desire to be in a positive relationship with others, God and the rest of creation speaks to people across time and cultures," theologian and former Franciscan friar Daniel P. Horan tells DW.

According to cultural historian Amanda Minervini, this broad appeal is also what made Francis so adaptable across eras.

"In Francis, there seems to be something for everyone: the pacifist, the ascetic, the mason, the environmentalist, the minimalist, the lover of animals also loved by animals, the diplomat, the mystic, the radical, the performer, and even the model of a different kind of masculinity," they tell DW.

Minervini points out that his image has been reconstructed throughout history. Their research highlights one particularly jarring example: Francis' political appropriation by Benito Mussolini's fascist regime.

In their recent book, "St. Francis and Mussolini: Fascist Appropriations of a Beloved Italian Saint" (2026), they explain how fascist rhetoric manipulated Francis' stigmata, reframing them as symbols of heroic suffering and sacrifice. "The wounds of the saint were thus invoked to glorify sacrifice and inspire Italian youth to go to war."

Noting how Mussolini chose to be symbolically baptized in 1927 to strengthen his alliance with the Vatican, they add, "St. Francis offered a particularly useful means of conferring religious legitimacy upon political power."

Minervini's research shows that Francis' pacifism, "likely because he had known what war meant," was deliberately played down. Their book reproduces a confidential note addressed to military chaplains in 1929, which expressed concern that they might preach ideals of peace to the troops.

"This document provides clear evidence that peace was an aspect of Francesco's legacy that fascist groups sought to suppress rather than promote."

Furthermore, after Italy — a former Axis power along with Nazi Germany — switched sides and joined the Allies during World War II, the latter were often willing to condone or overlook Italy's crimes rather than investigate its fascist past.

"In this climate, many fascists remained in place, and the politically compromised image of Francis was quietly set aside," explains Minervini.

Francis has, however, remained associated with peace-making.

In 1219, during the Fifth Crusade, he traveled with the crusading army to Damietta, a port city in northern Egypt and obtained permission to cross into the Muslim camp and meet personally with the sultan, Malik al-Kamil. He aimed to convert the sultan and end the bloody conflict.

Describing his action as "an extraordinarily audacious thing to do," Minervini says the encounter did not produce the conversion or political breakthrough Francis may have hoped for, but his unscathed return ensured early sources took notice of him.

Horan, the former Franciscan friar, adds that despite Christians viewing Muslims as the enemy back then, St. Francis "would not go along with that dehumanizing perspective" and instead celebrated "the shared humanity and brotherhood he recognized in the Sultan."

But he is most widely known as an animal lover and an early figure of environmental consciousness.

"He was ahead of his time both theologically and in terms of modern natural sciences, recognizing the complexity of the cosmos and the fact that human beings are also creatures in relationship with the world around us," says Horan, author of "Francis of Assisi and the Future of Faith" (2012).

In 1979, Pope John Paul II declared Francis the patron saint of ecology, while the Argentinian Jorge Mario Bergoglio chose the saint as his papal namesake when he became Pope Francis in 2013.

This pope would later publish "Laudato Si'" in 2015 — based on the saint's 1225 hymn "Canticle of the Creatures," in which he addresses natural elements as "Brother Sun," "Sister Moon" and "Sister Mother Earth."

The pope's encyclical connected environmental destruction directly to human suffering, besides critiquing consumerism and the "throwaway culture," and urging humanity to undergo an "ecological conversion."

"I also believe that in a time of deep polarization and economic inequality that St Francis models another way of being in the world, one that prioritizes relationship and community over individual gain and power," says Horan.

"It's always tricky to try and guess what St. Francis, who died 800 years ago this October, would think about anything today. Our world is so unlike his own. #1324. Spain grows fruit and vegetables for Europe, but it's running out of water

Spain's southern coast produces up to 25% of Europe's fruit and vegetables. But amid intensive farming, climate change and illegal wells, the water it takes to grow them is drying up.

Donana National Park in southwestern Spain — one of Europe's most important wetlands, home to millions of migratory birds and threatened species like the Iberian lynx — is running dry. But rising temperatures and drought caused by climate change are only part of the problem.

The UNESCO-protected region on the Atlantic coast relies on an underground aquifer that's been tapped by hundreds of illegal wells. The reason: Europe's fruit and vegetable supply. Spain provides one-quarter of Europe's fresh produce, supplying colder parts of the continent with fruit and vegetables year-round.

The scale of water extraction in Spain is staggering: In 2016, WWF Spain estimated that the Donana wetland was home to over 1,000 illegal wells and more than 1,700 ponds illegally irrigating an area as big as 4,200 football fields.

"It's gotten hotter every year. And the water dries up so quickly," one local resident in the town of El Rocio, just north of the wetlands, told DW.

"For me, day to day, the main issue is my animals," said another, hitching his horse up to a tourist carriage. "I have to draw water for them, but the well doesn't have enough water to provide it all at once."

The Donana area used to be royal hunting grounds, consisting of marshes and ponds. In the early 20th century, parts began to be used for dryland farming, agriculture in arid regions that relies heavily on rainwater.

But in 1971, Spain — still under the dictatorship of General Francisco Franco — declared the region's irrigation to be of national interest. The Almonte-Marismas Plan, named after the aquifer it tapped into, was part of a massive, state-sponsored effort to increase food production.

Authorities decided to bet on strawberries, which they called "red gold." According to calculations based on FAO data, strawberries can yield up to €150,000 (about $170,000) in gross revenue per hectare per year, compared to roughly €20,000 for potatoes or €15,000 for grapes.

But growing strawberries at scale also meant using a lot more water. Authorities handed out generous quotas and allotments, seemingly without considering how much extraction the aquifer could handle. Even those legal quotas weren't enough for the ever-expanding fields.

Farmers turned to illegal wells and reservoirs to make up the difference. They built them to stockpile water, draining supplies from local residents and the UNESCO World Heritage Site next door.

"There was extreme drought and lack of attention from the administration," said Pedro Banez, of the Condado Water Users' Association. "And we found ourselves with a number of farms — or parts of farms — that fell outside the land classified as irrigable agricultural land."

The turning point came in 2019 when, following numerous reports of unlawful wells, a 2-year-old fell into deep, reportedly illegal borehole on farmland near Malaga and died. In the aftermath, Spain's Civil Guard police force created dedicated units focused on water theft.

Two years after that, in 2021, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that Spain had failed to protect the Donana aquifer, finding the country in violation of EU law.

Today, police and NGOs like WWF Spain track illegal extraction — near Donana, and other overtaxed agricultural regions across the country — using a mix of old and new tools. They start with satellite images of the region, comparing them against land rights maps to flag areas that appear greener than their legal status allows. Drones follow, giving investigators a closer look at where the water is actually coming from.

Some wells are completely illegal. Others are legal but extract more than they're allowed, which requires closer, on-site inspection to prove.

Investigators check the flow meters that measure how much water a well is pulling. Tampering is common, often using a powerful magnet which disrupts the internal mechanism of the flow meter, allowing water to pass through unrecorded.

Farmers caught extracting illegally can be charged with crimes against the environment and fraud. Most face fines, but sentences can run up to five years in prison.

In a bid to undo decades of overuse, local and national authorities have negotiated a €1.4 billion joint plan. The government has continued shutting down illegal wells, sealing almost 300 in the Donana region alone.

Officials have also started buying up farmland, rewilding it and turning it into protected areas, while setting up funds to create jobs in other sectors like tourism and renewable energy.

Farmers, meanwhile, have had to change how they water their crops.

Many have switched to drip irrigation systems, where each plant gets its own individual dripper, paired with moisture sensors that report exactly how much water a plant needs. That cuts down on the overuse of both water and fertilizer.

Farmers have also begun planting less water-intensive crops and shifting their growing season to colder months.

Spain isn't alone in confronting this problem. As economies mature, fruit and vegetable production starts being outsourced to places with cheaper labor, fewer restrictions and more sun.

Mexico, Brazil and Colombia grow food for parts of the US, while countries in southeast Asia export a lot of produce to China or Japan. Some countries in northern Africa, including Morocco and Egypt, also supply fresh fruits and vegetables to their European neighbors to the north. But, as in Spain, intensive agriculture has strained local water supplies.

And in California, the US state has paid farmers to halt certain types of irrigation and introduced legislation to monitor and allocate water more sustainably.
