#2312. EFL rivals tracking Portsmouth boss John Mousinho after summer interview

Portsmouth have risen up the Championship table with an impressive run of form, taking 10 points from a possible 12 in a four-game unbeaten streak. It has marked the perfect response to a disappointing derby day loss to Southampton, after which Pompey boss John Mousinho faced strong criticism from supporters.

However, Mousinho remains held in high regard at Fratton Park, and their 11th-place standing is not going unnoticed elsewhere. TalkSPORT states that the 40-year-old is being monitored by ‘bigger’ clubs in the EFL as many start to consider changes.

Mousinho was interviewed by Bristol City in the summer, but they hired Michael Skubala from Lincoln City instead. The boss signed a long-term contract extension at Fratton Park in 2024, but it is not known when that deal expires.

As a coach, Mousinho was a complete unknown when he took charge of Portsmouth in January 2023. He had been working as a player-coach with Oxford United, but with limited experience and no managerial past for reference, no one really knew what to expect.

The former defender would become a quick favourite at Fratton Park, turning Pompey from League One’s nearly-men to division champions. Mousinho then kept them up in the Championship against the odds, and he led another successful survival fight in the 2025/26 campaign.

Mousinho proved his coaching abilities by successful shifting from a possession-heavy setup to a more pragmatic one to stay in the Championship. Portsmouth have averaged the 8th-highest average possession this season though (52.5% – Fotmob), shifting more towards the boss’ original play style, and their league standing shows it has been successful.

The goal is to progress up the Championship table now, and Mousinho is delivering once again. The boss has met or exceeded all goals and expectations at Fratton Park, justifying wider interest in his services.

This is not the first time Mousinho has drawn interest, and it won’t be the last. Portsmouth managed to hold onto their boss in the summer, but accepting the interview with Bristol City showed he is not opposed to the prospect of moving on from Fratton Park.

Pompey have to make sure their trajectory keeps matching that of Mousinho. If not, opportunities at more ambitious and upwardly-mobile clubs will be tempting.

It remains to be seen if this renewed interest develops into anything more serious. For now, Mousinho will be fully focused on the task at hand with Portsmouth, but that may change if offers start to arise.

By CALUM CROWE, SCOTTISH DEPUTY CHIEF SPORTS WRITER

Published: 20:46 GMT, 22 October 2026 | Updated: 13:36 GMT, 23 October 2026

If there were any suspicions that Mo Salah might be past his best and over the hill after leaving Liverpool to join Trabzonspor in the summer, here was a reminder that class is permanent.

In this UEFA Conference League victory over a Hearts side ravaged by injuries, Salah scored the first from the penalty spot before teeing up the second with an assist for strike partner Franculino.

Salah may not quite be the explosive force of old who lit it up during a glittering nine-year stint at Anfield, but he is still a player of serious pedigree.

When he joined Trabzonspor a couple of months ago, thousands of fans mobbed him at the airport before making their way to the stadium for his unveiling.

In this fanatical hotbed of Turkish football, the Egyptian King reigned supreme against Hearts and was worshipped by an adoring public when he took his leave from the field midway through the second half.

For Hearts, it was in some ways quite a strange night. They did not play poorly. Without wishing to glorify defeat or patronise them, there were positives to be taken.

Mo Salah celebrates in muted fashion after handing Trabzonspor the lead from the penalty spot

The performance was vastly improved on what they delivered against Celtic last weekend when they suffered a 7-1 mauling in Glasgow.

There were fears that a similar trouncing in Trabzon could be on the cards for Wouter Vrancken’s side but they emerged with credit and respectability intact.

The main problem for Vrancken is now an ever-lengthening injury list. Harry Milne suffered a knee injury in training on Wednesday night and could be set for a lengthy spell on the sidelines.

Vrancken already had several key players out of action. Indeed, Hearts have enough players injured at the moment to fill a small hospital wing.

On the back of taking a hammering at Parkhead last weekend, the Belgian made four changes to his Hearts team for this trip to Turkey. It was a case of dealing with an injury crisis which seems to worsen by the week, whilst also preserving others such as Stephen Kingsley and Laurent Mendy who were carrying slight issues.

Josh McPake and Oisin McEntee also dropped to the bench, while in came Christian Borchgrevink, Tomas Magnusson – effectively the only fit central midfielder – Rogers Mato and James Wilson in what was a depleted and makeshift XI in a lot of positions.

Especially in the case of Mato. Signed as a forward player who can play out wide, he was asked to play as an emergency left-back last night – and his direct opponent was Salah.

Former Liverpool star Salah was a constant menace for a Hearts side riddled by injury

Jordi Altena, normally a right-back, was also asked to play in central midfield alongside Magnusson. In a stadium famed for its noise and hostility, this always looked like a tough assignment, all the more so with a lot of square pegs in round holes.

But credit to Hearts. They started the match fairly well and did not appear to be suffering from any sort of hangover after such an appalling display last Sunday.

They should have taken the lead after only six minutes when Malachi Fagan-Walcott rose to meet a Calvin Miller corner. The Englishman leapt with power and authority, but his header came back off the post when he really ought to have scored.

Even so early in the match, it was a huge chance. Especially against this calibre of opposition, Hearts could ill afford to pass up gilt-edged opportunities.

Hearts were pressing the game far better than they did against Celtic. There was more cohesion to what they were doing, more intelligence in how they went about it. But they were undone by a clumsy foul by Magnusson on the half-hour when he tangled with Trabzonspor striker Franculino inside the box.

Belgian referee Lawrence Visser, who has some history with Vrancken back in their homeland, pointed to the spot and would also book the Hearts boss shortly after for some protestations.

Salah stepped up and calmly slotted the penalty straight down the middle past Hearts keeper Beau Reus. In the stands, it was clear that the Trabzonspor fans have a new hero.

The referee was proving to be fussy and card-happy, dishing out five bookings in total across the first half. There was also a very strong claim that Hearts should have had a penalty on the cusp of half-time.

Trabzonspor striker Franculino celebrates after scoring the second following a Salah one-two

Claudio Braga tangled with Trabzonspor right-back Melih Kabasakal and it did appear like there was contact, yet Braga did not make any great appeal for it to be given.

Already down to the bare bones in midfield and in defence, Hearts saw another body head for the treatment room when Borchgrevink pulled up with what appeared to be a hamstring strain, with Kingsley replacing him.

Trabzonspor went close to making it 2-0 when a clever dummy from Salah teed up Benjamin Bouchouari, but his shot was well saved by Reus.

For all Vrancken’s side trailed 1-0 at half-time, they were by no means out of the game and had enjoyed some really tidy spells of possession.

James Wilson linked well with Braga up front. Out wide, Miller also looked capable of causing problems. But any hopes of a fightback were scuppered ten minutes after the restart when the combination of Salah and Franculino sparked into life in devastating fashion.

They played a clever one-two on the edge of the box, with Salah’s lay-off allowing Franculino to steer a brilliant left-foot finish into the top corner past Reus.

It was a really classy finish from the Guinea-Bissau forward, who joined Trabzonspor for a fee of around £15million from Midtjylland at the start of the season.

It was now difficult to see a way back for Hearts. There was no denying the performance had been much-improved on what they delivered at Celtic Park. Yet, as the game approached the hour-mark, and for all their spells of possession, the reality was that Hearts hadn’t tested Trabzonspor goalkeeper Andre Onana anywhere near often enough.

The game fizzled out thereafter. Hearts looked leggy and like a team who are now reeling from the loss of so many key players to injuries.

Trabzonspor had Sidney Cabral sent off late on but the game was long since won at that point

Vrancken could ask no more from them. Especially on the back of what happened last weekend, this was a performance which could at least be passed as credible and competitive.

The Hearts boss did not have a huge amount of options on the bench to change things and was probably of a mind to try and save some legs, with a game away at Motherwell to come on Sunday.

Trabzonspor were reduced to ten men in stoppage-time when Sidny Cabral was sent off for a late challenge on Sabah Kerjota, but there was no time – or energy – left for Hearts to try and take advantage.

#2307. Outworked, outfought and outthought: Tactical analysis of Bournemouth 3-0 AC Milan

AC Milan suffered yet more Europa League misery on Thursday night as they were beaten 3-0 by AFC Bournemouth away from home.

It took just a few minutes for Koni De Winter to block a shot with his hand, prompting a VAR check and a penalty awarded that Justin Kluivert converted. Then, Mike Maignan had a moment of madness as he shoved Tyler Adams in the face from a corner to draw a red card and a second spot kick.

Marcus Tavernier despatched that one to give Milan a mountain to climb, which did not happen before a third goal via a wonderful free-kick from Amine Adli. Below is Rohit Rajeev‘s tactical breakdown from a night to forget in the rain on the south coast.

Ruben Amorim made six changes to the starting line-up from the team that beat Atalanta at the weekend. Fikayo Tomori, Pervis Estupinan, Ardon Jashari, Omari Hutchinson, Ruben Loftus-Cheek and Alphadjo Cisse coming into the side.

Bournemouth made their intentions clear from the opening minutes, opting for an aggressive high press against Milan.

Evanilson led the press by closing down Maignan, while the other three attackers used cover shadows to block passing lanes and cut off Milan’s build-up options.

The pass towards Milan’s left side acted as Bournemouth’s pressing trigger. As soon as the ball reached Pavlovic or Estupiñán, Bournemouth players would abandon their markers and aggressively close down the ball carrier, applying immediate physical pressure to disrupt the build-up.

Milan also attempted to press Bournemouth, but with considerably less intensity. They preferred to maintain a mid-block, prioritising the coverage of passing lanes while one player, usually Cissé, stepped out to press the ball carrier.

However, what truly changed the game was Bournemouth’s counter-pressing rather than their initial press.

Immediately after Truffert lost possession, he recovered quickly to block the passing lane from De Winter to Moreira, while Evanilson closed down De Winter. Under pressure, De Winter surrendered possession, leading to a corner and eventually the penalty.

Even after taking the lead, Bournemouth showed no intention of easing off, continuing to apply an aggressive, physical press.

Operating in a 4-4-2, they narrowed their shape to create central overloads in midfield. As seen here, Loftus-Cheek was surrounded by four Bournemouth players, leaving him with very little room to turn or progress the ball.

.Another use was in field tilt by Bournemouth. They would create an overload on a particulat side of the field and attract Milan players toward the ball and this would create a field tilt which Bournemouth would try to play out of it.

Bournemouth clearly identified Moreira as Milan’s primary creative outlet and made sure to neutralise his influence.

They frequently used a 3v1 numerical advantage against him, leaving Moreira with little room to operate. He was often forced to surrender possession or recycle the ball backwards rather than progress play.

Milan went back to a 5-3-1 formation after the goal 2-0. Bournemouth also exploited Milan’s defensive organisation during the free kick. Their players positioned themselves in the gap between the two lines of Milan’s wall, obstructing Torriani’s line of sight and affecting his positioning.

As Pavlovic jumped slightly to his right, he created a gap between himself and Koni De Winter. That small opening proved crucial, allowing the ball to find its way into the net.

Milan also played into Bournemouth’s hands with their possession approach. They attempted to build slowly from the back, forming passing triangles to progress through the thirds.

However, Bournemouth’s aggressive pressing punished the slow circulation, allowing them to close down passing options, disrupt Milan’s rhythm and force turnovers.

The biggest difference between the two sides was their physical output. Bournemouth covered 110km compared to Milan’s 101km, highlighting the gap in intensity and work rate.

Amorim needs to improve the squad’s physical conditioning if Milan are to execute his pressing game effectively. Bournemouth’s PPDA of 6.82 meant they allowed Milan fewer than seven passes per defensive action on average, underlining the intensity of their pressing.

That got me wondering whether there’s any historical evidence that this level of tactical rigidity eventually pays off in the Premier League, particularly at clubs without enormous transfer budgets. So I asked AI for some help.

Looking back over the last 25 years, I struggled to find a convincing example of a manager who initially performed poorly, steadfastly persisted with essentially the same formation despite those problems, and eventually achieved at least two successful Premier League seasons without substantial financial backing.

Consider these past and present Premier League managers:

Jurgen Klopp: Stuck to his footballing philosophy but adapted his formations and tactics. Sustained success. Mikel Arteta: Maintained his principles but changed formation and tactics during difficult periods. Sustained success, albeit with considerable investment. Mauricio Pochettino: Consistent philosophy, flexible formations. Sustained success without the spending power of Manchester City or Chelsea. David Moyes: Adapted his tactics and formation to the players available. Sustained success relative to resources. Sean Dyche: Probably the closest example of sticking to a recognisable system on a limited budget, but even he made tactical adjustments. Sustained success relative to resources. Marcelo Bielsa: Relatively rigid tactical approach. Initially successful at Leeds but couldn’t sustain it into a second successful Premier League season. Ange Postecoglou: Remained strongly committed to his attacking philosophy despite deteriorating results. No sustained Premier League success. Ruben Amorim: Persisted largely with his preferred back-three system despite struggling. No sustained Premier League success.

The pattern among these examples is interesting. Managers who achieved sustained success generally retained their footballing principles but were prepared to change formations or tactics when necessary. Those who remained particularly rigid struggled to achieve comparable long-term results.

As another Newcastle United fan mentioned in their article over the weekend, whilst the Matthias Jaissle achievements are genuine, there is little evidence of him achieving sustained success at a club operating under comparable financial constraints to those Newcastle United face relative to their Premier League competitors. We don’t have the funds to buy the players to suit his system this season.

Surely part of the manager’s responsibility is to adapt his approach to the strengths and weaknesses of the players available?

I’m not suggesting Matthias Jaissle should abandon his footballing philosophy after a few poor results. Nor am I suggesting he’s made no tactical adjustments whatsoever. But there’s a fundamental difference between sticking to your principles and stubbornly sticking to a formation.

My concern is that, among the historical examples I’ve examined, there’s very little evidence of managers persisting with a system that is producing consistently poor performances, making no significant structural changes, and eventually enjoying sustained Premier League success without major financial advantages.

Perhaps Matthias Jaissle will prove to be the exception. But if our midfield continues to be overrun and the shot statistics remain so heavily against us, what evidence is there that simply persevering with the same basic structure will eventually make it work?

I accept that a manager needs time to establish his ideas. But surely that patience should be accompanied by evidence of progress, or at least a willingness to address obvious weaknesses.

For me, tactical flexibility isn’t a sign of weakness. It’s one of the qualities that separates successful managers from those who ultimately get the sack.

Indeed, Bayern Munich marksman Harry Kane is the bookies' favourite to be crowned England's first Ballon dOr winner since a fledgling Michael Owen in 2001, but if some of us had our way, he would miss out on this individual honour.

Here, the Sports Mole team selects who they think should be crowned the winner of the 2026 Ballon d'Or.

Not since Lionel Messi's 2019 triumph has the Ballon d'Or been awarded to a player who failed to win any of the World Cup, Euros, Copa America or Champions League, but I am fully expecting that streak to be snapped this time around.

While not quite matching Lionel Messi's astronomical 91 goals from the 2012 calendar year, Harry Kane racked up an outrageous 73 for club and country in the 2025-26 campaign, 61 of which came in Bayern colours.

Taking home a second European Golden Shoe and becoming the first English player in 98 years to hit a 60+ goal season - emulating the iconic Dixie Dean - Kane reaffirmed his status as the best goalscorer on the planet, even if his World Cup numbers did not suggest to.

Bayern's UCL failure, England's World Cup disappointment and Kane only finishing joint-fourth in the Mundial scoring charts will all work against him, but so will Lamine Yamal's underwhelming individual World Cup campaign and Kylian Mbappe's Champions League and La Liga-less season.

The World Cup has not come home for England in the 21st century, but the Ballon d'Or surely will for the leader of the Three Lions.

After more than a decade watching Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo dominate the football award landscape, the race for the Ballon d’Or has opened up in recent years and there is no undisputed frontrunner for the coveted 2026 prize.

However, Harry Kane stands out ahead of players including Lamine Yamal and Kylian Mbappe as someone who deserves recognition for his remarkable influence as Bayern Munich and England’s talisman.

While winning a World Cup or Champions League carries a lot of weight in the Ballon d’Or votes, I think the influence a player can have on a team should also be recognised for this prestigious accolade, and with someone like Kane, you just wonder whether Bayern and England would have been so successful without him and his goals? Probably not.

At 33 years of age, Kane is at the peak of his powers, scoring a whopping 54 goals for club and country this calendar year, helping Bayern win back-to-back Bundesliga titles and inspiring England to the World Cup semi-finals.

His leadership, match-winning reliability, footballing intelligence and ridiculously clinical goalscoring record at the highest level makes him a deserving winner – potentially the first Englishman since Michael Owen in 2001 to claim the golden trophy.

As impressive as Harry Kane, Kylian Mbappe and Lamine Yamal all were for club and country, I think Michael Olise should win the Ballon d’Or. He stepped up in nearly every game, big or small, for Bayern Munich and France and was rarely, if ever, shut down. Not only did he have a record-setting World Cup campaign for assists, but he was the catalyst for his team’s success, just as he was in Bayern’s marvellous campaigns in Europe and domestically.

I feel the number of goals you score often matters more than creativity in voting, but the difficulty of many of Olise’s passes to Kane and Mbappe was often extraordinary and should be taken into consideration. You can reasonably argue that neither of those players would have been as potent in front of goal without Olise’s hard work in providing them with the perfect service and placing the ball in the ideal position for them to score. Without Olise, I don’t see either of them being quite as strong.

I don’t know if he’ll get the votes because he doesn’t score as much, but I don’t think anyone in the world is as good on the ball, as precise in tight spaces, or as tough to dispossess as Olise. If you evaluate what he did for his two teams and how successful his team’s were, than it should be Olise winning this award. I don’t think either Bayern or France would have achieved as much without Olise on the pitch. If we're evaluating a player’s overall game, their impact on the team, and the trophies they've won, Olise ticks all those boxes and deserves the Ballon d’Or.

You could make a case for and against each of the perceived top three contenders, making this year’s award rather interesting. Lamine Yamal had a top season but did not pull up trees at the World Cup, and Barcelona could not go all the way in the Champions League, while Kylian Mbappe had an outstanding individual season, culminating in becoming the top goalscorer in World Cup history, but won no major title with club or country. Harry Kane had a storming season with Bayern and did okay to a point at the World Cup, but the Bundesliga is often looked down upon, potentially hurting his prospects. Nonetheless, yours truly has always had a soft spot for the Bayern forward and would want him to claim the award. Heart vs. head. Heart wins.

Despite having a less-than-exemplary World Cup, he did still contribute to Spain's victory despite rushing back from injury, and he was central to Barcelona's La Liga success. He is arguably the most naturally gifted footballer in the world at the minute and so it makes him the best currently playing.

The Bayern Munich marksman netted 73 goals in 65 appearances across all competitions for club and country last season while also winning a league and cup double.

He also captained England to a third-place finish at the World Cup with six goals in seven matches.

The 2026 Ballon d'Or feels like one of the most open races ever but I think Kane is the most deserving winner.

The Ballon d’Or has historically been awarded to players that have won one of the game’s biggest trophies in the selection period, but Harry Kane deserves to claim the prize without either the Champions League or World Cup to his name.

Lamine Yamal made headlines as he lifted the World Cup with Spain, though the reality is that the 19-year-old was largely ineffective in the summer, scoring just once during the 4-0 group victory over Saudi Arabia and failing to provide a single assist after heading into the tournament with an injury.

By contrast, Kane bagged six goals and created another for his teammates, despite falling short of the crown.

Yamal has a case for having made the best start to the 2026-27 campaign, but for the selection period, it is difficult to overlook Kane’s staggering 73 goals and eight assists in 65 matches - even if football is about more than just raw output.

For comparison, the Englishman found the back of the net 61 times for Bayern Munich last term, far beyond the 42 that Kylian Mbappe mustered for Real Madrid and 38 Erling Haaland converted for Manchester City, while eclipsing Yamal’s 24 strikes for Barcelona.

Had things turned out differently a few years ago, Derek McInnes could have been the manager who gave Josh Fowler his first big break in professional football.

Back in the summer of 2023, whilst in charge of Kilmarnock, McInnes took the young striker on a pre-season trip to Northern Ireland.

This was at a time when Fowler had just scored 49 goals in 78 games across two seasons with Beith Juniors.

Killie were not the only top-flight club keeping tabs on him, with Fowler also going on trial with Motherwell during a pre-season trip to the Netherlands.

In the end, neither club decided to take a punt on a young man who was still only 21 years old and who had been working as a joiner.

It could easily have been a sliding doors moment, the kind which sees a young player perhaps start to feel like their chance of making it as a professional has gone.

St Johnstone striker Josh Fowler is joint-top scorer in the Scottish Premiership

But Fowler chose a different route. He was a man in demand. He would eventually leave Beith to embark on spells with Gulf United in Dubai then St Albans Dinamo in Australia before heading back to Dubai City.

He returned home in 2025 for a spell at Queen’s Park which saw him score 19 goals in 25 matches and become top scorer in the Championship, before eventually signing for St Johnstone in January earlier this year.

The goals are now starting to flow in Perth as well. Fowler hit the winner in a 1-0 triumph over Aberdeen a fortnight ago and netted again in a 1-1 draw with Dundee United last week.

He has five goals in his last six matches. All in all, six goals in nine matches so far this season sees him arrive at Ibrox this afternoon to take on McInnes and Rangers as the league’s joint-top scorer.

It is quite a tale. A striker with electric pace plucked from obscurity further down the leagues, before eventually going on to score goals by the bucketload in the top flight, he could well be Scottish football’s answer to Jamie Vardy.

Reflecting on Fowler’s journey, Chris Irving, the club secretary at Beith for the past decade, said: ‘The first thing I would say about Josh is that he is a really humble guy. He doesn’t forget his roots.

Fowler's nomadic career thus far has taken him to Dubai and Australia before his move to Perth

‘Even now, he still keeps in touch and takes an interest in how Beith are doing. I think there is a lot to be said for that kind of humility.

‘I can vividly remember Josh arriving at Beith as a young boy a few years ago. To be honest, the signing kind of came about by chance as much as anything.

‘Josh was working as an apprentice joiner and his lecturer at Cardonald College was Danny Shearer. Danny was our club president at the time, so Josh just asked him if he could come down for a trial.

‘I think he trained for a couple of nights then his first game was a pre-season friendly against Lugar Boswell Thistle which we won 8-0.

‘Josh played out wide on the left wing in that game and I remember speaking to our manager Bryan Young, who seemed to be a bit unsure whether we should sign him.

‘I basically said to Bryan: “Listen, we’ve got to sign him before someone else does”. I think the fact he then went on to score nearly 50 goals over two seasons probably vindicated that decision.

‘From what I could gather, I think Killie and Motherwell both liked him when they had him on trial. Why they didn’t sign him, I don’t know. You would have to ask them.

On his return from a second spell in Dubai, Fowler immediately caught the eye at Queen's Park

‘But, just generally, I think there is maybe still a slight reluctance from Premiership clubs to take a chance on a player from lower down the leagues.

‘Some people still have this idea about the Juniors from 20, 30, 40 years ago that it’s all just long ball and kick-and-rush. But it’s not like that at all.

‘I’ve been involved with Beith for a long time now and I can tell you the overall quality and the level is far, far better than most people would give it credit for.

‘Josh is the perfect example of how you can find a hidden gem if you are willing to look down the leagues and take a chance on someone.

‘Listen, you never know, if he can maintain this form and finish the season as top scorer in the country, Sebastien Pocognoli could have a look at him for Scotland. I don’t see why not. What a story it would be.’

Given the way that Fowler has continually thrived every time he’s moved up the ladder to a higher level, it would be foolish to rule out a Scotland call-up.

Could Sebastien Pocognoli be tempted to cap Fowler's rapid rise with a Scotland call-up?

He is a striker with blistering pace, clever movement, and has an eye for goal. In case anyone hasn’t noticed, Scotland are not blessed with many strikers who can tick those boxes.

Since taking charge, Pocognoli has spoken of a desire to freshen the squad up and to try and bring through a newer generation of players.

Fowler is still only 24. All of his best years are ahead of him. There will be some, of course, who get sniffy about the idea of a St Johnstone player being called up to the Scotland squad. But Josh McPake would be a prime example of a player who has continued to blossom, having shone for Saints as well.

Conner McGlinchey was a close friend of Fowler’s during their time together at Beith and he believes it’s only a matter of time before clubs from England start taking notice.

‘My first impression of Josh was: “Wow! He is rapid”,’ said McGlinchey. ‘Don’t get me wrong, he was quite raw. But he was so quick and he was confident as well.

‘He was quite off-the-cuff with a lot of the things he did. In his first season, he was playing on instinct a lot of the time.

‘Then, in his second season, he went on to another level. In that second season he was easily the best striker in the league by a mile. He was lethal.

Fowler will lead the line for Simo Valakari's St Johnstone against Rangers at Ibrox this weekend

‘I was vice-captain at the time and me and a couple of boys used to car share with him. We knew there was interest in him.

‘Given the start he’s had to the season with St Johnstone, top scorer in the league, I would guarantee you there are clubs looking at him right now.

‘Especially if he goes and scores against Rangers at Ibrox this weekend, an English Championship club will come in for him in January. Clubs down south will always take a chance on someone who can score goals.’

Fowler has emerged as one of the feel-good stories in Scottish football over the past year or so.

A rough diamond plucked from the lower leagues, now thriving in the top flight.

His path has been anything but conventional. But he has proven he can cut it at the top level in Scotland. A young man who continues to take everything in his stride, it will be fascinating to see just how far he can go.

It is not a tale of football superstardom, but rather one of personal growth, hard lessons and a changing outlook on life.

One of the most interesting themes throughout the book is Carroll’s evolving attitude towards wealth and status. He reflects on how material possessions gradually became less important as he got older. His time in France, particularly at Bordeaux, appears to have reinforced that perspective. Far removed from the Premier League spotlight, he found himself playing football in modest surroundings and rediscovering the simple enjoyment of the game.

From a West Ham perspective there are also some revealing observations about the managers he worked under, including Sam Allardyce, Slaven Bilic, David Moyes and Manuel Pellegrini. His comments on Moyes’ tactical approach are particularly noteworthy, suggesting that the emphasis on structure sometimes made the team too predictable regardless of the opposition.

Having recently finished Michail Antonio’s autobiography, I was intrigued by Carroll’s assessment of both his former teammate and the London Stadium. Carroll describes it as lacking the intimacy and character of a traditional football ground, especially in its first season as West Ham’s home.

What emerges throughout the book is a picture of a player who has gained perspective. The perception of Carroll as merely a headline-generating footballer is challenged by a surprisingly reflective and self-aware account of his career.

He discussed mental health, and also reveals a disturbing incident that occurred to him, which the National press have since reported on.

Now 37, Carroll remains deeply involved in football at Dagenham & Redbridge as a player, assistant manager and minority shareholder. He appears to be relishing this latest chapter, embracing the game on his own terms while passing on his experience to others.

Owning It: In My Own Words is not a story of unfulfilled potential or regret. Instead, it is the account of a footballer who has made peace with the twists and turns of his career. Whether you admired him, criticised him or simply wondered what might have been, Carroll’s story is an engaging and honest read that reveals far more than the headlines ever did.

The scissor kick against Crystal Palace. The hat-trick against Arsenal in 2016. Those are the moments that define Andy Carroll’s West Ham career for me. He had the talent to become a club legend, but sadly injuries denied both him and the fans that storybook.

Bayern won the Bundesliga and German Cup but lost to Paris Saint-Germain in the Champions League semi-finals; a win would make Kane the first English recipient since Michael Owen in 2001.

Yamal won La Liga with Barcelona and the World Cup with Spain, playing all 120 minutes of the final at 19. He had 41 goal involvements for Barcelona, made up of 24 goals and 17 assists.

No La Liga player created more chances than his 118 in all competitions, and he would be the youngest winner in the award’s history should he waltz up to the stage later on.

Mbappe scored 58 goals in 60 appearances, topping the scoring charts in La Liga (25) and the Champions League (15). His 15 Champions League goals beat Kane’s 14, yet Real Madrid went out in the quarter-finals.

His 10 goals at the World Cup made him the tournament’s all-time leading scorer, totalling an unrivalled 22, but Real Madrid finishing the season without a trophy is the obvious obstacle to his vote count.
