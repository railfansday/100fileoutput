#4513. Games with loot boxes and other "paid random items" re-rated PEGI 16 as new rules come into force, hopefully cutting down on kids gambling somewhat

I'm strongly of the opinion that loot boxes, gacha mechanics and whatever other form of gambling remains present in games is a scourge that ought to be wiped out. Absolutely no game is better for having gacha mechanics in it, only worse. The normalisation of gambling through alternative, softer names, cool cosmetics, and in many cases, cute anime girls is honestly abhorrent. European ratings board PEGI doesn't seem keen on it either, as they've begun enacting recent rule changes bring the default age rating of games that contain these mechanics up to a minimum of PEGI 16.

This change is the enforcement of a plan first announced in March, intended to ensure that predatory mechanics like loot boxes are less accessible to players. Specifically, policy manager Connor Hinde notes that "Existing digital-only games that are currently rated PEGI 3, 7 or 12 and feature Paid random items, such as loot boxes, will have their age rating updated to PEGI 16 – Paid random items." It's unclear if PEGI are also applying other changes proposed back in March, such as such as games with 'time-limited' content (battle passes) being PEGI 12 by default, games with NFTs or 'blockchain-related mechanisms' (lol) gaining a PEGI 18 rating, and 'play-by-appointment' (games that deliberately induce FOMO as a monetisation tactic) also being rated at PEGI 12. Both Hinde's post and an update on PEGI's site specifically cover the "Paid random items" change.

This mass-reclassifying will take some time, but some rating changes have already been already made: PocketGamer.biz reports that Pokémon TCG Pocket is newly rated at 16, as has Fire Emblem Heroes (unique in the series for bearing a gacha mechanic). Hinde also states that "We recognise that a high volume of rating changes will take place over a relatively short period. Publishers and developers with any questions, queries or concerns regarding the process should contact the PEGI Helpdesk for further guidance."

PEGI's move follows the Belgian government having treated paid loot boxes as outright gambling since 2018, and the New York attorney general's 2026 suit against Valve to classify loot boxes as gambling, which Valve moved to dismiss, claiming "People enjoy surprises." The more pressure and, ideally, legislation against this the better, honestly.

Published Oct 27, 2026 12:57 PM EDT

Baby bears need to learn a lot during their first few summers—how to catch salmon, how to swim, how to climb trees, the importance of avoiding porcupine quills, etc. For one cub in Alaska’s Tongass National Forest, learning how to play hide & seek was added to that growing list of skills.

The United States Forest Service team at Tongass National Forest have awarded the official 2026 Worst at Hide-and-Seek Award to a baby bear who needs a bit more practice at this game. The baby bear belongs to Osso, who won the 2026 Mama Bear Award for her prowess.

“It [the cub] stole our Rangers’ hearts,” the team writes. “It looked like it wanted to play hide and seek but couldn’t fully grasp the concept. Our Rangers were glad the cub was bad at the game, because it meant they saw it more.”

Osso had three cubs this season. However, Rangers only spotted her with two cubs in August and believe that one died. Black bear cub survival rate can range from 51 to 88 percent depending on the location.

Tongass is the nation’s largest national forest and covers most of Southeast Alaska, including the capital city of Juneau. The area includes Mendanhall Glacier (a popular destination for Alaskan cruises), numerous hiking trails, eagles, and plenty of bears. One of the hotspots is Anan Creek, which is home to one of the largest runs of pink salmon in Southeast Alaska.

According to Friends of Anan, 39 known bears feast on the salmon at Anan Creek. The population includes 31 black bears (Ursus americanus) and eight brown bears (Ursus arctos). Like their counterparts further to the north in Katmai National Park, the bears return here every summer to eat salmon and teach their young how to fish and survive. And also like the bears of Katmai, every bear here has their own habits.

The U.S. Forest Service tracks and documents every bear and explore.org’s cameras broadcast live streams of their antics.

The pack slows down through their longest green-flag runs, and everyone saves fuel, shaving a few seconds off their pit stops as they jockey for position between the racing.

But the three RFK drivers had a different plan: once they had the lead, they floored it and started running true lap times at Talladega. Within ten laps, they had more than 30 seconds on the field; they extended it to as much as 45 seconds over the first green-flag run, meaning they were catching the back of the pack.

“Great game of poker where we just called the field,” Keselowski said over the No. 6 team radio. “Got to love it.”

The first pit cycle came in four waves, with drivers outside the playoffs like Connor Zilisch, Austin Dillon, and Shane van Gisbergen coming in first, followed by a larger pack of playoff Chevys and Fords.

The larger pack kept cutting into the lead, but nowhere near fast enough. Without a caution, the championship contenders fought for the best finish of fourth.

Kyle Larson, the championship leader, took the position ahead of William Byron, Riley Herbst, Chase Briscoe, Ryan Blaney, Joey Logano, and Tyler Reddick.

With this, Larson gets seven points, Briscoe four points, Blaney three points, and Reddick a single point. Denny Hamlin finished 15th, outside the points. Bell, with his stage-one trouble, finds himself in the hardest spot among the championship hopefuls, finishing off pace in 40th.

#4485. Why are bogs so good at preserving dead bodies?

Published Oct 27, 2026 3:43 PM EDT

In August of 1984, commercial peat cutters working near Wilmslow, about a half-hour drive south of Manchester, England, discovered a dead body in the moss. The corpse was determined to be a 25-year-old man with a closely shorn beard and well-manicured nails. He had suffered two or three heavy blows to the head, and had sustained a cut throat and a fractured rib. But this wasn’t a fresh corpse: His body had been laying in that peat bog for nearly 2,000 years.

The “Lindow Man” is just one of at least 2,000 bog bodies recovered from Europe’s peatlands. They date from the Mesolithic period, which ended in Europe between 8,500 to 5,500 years ago, all the way up through World War II.

“Bog bodies” are essentially human corpses that peat bogs have naturally preserved and mummified. Quite often, their skin, hair, nails, and internal organs are remarkably intact for thousands of years. Their bones, however, are a whole other story.

To find out more about why bogs are so good at preserving bodies, Popular Science turned to author Anna North, a journalist whose fascination with peat bogs and preserved “bog bodies” inspired her national bestseller Bog Queen. This eco-historical mystery novel details what happens when a forensic anthropologist starts investigating a well-preserved Iron Age bog body, and required extensive research on North’s part. The author first got the idea for her book after coming across the Lindow Man at London’s British Museum, where he’s on permanent display.

“He doesn’t look great,” she says, “but he’s just so recognizably still a human.” The fact that North was sharing a room with this person who ate, drank, and walked the earth 2,000 years ago left an indelible mark on her. She wanted to learn more. “One of the first things I realized is that I didn’t have a good sense of what a peat bog would even look like.”

A bog or “peat bog” is an acidic, nutrient-poor wetland ecosystem. It’s made up of peat, which is ground that’s soft and spongy and consists of partially decayed plant material that builds up over hundreds or thousands of years.

“Peat is really any kind of organic matter that builds up faster than it decays,” says North. It can be grasses, reeds, roots, and shrubs. But peat mostly consists of Sphagnum moss, a large group of shaggy-looking, moisture-loving mosses.

“Sphagnum moss has the capacity to take in a lot of water and store it, so it becomes really spongy and waterlogged.” This creates an excellent environment for preservation, says North, “because there’s not much air.”

While bogs exist across the globe, you’ll find them mostly in cool, northern climates with lots of rain. For years, these mysterious landscapes have appeared as ominous settings in literature. They’re especially popular in Victorian detective fiction and gothic novels like Sir Arthur Conan Doyle’s The Hounds of the Baskervilles and Daphne du Maurier’s Jamaica Inn. And for good reason. Their mist-shrouded surfaces (a result of the cool, damp air that sits above their spongy terrain), unstable footing, and stagnant waters make them natural backdrops for the unknown. Of course, there are also the bodies.

The preservation of bog bodies results from a highly-detailed balance of water source, chemistry, and vegetation.

Bogs get almost all of their water from rain and snow, which makes them low in minerals and nutrients. The water also doesn’t move thanks to the bog’s sponge-like moss.

Thick layers of decomposed organic material choke off the air supply to the decaying plant matter underneath. Without oxygen, the environment becomes highly acidic and aerobic bacteria and fungi are unable to thrive. This means they can’t break down that dead plant (and human) material the way they normally would.

Instead, the bog’s high acidity essentially “pickles” human corpses, many of which were left behind as part of a ritual sacrifice or the result of executions. The environment’s low pH (the result of its high acidity) acts like a pickle juice brine, stopping harmful bacteria from growing and preserving bodies almost like “living time capsules.” Sphagnum moss also releases a complex carbohydrate that binds to the proteins in human skin and tissue, called sphagnan. This carbohydrate turns the skin leathery and a dark-brown “tea” color while protecting it against decay. But while this unique environment slows a body’s decomposition, the bog’s intense acid leaches the calcium right out of human bones.

“If you look at X-rays of bog bodies, they’re not going to look like an X-ray of a living person,” says North. Mostly because the bones are softened, warped, or have dissolved completely.

Raised bogs, which are deep, dome-shaped peatlands that are both wetter and higher than their surrounding lands, are best at preserving bodies. That’s because they’re both highly acidic and entirely void of oxygen.

The shallower peat found in blanket bogs means that bodies decompose faster (though bog bodies are still found here). Fens, which are peat-accumulating wetlands that both  surface water and mineral-rich groundwater replenish, are less acidic. This means that a body’s soft-tissue will typically decay.

One way that forensic anthropologists can typically tell the difference between a recently decreased person and a bog body is through their teeth. While researching for her book, North discovered that people who lived and died before there was refined flour would have really different patterns of tooth decay than modern-day eaters.

Back then, she says, “They might actually grind wheat or corn with stone, and little bits of stone would end up in their flour. Those people would then chew on that stone and it would grind down their teeth.”

North also learned that identifying a bog body is a collaborative effort. A bioarchaeologist, who studies human biological remains at archaeological sites, might be brought in to determine the corpse’s age and sex, while a pathologist would examine the cause of death.

“It turns out that the same process that makes bogs really good at preserving a body also means that they’re storing this enormous amount of carbon,” says North.

Since there’s no oxygen to break down a bog’s organic plant matter, it just piles up layer by layer forming thick, carbon-rich peat. In fact, peatlands (including bogs) only cover roughly three-perfect of the planet’s land surface, though they store twice as much carbon as all the world’s forests combined. “They’re just a really powerful resource.”

Bogs are generally safe to walk across as well, despite their reputation for being “graveyards” of sorts. “There are cases of people appearing to have accidentally died on bogs,” says North. “Still, if you’re an average person visiting a bog today and you stay on the marked trail, I am not concerned for your safety.”

I could have bought a service manual, but that would have taken too much time and effort. So, I looked up a YouTube video. I blame the increasingly high number of materials needed to craft higher-level weapons and armor, and the multiple station types that could have been combined into one. As you get stronger, the number of materials you need for your gear increases, too. For example, my gear now needs Fine Cloth woven from threads and gold bars. By the time I reached the halfway point of the game, I had separate stations for tools and weapons, armor, jewelry, bows and arrows, and magical weapons like wands.

Classes don’t really exist in Dragonwilds. Instead of explicitly choosing a class like in a traditional RPG, you swap between roles by switching your gear – like swapping from armor to robes to switch from melee to magic. Changing classes felt pointless because I could force my way through most challenges with a sword, and bows and wands are less effective given the circumstances. Many confrontations involve flanking from multiple sides, so long-range combat isn’t ideal. Magic isn’t bad, but not many creatures have magical weaknesses and melee can easily do more damage with attack speed and buffs.

Dragonwilds is massive. Just the first arc took 20 to 30 hours to finish before I slew my first dragon. Fellhollow, the second arc, took about the same time to complete its story quests, and it didn’t make me any more excited to reach Dowdun Reach because I adapted so quickly to expectations. At the start of each region, I expect to grind for materials for three to five hours before I could expect to survive an “expedition” from my base, let alone bungling around uncharted territory to complete a quest with vague instructions. Thankfully, this steep on-ramp can be smoothed over through a setting that eases the experience grind, but the base way to play encourages the tedium to set in. To its credit, Dragonwilds spices up exploration in each region with gimmicks like “soulrifting” in Fellholllow, which unlocks alternate planes and invisible bridges.

Ideally, you avoid dying in any game, but Dragonwilds twists the knife as much as a Soulslike; when your health depletes, you drop your entire inventory and teleport back to the last place you camped. To avoid the inconvenience of refilling your bag, it’s easier to run back to the gravestone to retrieve your inventory. I’m grateful that I don’t just lose all the items, and that Dragonwilds spawns gravestones outside of dungeons instead of making me go back inside to claim them. Still, it’s annoying to be unexpectedly overpowered and then need to dive back into dangerous territory just to avoid another crafting spree. You can turn this annoying convention off in the settings, but playing the game as intended leads to cumbersome situations.

For better or worse, Dragonwilds brings back the “old school” feeling of discovering what to do without overt handholding. An NPC may suggest a general direction, but won’t tell you that you need an Iron Pickaxe to mine a dragon's teeth to complete your quest. Players who have trouble understanding these hints could spend hours trying to decipher exactly how to find the items they need to progress.

Dragonwilds prioritizes the journey of completing quests over how easy they are to finish. After all, that means more time in the crafting system and discovering more about your environment, like rocks you didn’t know you could mine or hidden lore from a side quest you might have missed. Alternatively, it could lead to hours of repetitive grinding for materials, trying to unlock new clues or power-ups without getting any closer to the next objective.

RuneScape: Dragonwilds freshened my perspective on survival-crafting games with base-building and farming under dragon-dominated skies. I floundered for answers on how to progress to the next story quest without clear direction, and while I could explore in the meantime, other games in similar genres can offer the same thing with more entertainment along the way. In a genre full of compelling modern classics like Valheim, RuneScape: Dragonwilds is a difficult game to recommend.
